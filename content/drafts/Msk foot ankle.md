# MSK — Foot & Ankle — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Lisfranc injury — midfoot pain post-trauma, plantar bruising, inability to weight bear — urgent orthopaedic referral
- Acute compartment syndrome of the foot or leg — severe pain, paraesthesia, tense compartment — emergency transfer
- Open fracture or dislocation — call 000
- Achilles tendon rupture with neurovascular compromise — emergency
- Suspected septic joint — hot, swollen ankle/foot joint with fever — emergency
- Calcaneal fracture from fall from height — associated spinal injury must be excluded
- Acute arterial insufficiency — sudden cold pale foot, absent pulses — call 000
- Necrotising fasciitis of the foot — rapidly spreading erythema, crepitus, systemic toxicity — call 000

### Overview
Foot and ankle conditions are extremely common in Australian primary care, emergency, and sports medicine settings. Accurate clinical assessment — particularly appropriate application of the Ottawa Rules — avoids unnecessary imaging while ensuring fractures are not missed. Early, accurate diagnosis and appropriate rehabilitation prevents chronic instability, arthritis, and long-term functional limitation.

### Common Foot and Ankle Conditions

#### Ankle Sprain
Most common musculoskeletal injury presenting to emergency departments. Lateral ankle sprain from an inversion mechanism is most common — the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) is injured first, then the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), then the posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL) in more severe sprains. Medial (deltoid ligament) sprains are less common and more often associated with fracture.

Ottawa Ankle Rules guide X-ray decision — applied in all ankle injuries to avoid unnecessary imaging. Functional rehabilitation with early mobilisation and physiotherapy is superior to prolonged immobilisation. Proprioceptive training is essential to reduce re-injury risk — lateral ankle sprain recurs in up to 70% without rehabilitation.

#### Plantar Fasciitis
Most common cause of heel pain in adults — affects approximately 10% of the population at some point. Degenerative condition (fasciosis) at the calcaneal insertion of the plantar fascia — not primarily inflammatory. Classic presentation: severe first-step pain in the morning that improves after walking (the fascia warms up), returning after prolonged rest. Pain at the medial calcaneal tuberosity (plantar fascia origin). Calcaneal spur on X-ray is common but incidental — present in 50% of asymptomatic adults and does NOT require treatment.

#### Achilles Tendinopathy
Degenerative condition (tendinosis) of the Achilles tendon — not inflammatory. Two forms: mid-portion (2–7cm above insertion) and insertional. Very common in runners and middle-aged adults. Morning stiffness, activity-related pain, tender swelling of the tendon. Eccentric and heavy slow resistance calf exercises are the most evidence-based treatment. Corticosteroid injection is contraindicated — significant tendon rupture risk.

#### Achilles Tendon Rupture
Sudden forceful plantarflexion — typically during recreational sport (push-off in racquet sports, jumping). Felt as a "pop" or a kick to the back of the leg. The patient may be able to walk (plantarflexion preserved through other flexors — do not be falsely reassured). Simmonds-Thompson (calf squeeze) test: patient prone, knees flexed 90°, squeeze the calf — no ankle plantarflexion = rupture. Both operative and non-operative (functional rehabilitation in equinus boot) achieve similar outcomes in most patients. Urgent orthopaedic referral.

#### Lisfranc Injury
Tarsometatarsal joint complex injury — frequently missed and underdiagnosed. Mechanism: axial load on plantarflexed foot (standing on tiptoes and falling), direct crush, twisting. Midfoot pain and swelling, inability to weight bear, bruising on the plantar aspect of the arch (pathognomonic). X-ray weight-bearing views: widening >2mm between base of 1st and 2nd metatarsals. High clinical suspicion with normal X-ray: MRI or CT. Missed Lisfranc injury leads to progressive midfoot arthritis and chronic disability. Urgent orthopaedic referral — surgical fixation required for unstable injuries.

#### Plantar Plate Injury
Plantar plate: fibrocartilaginous structure on the plantar aspect of the lesser MTP joints (2nd most common). Injury from repetitive hyperextension. Presents: plantar forefoot pain at the MTP joint, pain with push-off, positive drawer test (dorsal subluxation of the proximal phalanx). Management: metatarsal dome pad, taping to hold the toe in plantarflexion, rigid soled shoes, corticosteroid injection controversial (weakens already damaged plate), orthopaedic referral for surgical repair if conservative management fails.

#### Morton's Neuroma
Perineural fibrosis of the common digital nerve — most common between 3rd and 4th metatarsal heads. Burning pain and paraesthesia in the forefoot, worse in tight shoes, relieved by removing shoes and massaging. Mulder's click: squeeze the forefoot transversely while applying dorsoplantar pressure — reproducible click with pain. Ultrasound confirms diagnosis. Management: wide-toed footwear, metatarsal pad, corticosteroid injection (50–70% effective), surgical excision for refractory cases.

#### Hallux Valgus
Lateral deviation of the great toe at the MTP joint — bunion. Multifactorial — genetic predisposition, narrow footwear, hypermobility. Pain over the medial eminence (bunion), second toe overriding (hammer toe), shoe fitting difficulty. Management: wide-toed accommodative footwear, orthotics, splinting (does not correct deformity, only comfort). Surgical correction (osteotomy) for significant pain and deformity.

#### Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction
Degenerative condition of the posterior tibial tendon — most common cause of adult acquired flatfoot. Presents insidiously: medial ankle pain, progressive flatfoot deformity, "too many toes sign" (more than 2 toes visible lateral to the heel on posterior view — due to forefoot abduction). Single heel raise test: patient unable to perform single heel raise on affected side. Management: medial arch orthotic, physiotherapy (tibialis posterior strengthening), AFO (ankle-foot orthosis) for moderate dysfunction. Surgical reconstruction for advanced cases.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Foot and Ankle History

#### History
- Mechanism: inversion (lateral ankle sprain), FOOSH onto foot, direct crush, twisting, overuse
- Location: lateral (ATFL sprain), medial (deltoid sprain, post tibial tendon), heel (plantar fasciitis), midfoot (Lisfranc, stress fracture), forefoot (Morton's, MTP pathology), posterior (Achilles)
- Onset: acute trauma vs gradual onset
- Ability to weight bear: immediately after injury and currently
- Swelling: location, onset
- Morning pain/stiffness: plantar fasciitis, Achilles tendinopathy
- Footwear: narrow shoes (Morton's, hallux valgus), unsupportive (plantar fasciitis)
- Activity level: running (stress fractures, Achilles), sport type

#### Ottawa Ankle Rules — Apply to All Ankle Injuries
**X-ray the ANKLE if:**
- Bony tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the lateral malleolus (distal 6cm)
- Bony tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the medial malleolus (distal 6cm)
- Inability to weight bear 4 steps both immediately after injury AND in clinic

**X-ray the FOOT if:**
- Bony tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal
- Bony tenderness at the navicular
- Inability to weight bear 4 steps both immediately after injury AND in clinic

Sensitivity >99% for clinically significant fractures. Do NOT X-ray based on swelling and bruising alone.

### Foot and Ankle Examination

#### Observation
- Weight-bearing alignment: pes planus (flat foot), pes cavus (high arch), hindfoot valgus/varus
- Gait assessment: antalgic, foot drop, Trendelenburg
- Swelling distribution
- Bruising: plantar arch bruising (Lisfranc pathognomonic)
- Skin: calluses, ulceration (diabetic foot), colour

#### Palpation — Systematic
- Malleoli: posterior tips (Ottawa fracture points)
- ATFL: just anterior to lateral malleolus
- CFL: inferior to lateral malleolus
- Medial malleolus and deltoid ligament
- Base 5th metatarsal: avulsion fracture point (Ottawa foot rule)
- Navicular: Ottawa foot rule
- Medial calcaneal tuberosity: plantar fasciitis
- Achilles tendon: mid-portion vs insertional tenderness, swelling, palpable gap (rupture)
- Metatarsal heads: Morton's neuroma pressure point
- Midfoot: Lisfranc tenderness

#### Special Tests
- Simmonds-Thompson (calf squeeze): Achilles rupture
- Anterior drawer test: ATFL integrity (anterior translation of talus)
- Talar tilt test: CFL integrity (inversion stress)
- Windlass test: plantar fasciitis (passive great toe dorsiflexion)
- Mulder's click: Morton's neuroma
- Single heel raise: posterior tibial tendon function
- Squeeze test midfoot: Lisfranc (lateral compression reproduces midfoot pain)

#### Investigations
- X-ray: applying Ottawa Rules; weight-bearing views for Lisfranc
- MRI: ligament tears, tendon pathology, Lisfranc, stress fractures, osteochondral lesions
- CT: complex fractures, tarsal coalition, surgical planning
- Ultrasound: tendon pathology, Morton's neuroma, plantar fascia thickness, guided injection
- Bone scan: stress fracture (when X-ray normal and MRI unavailable)
- ABI: vascular assessment in diabetic foot

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 22-year-old with Lateral Ankle Sprain

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 22-year-old male, right ankle inversion injury playing basketball 2 hours ago.

History: Landed on another player's foot — right ankle inverted. Immediate pain over the lateral ankle. Able to limp off the court with assistance. Has been icing since. Pain currently 6/10 at rest, 8/10 with weight bearing. Mild-to-moderate lateral ankle swelling. No medial ankle pain. No midfoot or base of 5th metatarsal pain. First significant ankle injury.

Past History: No previous ankle injuries. Fit and active. Non-smoker. NKDA.

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: Stable

Right Ankle Examination:
- Inspection: swelling over the lateral ankle predominantly over the ATFL region. Early bruising appearing.
- Ottawa Ankle Rule application: No posterior malleolar tenderness (lateral or medial). Patient walked 4 steps in clinic — possible but painful. Ottawa Ankle Rule: does NOT meet criteria for X-ray.
- Palpation: Maximum tenderness over the ATFL (anterior to lateral malleolus). CFL moderately tender (inferior to lateral malleolus). No posterior malleolus tenderness. No medial tenderness. No base of 5th metatarsal tenderness. No navicular tenderness.
- Anterior drawer test: 2+ laxity compared to left (increased ATFL laxity), soft endpoint.
- Talar tilt: mildly increased, pain with testing.
- ROM: dorsiflexion restricted to 5°, plantarflexion 35°, inversion and eversion painful and guarded.
- Neurovascular: intact bilaterally.

No X-ray required — Ottawa Rules not met.

**A — Assessment**
1. Grade II right lateral ankle sprain — partial ATFL tear ± CFL involvement. Moderate laxity, able to partially weight bear.
2. Ottawa Rules applied — no fracture criteria met. X-ray not indicated.

**P — Plan**
Acute Management — POLICE Protocol:
- Protection: lace-up ankle brace applied — protects against re-inversion while allowing movement
- Optimal Loading: weight bear as tolerated — avoid prolonged non-weight bearing
- Ice: 20 minutes every 2–3 hours for first 48 hours
- Compression: tubigrip or compression bandage
- Elevation: rest with foot elevated above heart level

Analgesia:
- Ibuprofen 400mg TDS with food for 5 days (anti-inflammatory, analgesic)
- Paracetamol 1g QID — staggered with ibuprofen

Physiotherapy:
- Urgent referral — within 3–5 days
- Program: early ROM exercises, progressive weight bearing, peroneal strengthening, proprioception training (balance board), return-to-sport program
- Evidence: physiotherapy and supervised rehabilitation reduces re-injury rate from 70% to 30%

Bracing:
- Lace-up ankle brace for all sport activities for 6 months after returning to basketball
- Evidence: external support significantly reduces recurrence risk

Safety Netting:
- Return if: no improvement in weight bearing by 72 hours, increasing swelling, new midfoot pain developing (Lisfranc concern), neurovascular compromise

Review: Physiotherapy review within 1 week. Return to basketball when: pain-free walking, able to hop on affected leg without pain, proprioception equal bilaterally.

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: According to the Ottawa Ankle Rules, which finding indicates an ankle X-ray is required?
   A: Swelling and bruising over the lateral ankle after an inversion injury
   B: Bony tenderness at the posterior tip of the lateral or medial malleolus, OR inability to weight bear 4 steps both immediately after injury and in clinic
   C: ATFL tenderness on palpation
   D: Pain with active ankle inversion
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Ottawa Ankle Rules: X-ray the ankle if: bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the LATERAL malleolus (distal 6cm), OR bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the MEDIAL malleolus (distal 6cm), OR inability to weight bear 4 steps both immediately after the injury AND at the time of assessment. Swelling, bruising, and ATFL tenderness alone do NOT indicate fracture — these are soft tissue injury findings. The Ottawa Rules have sensitivity >99% for clinically significant ankle fractures and reduce unnecessary X-rays by 30–40%. Apply at every ankle injury — document your Ottawa assessment clearly.

2. Q: A 40-year-old male runner develops gradual onset right heel pain, worst in the first few steps in the morning and after sitting for long periods. Tenderness is maximal at the medial calcaneal tuberosity. His X-ray shows a calcaneal spur. What is the diagnosis and management?
   A: Calcaneal stress fracture — non-weight bearing and MRI
   B: Plantar fasciitis — calf and plantar fascia stretching, supportive footwear, load modification; the calcaneal spur is incidental and does not require treatment
   C: Fat pad atrophy — heel cup
   D: Retrocalcaneal bursitis — corticosteroid injection into the bursa
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Plantar fasciitis: classic first-step morning pain, medial calcaneal tuberosity tenderness, activity-related pain. Calcaneal spur is present in 50% of asymptomatic adults — it is an incidental finding and is NOT the cause of pain (the fascia insertion is the pain source). Evidence-based management: 1) Stretching — plantar fascia specific stretch (dorsiflex toes to stretch fascia before first step) and calf stretching — best evidence; 2) Supportive footwear with cushioned heel and arch support; 3) Load modification — reduce running volume temporarily; 4) NSAIDs — short-term symptom relief; 5) Taping (low-dye) — reduces fascia load acutely; 6) Intrinsic foot strengthening. Most resolve within 12 months with appropriate conservative management.

3. Q: A 35-year-old recreational tennis player hears a "pop" in the back of his left leg and falls to the ground. He cannot stand on his tiptoes. Simmonds-Thompson test is positive on the left. What is the diagnosis and management?
   A: Calf muscle (gastrocnemius) tear — RICE and physiotherapy
   B: Achilles tendon rupture — urgent orthopaedic referral; non-operative functional rehabilitation or surgical repair
   C: Ankle sprain — Ottawa Rules and X-ray
   D: Posterior tibial tendon rupture — orthotics
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Achilles tendon rupture: sudden "pop" or kick sensation during push-off activity. Simmonds-Thompson (calf squeeze) test: patient prone, knees flexed — squeeze calf — no ankle plantarflexion = complete rupture. Note: the patient may still be able to plantarflex weakly using other flexors (tibialis posterior, FHL) — this does not exclude rupture. Do not be reassured by preserved plantarflexion. Urgent orthopaedic referral. Both operative (surgical repair) and non-operative (functional rehabilitation in equinus boot with progressive weight bearing) have similar overall re-rupture rates and functional outcomes in most patients. The decision depends on patient factors, age, activity level, and surgeon expertise. Ultrasound or MRI confirms complete vs partial rupture.

4. Q: A 28-year-old falls from a ladder and lands on his right foot. He has severe midfoot pain and swelling, and cannot weight bear. Bruising is visible on the plantar surface of the arch. X-ray shows widening of 3mm between the bases of the 1st and 2nd metatarsals. What is the diagnosis and immediate management?
   A: Plantar fasciitis — supportive footwear and stretching
   B: Lisfranc injury — non-weight bearing immediately, urgent orthopaedic referral for surgical assessment
   C: 5th metatarsal avulsion fracture — buddy tape and weight bear as tolerated
   D: Ankle sprain — Ottawa Rules and RICE
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Lisfranc injury: tarsometatarsal joint complex disruption. Key features: midfoot pain post-trauma, plantar arch bruising (pathognomonic — bruising in the arch = Lisfranc until proven otherwise), inability to weight bear, X-ray widening >2mm between base of 1st and 2nd metatarsals. Management: non-weight bearing immediately — any weight bearing worsens displacement. Urgent orthopaedic referral. Significant instability requires surgical fixation (ORIF or primary arthrodesis). Missed or inadequately treated Lisfranc leads to progressive midfoot arthritis, chronic pain, and significant functional limitation. If X-ray appears normal but clinical suspicion is high — MRI or CT for definitive assessment. Do not allow weight bearing on a suspected Lisfranc injury.

5. Q: What does the Windlass test assess and how is it performed?
   A: Morton's neuroma — squeezing the forefoot reproduces a click
   B: Plantar fasciitis — passive dorsiflexion of the great toe tightens the plantar fascia via the windlass mechanism, reproducing medial heel pain
   C: Achilles tendon integrity — calf squeeze with patient prone
   D: Anterior talofibular ligament — anterior drawer of the talus
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Windlass test for plantar fasciitis: patient standing or supine, examiner passively dorsiflexes the great toe. This tightens the plantar fascia through the windlass mechanism (the plantar fascia wraps around the metatarsal heads and tightens as the toe is dorsiflexed — like a windlass tightening a rope). A positive test reproduces the patient's heel pain at the medial calcaneal tuberosity. High sensitivity and specificity for plantar fasciitis. The Windlass mechanism is also why first-step pain is worst — the fascia is cold and tight after a period of rest, and the first step loads the windlass mechanism maximally. Supportive shoes with a slight heel lift reduce the windlass load and improve symptoms.

6. Q: A 55-year-old male presents with burning pain and tingling between his 3rd and 4th toes of the right foot, worse in tight shoes and relieved by removing his shoes and massaging his foot. Squeezing the forefoot transversely while applying dorsoplantar pressure to the 3rd intermetatarsal space reproduces a click with pain. What is the diagnosis?
   A: Plantar fasciitis — Windlass test positive
   B: Morton's neuroma — Mulder's click positive; wide-toed shoes, metatarsal pad, corticosteroid injection, surgical excision
   C: Stress fracture of the 3rd metatarsal — X-ray and MRI
   D: Gout — elevated uric acid and first MTP joint involvement
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Morton's neuroma: perineural fibrosis of the interdigital nerve — most common between 3rd and 4th metatarsal heads. Burning pain and tingling in the affected webspace, worse in tight shoes (compression narrows the intermetatarsal space), relieved by removing shoes and massaging. Mulder's click: transverse forefoot compression while applying dorsoplantar pressure to the intermetatarsal space — reproducible click with pain reproduction — highly specific. Ultrasound confirms the diagnosis (hypoechoic mass at the intermetatarsal space). Management: wide-toed footwear, metatarsal dome pad (reduces pressure on the neuroma), corticosteroid injection (50–70% effective short-term), sclerosing alcohol injection, surgical excision for refractory cases.

7. Q: Which statement correctly describes the management of Achilles tendinopathy?
   A: Corticosteroid injection is first-line — reduces tendon inflammation effectively
   B: Eccentric calf loading (Alfredsson protocol) and heavy slow resistance training are the most evidence-based treatments; corticosteroid injection is contraindicated due to risk of tendon rupture
   C: Complete rest for 8 weeks — the tendon needs to heal without loading
   D: Surgery is required for all mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Achilles tendinopathy is a degenerative condition (tendinosis) not primarily inflammatory — corticosteroids do not address the pathology and significantly increase Achilles tendon rupture risk (avoid). Evidence-based management: Eccentric calf loading (Alfredsson protocol): single-leg heel drops, both straight and bent knee, 3 sets of 15 reps twice daily for 12 weeks — promotes tendon remodelling. Heavy slow resistance (HSR) training is an effective alternative with better compliance. NSAIDs for short-term pain relief. Heel raise to reduce Achilles load. Load management — reduce running volume temporarily. Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) for refractory mid-portion tendinopathy. PRP injections — emerging evidence. Surgery for truly refractory cases (>6 months failed conservative management).

8. Q: A 65-year-old diabetic female presents with a non-healing ulcer on the plantar surface of her right 1st MTP joint. There is surrounding callus, no erythema, and ankle pulses are palpable. She has reduced sensation to monofilament testing. What is the classification of this ulcer?
   A: Venous ulcer — compression bandaging
   B: Neuropathic diabetic foot ulcer — debridement of callus, pressure offloading (total contact cast or removable cast walker), podiatry referral, glycaemic optimisation, foot care education
   C: Arterial ulcer — urgent vascular surgery
   D: Pressure ulcer — foam dressing and mattress
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Neuropathic diabetic foot ulcer: located under areas of high pressure (MTP joint, heel, callus), surrounded by callus (protective sensation lost — patient unaware of pressure), adequate vascular supply (palpable pulses — distinguishes from ischaemic ulcer), reduced monofilament sensation. Management: 1) Offloading — total contact cast (gold standard) or removable cast walker; without offloading, the ulcer cannot heal; 2) Debridement of callus and necrotic tissue by podiatry; 3) Wound dressing appropriate to wound characteristics; 4) Glycaemic optimisation; 5) Regular podiatry follow-up (4–6 weekly minimum); 6) Screen for osteomyelitis if probe-to-bone positive or deep ulcer (MRI is most sensitive). MDT approach: podiatry, endocrinology, vascular surgery, wound nurse.

9. Q: A 30-year-old long-distance runner develops insidious onset 2nd metatarsal pain that has been worsening for 3 weeks. There is point tenderness over the 2nd metatarsal shaft. X-ray is reported as normal. What is the most likely diagnosis and next investigation?
   A: Morton's neuroma — Mulder's click
   B: Metatarsal stress fracture — MRI or bone scan; X-ray misses most stress fractures in the first 2–3 weeks
   C: Plantar fasciitis — Windlass test
   D: Lisfranc injury — weight-bearing X-ray with stress views
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Metatarsal stress fracture: insidious onset midfoot/forefoot pain in a runner, point tenderness over the metatarsal shaft, worsening with continued running, normal initial X-ray (X-ray detects only 50–70% of stress fractures initially — periosteal reaction appears at 2–3 weeks). 2nd and 3rd metatarsals most common. MRI is the most sensitive investigation for stress fracture (and identifies bone marrow oedema indicating stress reaction before cortical fracture develops). Bone scan is an alternative if MRI unavailable. Management: rest from impact activity for 4–6 weeks, stiff-soled shoe or CAM boot, gradual return to running. High-risk stress fractures (navicular, 5th metatarsal proximal diaphysis — Jones fracture) require orthopaedic referral and may need surgical fixation.

10. Q: The "too many toes" sign on posterior observation of a patient is associated with which condition?
    A: Hallux valgus — lateral deviation of the great toe
    B: Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction with acquired adult flatfoot — excessive forefoot abduction causes more than 2 toes to be visible lateral to the heel on posterior view
    C: Morton's neuroma — splaying of the forefoot
    D: Plantar fasciitis — altered gait pattern
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD): the posterior tibial tendon is the primary dynamic stabiliser of the medial arch. Degeneration causes progressive flatfoot deformity. "Too many toes" sign: from behind the patient, normally ≤2 toes are visible lateral to the heel; in PTTD, the forefoot abducts and more than 2 toes are visible (due to hindfoot valgus and forefoot abduction). Single heel raise test: patient cannot perform a single heel raise on the affected side (normally raises heel and inverts — requires functional posterior tibial tendon). Management by stage: Stage I (pain, no deformity) — physiotherapy, orthotics; Stage II (flexible flatfoot deformity) — custom orthotics/AFO, physiotherapy; Stage III–IV (rigid deformity) — surgical reconstruction or arthrodesis.

11. Q: A footballer sustains an ankle inversion injury. The Ottawa Rules are not met. On examination, he has significant ATFL tenderness and anterior drawer testing shows 3+ laxity with a soft endpoint compared to the other side. He cannot walk without pain. What grade of ankle sprain does this represent?
    A: Grade I — microscopic ligament tears, minimal laxity, normal function
    B: Grade III — complete ligament rupture, significant laxity, soft endpoint, functional impairment
    C: Grade II — partial tear, moderate laxity, moderate functional loss
    D: This presentation requires X-ray before grading
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Ankle sprain grading: Grade I — microscopic ATFL tears, minimal swelling, full weight bearing, normal anterior drawer. Grade II — partial ATFL tear, moderate swelling, antalgic gait, mild-moderate anterior drawer laxity. Grade III — complete ATFL rupture, significant swelling, unable to weight bear fully, marked anterior drawer laxity with soft endpoint (no firm stopping point — complete ligament discontinuity). This patient has Grade III features: 3+ laxity, soft endpoint, significant functional impairment. Management: Grade III may benefit from prolonged bracing (6 weeks) before active rehabilitation, physiotherapy is essential, consider MRI to exclude associated injuries (osteochondral lesion, peroneal tendon tear). Ottawa Rules assess fracture risk — not sprain grade.

12. Q: A 50-year-old female develops gradual onset medial ankle and arch pain over 6 months. She notes her right foot is becoming flatter. On examination, she cannot perform a single heel raise on the right. Palpation reveals tenderness along the posterior tibial tendon. What is the most appropriate initial management?
    A: Complete rest and non-weight bearing for 8 weeks
    B: Custom medial arch orthotic or ankle-foot orthosis (AFO), posterior tibial tendon strengthening physiotherapy, and podiatry referral
    C: Surgical reconstruction — first-line for all PTTD
    D: Corticosteroid injection into the posterior tibial tendon sheath — reduces inflammation
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD) Stage I–II management: medial arch support is the most important intervention — custom orthotics or prefabricated orthotic with medial heel wedge and arch support reduces the load on the posterior tibial tendon. AFO (ankle-foot orthosis) for more advanced dysfunction. Physiotherapy: tibialis posterior eccentric strengthening, calf stretching. Podiatry: gait assessment, orthotic fabrication. Corticosteroid injection around the posterior tibial tendon is controversial — may weaken the already-compromised tendon and risk rupture. Weight reduction if overweight. Surgery (tendon transfer, calcaneal osteotomy, or arthrodesis) for Stage III–IV or failed conservative management. Early management at Stage I–II can halt progression.

13. Q: A patient presents with acute foot pain and swelling 5 days after starting a new running program. Point tenderness is maximal at the navicular. Ottawa Foot Rule: the navicular is tender. What is the management?
    A: Weight bear as tolerated and review in 2 weeks
    B: X-ray (Ottawa Rule met) — if X-ray normal and high clinical suspicion, MRI is required; navicular stress fracture requires non-weight bearing and orthopaedic review — it is a high-risk stress fracture
    C: Morton's neuroma treatment — wide shoes and corticosteroid injection
    D: Plantar fasciitis management — stretching and supportive footwear
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Navicular stress fracture is a HIGH-RISK stress fracture — the dorsal third of the navicular has poor blood supply and is susceptible to delayed union and non-union. Ottawa Rule met (navicular tenderness) — X-ray required. However, X-ray misses most navicular stress fractures — MRI or CT is required if X-ray is normal and clinical suspicion remains. Management: strict non-weight bearing in a cast for 6–8 weeks (NOT a boot — must be non-weight bearing). Orthopaedic referral — surgical fixation considered for complete fractures or elite athletes requiring rapid return to sport. Do NOT allow continued weight bearing on a suspected navicular stress fracture — this leads to non-union and permanent disability. Compare to lower-risk 2nd/3rd metatarsal stress fractures which can be managed with activity modification.

14. Q: What is the evidence-based treatment for lateral ankle sprain rehabilitation?
    A: Complete immobilisation in plaster cast for 6 weeks — allows ligament healing
    B: Early functional rehabilitation — early weight bearing as tolerated, ankle brace, progressive proprioceptive and strengthening exercises, return to sport program
    C: Surgery — ligament reconstruction for all Grade II–III sprains
    D: Rest only — exercise risks re-injury during the healing phase
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Functional rehabilitation is superior to immobilisation for all grades of ankle sprain: Early weight bearing (with brace/support) is encouraged — offloading delays recovery. POLICE protocol: Protection (brace), Optimal Loading (weight bear as tolerated), Ice, Compression, Elevation. Physiotherapy program: ROM exercises, peroneal strengthening (resisted eversion), proprioception training (balance board — most important for preventing recurrence), sport-specific rehabilitation. Proprioceptive training is essential — lateral ankle sprain recurs in up to 70% without rehabilitation and in approximately 30% with appropriate rehabilitation. Ankle brace for return to sport for at least 6 months after Grade II–III sprain. Surgery (Brostrom repair) reserved for chronic ankle instability after failed rehabilitation.

15. Q: Which statement correctly describes hallux valgus management?
    A: Surgery is the first-line treatment for all hallux valgus
    B: Wide-toed accommodative footwear and orthotics for pain management; surgery (osteotomy) for significant pain and deformity not responding to conservative measures — surgery corrects the deformity but results are not always predictable
    C: Splints and toe spacers correct the deformity long-term
    D: NSAIDs alone are the most evidence-based treatment
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Hallux valgus (bunion) management: Conservative: wide-toed shoes (most important — narrow shoes worsen deformity and pain), orthotics (reduce overpronation — a contributing factor), bunion pads for pain relief, night splints (do not correct deformity but may slow progression). Avoid high heels. Surgical indications: significant pain affecting daily activities not responding to conservative measures, progressive deformity causing shoe-fitting difficulty, associated secondary deformities (hammer toes). Surgery types: distal osteotomy (mild-moderate), scarf/chevron osteotomy (moderate), proximal osteotomy (severe). Surgical outcomes are generally good but not universally excellent — patients should have realistic expectations. Recurrence can occur. Cosmetic correction alone is not an indication for surgery.

16. Q: A 45-year-old man presents with acute severe pain, swelling, and erythema of the right first MTP joint. The joint is exquisitely tender — even the weight of a sheet is intolerable. Serum uric acid is 0.62 mmol/L. What is the diagnosis and management?
    A: Septic arthritis — emergency joint aspiration and IV antibiotics
    B: Acute gout (podagra) — NSAIDs or colchicine for acute attack; urate-lowering therapy after the acute attack resolves
    C: Pseudogout — calcium pyrophosphate crystals — joint aspiration confirms
    D: Hallux valgus with bursitis — corticosteroid injection into the bursa
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Acute gout at the first MTP joint (podagra): classic presentation — severe acute pain, erythema, warmth, and swelling of the first MTP joint. Often wakes the patient at night. Exquisite tenderness (even sheet weight is intolerable) is characteristic. Note: serum uric acid is frequently NORMAL during an acute gout attack — do not use it to rule out gout. Acute treatment: NSAIDs (naproxen 500mg BD or indomethacin 50mg TDS) for 5–7 days, or colchicine 1mg then 0.5mg 1 hour later. Corticosteroid injection or oral prednisolone if NSAIDs contraindicated. Do NOT start allopurinol during an acute attack. Urate-lowering therapy: start 4–6 weeks after the acute attack resolves — target uric acid <0.36 mmol/L. Colchicine prophylaxis for 3–6 months when starting allopurinol.

17. Q: A patient is referred for a diabetic foot assessment. What is the correct frequency of foot review for a patient with peripheral neuropathy and a previous foot ulcer?
    A: Annual review only — if no current ulcer
    B: Every 1–3 months — high-risk feet require frequent review; includes monofilament testing, foot inspection, footwear review, and podiatry
    C: Only when symptomatic — patients can self-manage between appointments
    D: Six-monthly — standard for all diabetic patients
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Diabetic foot risk stratification guides review frequency: Low risk (no neuropathy, no vascular disease, no deformity) — annual review. Moderate risk (one risk factor present — neuropathy, PAD, or deformity) — every 3–6 months. High risk (multiple risk factors, previous ulcer, previous amputation, renal replacement therapy) — every 1–3 months with multidisciplinary podiatry involvement. This patient (neuropathy + previous ulcer) is HIGH risk — review every 1–3 months. Review components: monofilament testing (10g monofilament), skin inspection, callus management, footwear adequacy, ABI if vascular concerns, education. Diabetic foot disease is the leading cause of non-traumatic lower limb amputation in Australia — intensive preventive care dramatically reduces amputation rates.

18. Q: A 33-year-old soccer player develops outer ankle pain after a tackle. The Ottawa Rules indicate an X-ray is required. X-ray shows a small avulsion fracture at the base of the 5th metatarsal. What is the management?
    A: Surgical fixation — all 5th metatarsal fractures require surgery
    B: Avulsion fracture of the base of the 5th metatarsal (from peroneus brevis pull) — symptomatic treatment, weight bear as tolerated with supportive footwear or CAM boot; heals well conservatively in most cases
    C: Non-weight bearing cast for 8 weeks — treat as a Jones fracture
    D: This finding is incidental — treat as an ankle sprain
    Correct: B
    Explanation: 5th metatarsal base avulsion fracture: the peroneus brevis tendon inserts on the styloid process at the base of the 5th metatarsal — a forceful inversion avulses this fragment. These fractures heal well conservatively in most cases — weight bearing as tolerated with a firm-soled shoe or CAM boot for 4–6 weeks. Distinguish from a Jones fracture (transverse fracture through the proximal diaphysis — at the junction of the metaphysis and diaphysis, approximately 1.5cm from the tip of the styloid) — Jones fractures have poor blood supply and high non-union rate, requiring non-weight bearing and often surgical fixation. This distinction is clinically important — examine the fracture line location on X-ray carefully.

19. Q: A 60-year-old woman has had bilateral forefoot pain for 2 years. Examination shows significant hallux valgus bilaterally with lateral deviation and overriding of the 2nd toes. There are calluses under both 2nd metatarsal heads. She has been unable to find comfortable shoes. Conservative management has failed to adequately control symptoms. What is the most appropriate next step?
    A: Continue conservative management indefinitely — surgery is too risky in a 60-year-old
    B: Referral to orthopaedic surgery or podiatric surgery for surgical correction — bilateral significant hallux valgus with failed conservative management and functional limitation is an indication for surgery
    C: Custom orthotics only — never refer for surgery
    D: Corticosteroid injection into the MTP joints bilaterally
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Indications for hallux valgus surgery: significant pain affecting daily activities and footwear, failed conservative management (minimum 3–6 months), progressive deformity causing secondary problems (hammer toes, metatarsalgia from lateral transfer), or significant difficulty with shoe fitting. Age alone is not a contraindication — surgical outcomes are good in appropriately selected patients. Bilateral surgery can be performed staged or simultaneously (discuss with surgeon). Surgical options depend on deformity severity: mild (chevron/Austin osteotomy), moderate (scarf osteotomy), severe (Lapidus procedure — 1st TMT joint fusion). Pre-operative assessment includes weight-bearing X-rays and assessment of associated deformities. Realistic patient expectations are important — recovery takes 3–6 months.

20. Q: A 25-year-old triathlete develops gradual onset medial shin pain that has been progressively worsening over 3 weeks of increased training. Pain is reproduced by hop testing and palpation along the posteromedial tibial border. X-ray is normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    A: Deep vein thrombosis — doppler ultrasound
    B: Medial tibial stress syndrome (shin splints) — activity modification, progressive load management, biomechanical assessment; if pain is point tender and severe, MRI to exclude tibial stress fracture
    C: Compartment syndrome — urgent surgical fasciotomy
    D: Peripheral arterial disease — ABI measurement
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Medial tibial stress syndrome (MTSS/shin splints): diffuse medial tibial border pain in runners — caused by traction periostitis along the tibial periosteum from soleus and deep flexor muscle pull. Distinguished from tibial stress fracture: MTSS has diffuse pain over 5+cm of the posteromedial tibia, whereas stress fracture has point tenderness at one spot (often with positive hop test). MRI if: severe point tenderness, hop test positive, or not resolving — tibial stress fractures appear as cortical breaks or periosteal reaction. MTSS management: reduce training load, correct contributing factors (overpronation — orthotics, training errors — gradual progression, footwear), calf strengthening, progressive return to running. Most resolve with 4–8 weeks of load modification.
