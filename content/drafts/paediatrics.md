# Paediatrics — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Refer Urgently or Call 000
- Stridor at rest — upper airway obstruction
- Respiratory distress with SpO2 <92%
- Altered consciousness or seizure not resolving
- Purpuric or petechial rash with fever — meningococcal until proven otherwise
- Severe dehydration — sunken fontanelle, no tears, no urine output >8 hours
- Bilious vomiting in a neonate — bowel obstruction until excluded
- Bulging fontanelle with fever — meningitis
- Limp with fever — septic arthritis
- Burns >10% BSA or involving face, hands, genitals, or circumferential
- Any suspicion of non-accidental injury

### Common Presentations by Age Group

#### Neonates (0–28 days)
- Neonatal jaundice — physiological vs pathological
- Feeding difficulties — latching, supply, tongue tie
- Neonatal sepsis — temperature instability, poor feeding, lethargy
- Umbilical granuloma and omphalitis
- Congenital hip dysplasia

#### Infants (1–12 months)
- Bronchiolitis — RSV, seasonal peaks winter
- Gastroenteritis — dehydration assessment critical
- Otitis media
- Eczema and nappy rash
- Developmental concerns — milestones
- Febrile illness — source identification

#### Toddlers and Preschool (1–5 years)
- Croup — barking cough, stridor, seal-like
- Febrile convulsions
- Hand foot and mouth disease
- Asthma — first presentation
- Foreign body ingestion or aspiration
- Behaviour and sleep concerns

#### School Age (5–12 years)
- Asthma management
- ADHD assessment and management
- Recurrent tonsillitis
- Enuresis
- Headache — tension vs migraine
- Anxiety and school refusal

#### Adolescents (12–18 years)
- Mental health — depression, anxiety, self-harm
- Eating disorders
- Acne management
- Contraception and sexual health
- Substance use
- Sports injuries

### Key Paediatric Conditions

#### Bronchiolitis
Most common lower respiratory tract infection in infants under 12 months. RSV is the predominant cause. Management is supportive — there is no role for bronchodilators, corticosteroids, or antibiotics in typical viral bronchiolitis. Admit if: SpO2 <92% on air, significant respiratory distress, poor feeding (<50% normal intake), age <6 weeks, or social concerns.

#### Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis)
Caused by parainfluenza virus. Characterised by barking cough, hoarse voice, inspiratory stridor. Severity graded mild to severe. Treatment: dexamethasone 0.15mg/kg oral (single dose) for moderate-severe. Nebulised adrenaline for severe croup in emergency setting. Most cases managed in primary care with observation and parental education.

#### Febrile Convulsions
Occur in 2–5% of children aged 6 months to 5 years. Simple febrile convulsion: generalised, <15 minutes, single episode in 24 hours, full recovery. Complex: focal, >15 minutes, multiple in 24 hours. Management: ensure airway, time seizure, manage fever. Investigate for source of fever. Lumbar puncture if meningism or complex features. Reassure parents — excellent prognosis for simple febrile convulsions.

#### Gastroenteritis and Dehydration
Assess dehydration using WHO or NICE criteria. Mild (<5%): slightly dry mucous membranes, normal skin turgor. Moderate (5–10%): sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor, decreased urine output. Severe (>10%): shock signs. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) is first-line for mild-moderate dehydration. Ondansetron 0.15mg/kg oral for persistent vomiting preventing ORS.

#### Immunisation Schedule
Follow the Australian National Immunisation Program (NIP) schedule. NPs can administer and prescribe vaccines on the NIP. Key milestones: 2, 4, 6, 12, 18 months; 4 years; 12–13 years (school program). Check AIR (Australian Immunisation Register) before every vaccination encounter.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Paediatric History

#### Always Ask
- Age and weight — critical for dosing
- Presenting complaint — from parent AND child (if age appropriate)
- Onset, duration, and progression
- Fever — temperature, duration, pattern, response to antipyretics
- Feeding — breast or formula, solids, recent changes, intake compared to normal
- Urine output — wet nappies or toilet visits
- Bowel habit — last stool, consistency
- Vomiting — frequency, volume, bilious?
- Rash — distribution, progression, blanching?
- Exposures — sick contacts, daycare, travel
- Birth history — gestation, birth weight, NICU stay
- Developmental history — milestones met?
- Immunisation status — check AIR
- Medications — including over the counter and complementary
- Allergies
- Family history — relevant conditions
- Social history — who is at home, any concerns

#### Growth and Development
- Plot weight, height, and head circumference on age-sex appropriate growth chart
- Track percentile trends — single measurements less meaningful than trends
- Developmental milestones — gross motor, fine motor, language, social
- Use standardised tools where indicated — ASQ-3, M-CHAT for autism screening

### Paediatric Examination

#### General Observations
- Does the child look well or unwell? — the "sick or not sick" gestalt
- Activity level — alert and interactive, or quiet and lethargic?
- Colour — pink, pale, mottled, cyanosed?
- Work of breathing — at rest, with feeding, with activity
- Hydration — mucous membranes, skin turgor, fontanelle (infants), eyes

#### Vital Signs — Age-Specific Normal Ranges
| Age | RR (breaths/min) | HR (bpm) | SBP (mmHg) |
|-----|-----------------|----------|------------|
| Neonate | 30–60 | 100–160 | 60–90 |
| Infant 1–12m | 25–50 | 100–160 | 70–100 |
| Toddler 1–3y | 20–40 | 90–150 | 80–110 |
| Preschool 3–5y | 20–30 | 80–140 | 80–110 |
| School 5–12y | 15–25 | 70–120 | 90–120 |
| Adolescent | 12–20 | 60–100 | 100–130 |

#### System Examination
- ENT — tympanic membranes, throat, cervical nodes
- Respiratory — RR, work of breathing, auscultation, SpO2
- Cardiovascular — HR, perfusion, murmur
- Abdomen — tenderness, organomegaly, hernias
- Skin — rash characterisation, eczema severity, bruising pattern
- Neurological — tone, reflexes, fontanelle, behaviour

#### Investigations to Consider
- FBE and CRP — fever without source, suspected infection
- Blood cultures — febrile neonate, toxic-looking child
- Urine MCS — UTI (clean catch or catheter specimen in young children)
- Throat swab — suspected strep pharyngitis
- Respiratory viral panel — bronchiolitis, croup
- Blood glucose — unwell infant or child
- Lumbar puncture — suspected meningitis, complex febrile convulsion
- CXR — respiratory distress, suspected pneumonia
- Developmental screening tools — ASQ-3, M-CHAT

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 18-month-old with Croup

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 18-month-old male brought in by mother with 1-day history of barking cough and noisy breathing, worse overnight.

History of Presenting Complaint: Onset yesterday afternoon with mild coryzal symptoms. Developed barking, seal-like cough overnight. Mother noticed noisy breathing (inspiratory) when upset or crying. No stridor at rest currently. Low-grade fever 37.8°C at home managed with paracetamol. Feeding slightly reduced but still taking fluids. No drooling. No difficulty swallowing. No rash. One sibling had a cold last week. Up to date with immunisations. No previous croup episodes.

Past History: Born term, no NICU. No significant past medical history. No medications. NKDA.

Social History: Lives with parents and one sibling. Non-smoker household. Attends family daycare 3 days per week.

Parent Concerns: "He sounds terrible and we didn't sleep at all — is it serious?"

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: T 37.6°C | HR 118 bpm | RR 28 breaths/min | SpO2 97% on air | Weight 11.2kg

General: Alert, active, interactive with toys in room. Mildly distressed when examined. No stridor at rest. Barking cough audible during examination.

Respiratory: Mild subcostal recession during crying. No stridor at rest. Transmitted upper airway sounds bilaterally. Air entry equal bilaterally. No wheeze. No crackles.

ENT: Mild rhinorrhoea, clear. Oropharynx mildly injected. No exudate. TMs normal bilaterally.

Cardiovascular: Regular rate and rhythm. No murmur. Good perfusion. CRT <2 seconds.

Westley Croup Score: Stridor 0 (none at rest) + Retractions 1 (mild) + Air entry 0 (normal) + Cyanosis 0 + Consciousness 0 = Score 1 — Mild croup

**A — Assessment**
Primary Diagnosis: Mild viral croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) — J05.0

Differential Diagnoses Considered:
- Bacterial tracheitis — excluded: not toxic, no high fever, no progression to severe distress
- Epiglottitis — excluded: no drooling, no tripod position, immunised, able to drink
- Foreign body aspiration — less likely: gradual onset, coryzal prodrome, bilateral air entry
- Asthma — less likely: barking cough quality, upper airway stridor, age

**P — Plan**
Management:
- Dexamethasone 0.15mg/kg oral = 1.7mg — rounded to 2mg oral (single dose). Given in clinic.
- Paracetamol 15mg/kg = 168mg 4-6 hourly PRN for fever and comfort
- Cool humidified air at home — open freezer briefly, cool night air
- Encourage oral fluids — small frequent amounts
- Written croup action plan provided

Safety Netting — Return immediately if:
- Stridor at rest that does not settle
- Increased work of breathing or retractions at rest
- Unable to swallow fluids or drooling
- Cyanosis or SpO2 drops
- Becomes increasingly distressed or lethargic
- Parents unable to manage at home

Education: Discussed viral aetiology, typical 3–5 day course, role of dexamethasone (reduces severity and duration), safety netting criteria. Parents verbalised understanding. Written information provided. Review in 24 hours or earlier if concerned.

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 3-month-old infant presents with 2 days of increased work of breathing, poor feeding, and wheeze. Temperature 37.4°C. SpO2 90% on air. Widespread fine crackles and expiratory wheeze bilaterally. What is the most appropriate management?
   A: Salbutamol nebuliser and discharge home with bronchodilator
   B: Supportive care only — bronchodilators and steroids are not indicated; admit given SpO2 90%
   C: Oral prednisolone and review in 24 hours
   D: Antibiotics for secondary bacterial pneumonia
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Bronchiolitis management is supportive. Bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and antibiotics have no proven benefit in viral bronchiolitis. SpO2 <92% on air is an indication for admission. Additional admission criteria include age <6 weeks, poor feeding (<50% normal intake), significant respiratory distress, or social concerns.

2. Q: A 2-year-old presents with barking cough and inspiratory stridor at rest. RR 36, HR 130, SpO2 95%, moderate subcostal recession. Westley score 5. What is the correct treatment?
   A: Observation only — mild croup does not require treatment
   B: Nebulised salbutamol and oral prednisolone
   C: Dexamethasone 0.15mg/kg oral and nebulised adrenaline — call 000
   D: Antibiotics for bacterial tracheitis
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Westley score 5 indicates moderate-severe croup. Dexamethasone 0.15mg/kg oral is first-line. Nebulised adrenaline (1:1000, 0.5mL/kg to max 5mL) is used for severe croup and provides temporary relief. This child needs emergency transfer — stridor at rest with significant recession requires hospital assessment.

3. Q: According to the Australian National Immunisation Program, at what age does a child receive their first MMR (measles-mumps-rubella) vaccine?
   A: 6 months
   B: 12 months
   C: 18 months
   D: 4 years
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The first MMR (combined measles-mumps-rubella-varicella, MMRV) is given at 12 months on the Australian NIP schedule. A second dose is given at 18 months. Checking the Australian Immunisation Register (AIR) before vaccination encounters is essential to identify catch-up needs.

4. Q: A 6-week-old febrile infant (T 38.2°C) presents looking unwell with poor feeding and lethargy. What is the most appropriate management?
   A: Paracetamol and review in 24 hours
   B: Urine MCS only — most febrile neonates have UTI
   C: Emergency transfer — febrile neonates require full septic workup and IV antibiotics
   D: Viral swab and reassurance
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Febrile neonates (<3 months, especially <28 days) require emergency assessment. They are unable to localise infection and sepsis can progress rapidly. Full septic workup including blood cultures, FBE, CRP, urine MCS, and lumbar puncture is required. IV antibiotics should not be delayed. Call 000 or transfer urgently.

5. Q: A 4-year-old presents with sudden onset high fever, toxic appearance, drooling, and sitting in a tripod position. No barking cough. What is the most likely diagnosis and immediate action?
   A: Croup — give dexamethasone and observe
   B: Tonsillitis — examine throat and swab
   C: Epiglottitis — call 000 immediately, do not attempt to examine the throat
   D: Foreign body aspiration — arrange urgent CXR
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Drooling, tripod positioning, toxic appearance, and high fever without barking cough is epiglottitis until proven otherwise. Do NOT examine the throat — this can precipitate complete airway obstruction. Call 000 immediately. Keep the child calm and in the position of comfort. Epiglottitis is a life-threatening emergency requiring urgent airway management in hospital.

6. Q: Using the NICE dehydration assessment, which finding indicates SEVERE dehydration in a child with gastroenteritis?
   A: Slightly dry mucous membranes and normal skin turgor
   B: Sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor, decreased urine output
   C: Shock — mottled skin, prolonged CRT >3 seconds, weak pulse, altered consciousness
   D: Mild irritability and slightly sunken fontanelle
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Severe dehydration (>10% body weight loss) presents with shock signs — mottled or grey skin, prolonged CRT, weak or absent peripheral pulses, altered consciousness. This requires immediate IV fluid resuscitation (20mL/kg normal saline bolus) and emergency transfer. Moderate dehydration (5–10%) shows sunken eyes, reduced turgor, and decreased urine output — managed with oral rehydration solution if tolerating.

7. Q: A 9-month-old has had 3 days of diarrhoea and vomiting. She is alert, has mildly dry lips, normal skin turgor, and has had 3 wet nappies today (reduced from usual 6). What is the most appropriate management?
   A: IV fluids in hospital — any dehydration in an infant requires admission
   B: Oral rehydration solution (ORS) 5mL every 1–2 minutes, reassess in 4 hours
   C: Continue normal feeds only — ORS is not necessary for mild dehydration
   D: Ondansetron and antibiotics
   Correct: B
   Explanation: This infant has mild dehydration (~3–5%). Oral rehydration solution (ORS) is first-line. Small frequent volumes (5mL every 1–2 minutes) prevents vomiting. Reassess after 4 hours of oral rehydration. Ondansetron 0.15mg/kg oral can be used if vomiting prevents ORS intake. Antibiotics are not indicated for viral gastroenteritis.

8. Q: A 7-year-old presents with 3 days of dysuria, frequency, and low-grade fever. Urine dipstick shows leukocytes ++ and nitrites +. What is the correct interpretation and management?
   A: Normal finding — reassure and discharge
   B: Likely UTI — send urine MCS and treat empirically with trimethoprim for 3 days
   C: Likely UTI — send urine MCS and treat empirically with trimethoprim for 7 days
   D: Likely UTI — arrange urgent renal ultrasound before starting antibiotics
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Positive leukocytes and nitrites with UTI symptoms in a school-age child is a UTI until proven otherwise. Australian Therapeutic Guidelines recommend 7 days of trimethoprim for uncomplicated UTI in children (vs 3 days in adult women). Send urine MCS before starting antibiotics. Renal ultrasound is indicated after first UTI in children under 6 months or recurrent UTIs — not routinely required for a first UTI in a 7-year-old.

9. Q: A mother brings her 15-month-old son for a developmental check. He is not yet walking independently, says 3 words, and does not point to objects. Which finding is the most concerning developmental red flag?
   A: Not walking at 15 months — most children walk by 12 months
   B: Only 3 words — most 15-month-olds say 5 or more words
   C: Not pointing — absence of pointing by 12 months is a red flag for autism spectrum disorder
   D: All three findings are equally concerning
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Absence of pointing by 12 months (and certainly by 15 months) is a significant red flag for autism spectrum disorder. Pointing is a key joint attention behaviour. Not walking at 15 months is also a red flag (typical range 9–15 months, most walk by 12–13 months). This child warrants urgent developmental assessment including M-CHAT screening and referral to paediatrician. Do not adopt a wait-and-see approach with multiple developmental concerns.

10. Q: A 6-year-old has a generalised tonic-clonic seizure lasting 4 minutes in the context of a fever of 39.2°C. This is his first seizure. He recovers fully within 10 minutes. What is the correct classification and management?
    A: Complex febrile convulsion — admit for EEG and neurology review
    B: Simple febrile convulsion — investigate and treat the source of fever, reassure parents
    C: Epilepsy — start anticonvulsant therapy
    D: Meningitis until proven otherwise — lumbar puncture required
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Simple febrile convulsion criteria: generalised (not focal), <15 minutes, single episode in 24 hours, full recovery, age 6 months to 5 years (6 years is borderline). This meets simple criteria. Investigate and treat the source of fever. Lumbar puncture is NOT routinely required for a typical simple febrile convulsion in a child over 18 months who is fully immunised and recovered well. Reassure parents — recurrence risk is 30% but long-term prognosis is excellent.

11. Q: What is the recommended first-line oral antibiotic for a child with non-severe community-acquired pneumonia confirmed on CXR, with no penicillin allergy?
    A: Azithromycin 10mg/kg daily for 5 days
    B: Amoxicillin 25mg/kg (max 500mg) three times daily for 5 days
    C: Cefalexin 25mg/kg (max 500mg) four times daily for 7 days
    D: Co-amoxiclav 22.5mg/kg twice daily for 5 days
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Australian Therapeutic Guidelines recommend amoxicillin as first-line for non-severe CAP in children. Amoxicillin covers Streptococcus pneumoniae which is the most common bacterial cause. Azithromycin or doxycycline (in older children) is used if atypical organisms are suspected. Duration is 5 days for non-severe CAP.

12. Q: A 4-year-old presents with a widespread vesicular rash starting on the trunk, spreading to the face and limbs, with fever 38.5°C. The child is otherwise well. What is the diagnosis and appropriate management?
    A: Hand foot and mouth disease — supportive care, no exclusion required
    B: Chickenpox (varicella) — supportive care, exclude from childcare until all lesions crusted
    C: Impetigo — oral flucloxacillin for 5 days
    D: Meningococcal — call 000 immediately
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Chickenpox presents with vesicular rash starting centrally (trunk) and spreading peripherally, with fever. Management is supportive — paracetamol (not ibuprofen or aspirin — Reye syndrome risk), calamine lotion, trim nails. Exclude from childcare/school until ALL lesions have crusted (typically 5–7 days). Aciclovir is considered for immunocompromised children or severe cases. Varicella is now on the Australian NIP at 18 months.

13. Q: Which of the following is the most appropriate management of a 2-year-old with mild eczema affecting the antecubital fossae and popliteal fossae?
    A: Oral antihistamines daily and topical hydrocortisone 1% as needed
    B: Regular emollient application, topical hydrocortisone 1% for flares, trigger identification
    C: Topical betamethasone valerate 0.1% (potent steroid) twice daily
    D: Oral prednisolone for 5 days to settle the flare
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Mild eczema management follows a stepwise approach. Regular emollients (applied at least twice daily) are the cornerstone — they restore the skin barrier. Low-potency topical corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 1%) are used for flares on the body. Potent steroids are not appropriate first-line in a 2-year-old. Trigger identification (soap, dust mites, heat, certain foods) and avoidance is important. Oral antihistamines have limited evidence in eczema but may help sleep disruption.

14. Q: A 14-year-old female presents with 6 months of low mood, loss of interest, poor concentration, and social withdrawal. She denies self-harm. PHQ-A score is 14 (moderate depression). What is the most appropriate next step?
    A: Start fluoxetine 10mg daily immediately
    B: Reassure — adolescent low mood is normal and will resolve
    C: Psychological therapy first-line — refer to psychologist; safety plan; involve family; review in 2 weeks
    D: Admit to inpatient psychiatry
    Correct: C
    Explanation: For moderate adolescent depression, psychological therapy (CBT or interpersonal therapy) is first-line before medication. Refer to a psychologist under a Mental Health Care Plan (Medicare item). Develop a safety plan, involve family with consent, and arrange close follow-up. SSRIs (fluoxetine is the preferred agent in adolescents if medication needed) are considered if psychological therapy is ineffective or unavailable, or for severe depression. Always assess suicide risk at every visit.

15. Q: A parent asks whether their 5-year-old needs antibiotic treatment for acute otitis media. The child has had ear pain for 2 days, temperature 37.8°C, and otoscopy shows a red bulging right tympanic membrane. What is the evidence-based approach?
    A: All children with AOM require immediate antibiotics
    B: Watchful waiting for 48–72 hours is appropriate for children over 2 years with non-severe AOM; amoxicillin if no improvement
    C: Antibiotics are never indicated for AOM — it always resolves spontaneously
    D: Refer to ENT for myringotomy
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Australian and international guidelines support watchful waiting for 48–72 hours in children over 2 years with non-severe AOM (temperature <39°C, mild-moderate pain, not toxic). Most AOM resolves without antibiotics. A safety net prescription (to fill only if no improvement in 48–72 hours) is a practical approach. Immediate antibiotics are recommended for: age <2 years, severe AOM (T≥39°C, severe pain), bilateral AOM, otorrhoea, or immunocompromised.

16. Q: A 10-year-old boy is referred by his school for assessment of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity present in both school and home settings for over 12 months. What is the correct diagnostic approach for ADHD?
    A: Start methylphenidate immediately based on parental and teacher reports
    B: Comprehensive assessment including standardised rating scales from multiple settings, developmental history, examination, and exclusion of other causes
    C: EEG to rule out absence seizures before diagnosing ADHD
    D: ADHD cannot be diagnosed in primary care — refer to paediatrician only
    Correct: B
    Explanation: ADHD diagnosis requires comprehensive assessment — symptoms present in multiple settings (home AND school), duration >6 months, onset before age 12, causing functional impairment. Use standardised rating scales (Conners, SNAP-IV) from parents AND teachers. Take full developmental, educational, and family history. Examine to exclude medical causes. Diagnosis can be made by NPs, GPs, or paediatricians with appropriate training. Medication is not first-line for mild-moderate ADHD in school-age children — behavioural therapy and school support strategies first.

17. Q: What is the correct paracetamol dose for a child weighing 14kg?
    A: 120mg (one junior paracetamol tablet)
    B: 210mg (15mg/kg)
    C: 500mg (adult dose)
    D: 140mg (10mg/kg)
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Paracetamol dose is 15mg/kg per dose, every 4–6 hours, maximum 4 doses in 24 hours. For a 14kg child: 15 x 14 = 210mg per dose. Always dose by weight in children, not age. Maximum single dose is 1g, maximum daily dose is 4g (rarely reached in children). Underdosing is common when parents use age-based dosing — weight-based dosing is more accurate and effective.

18. Q: A 6-month-old is brought in with a spiral fracture of the femur. The parents report the child "rolled off the change table." The child has two healing bruises on the torso noted incidentally. What is the most appropriate response?
    A: Accept the history and treat the fracture — accidents happen
    B: This presentation raises significant concern for non-accidental injury — mandatory report to child protection, senior clinician involvement, do not discharge without safety assessment
    C: Arrange bone density scan to exclude osteogenesis imperfecta before reporting
    D: Ask the parents to return in 1 week for review
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Spiral femur fracture in a pre-mobile infant is highly suspicious for non-accidental injury (NAI). A 6-month-old cannot roll off a change table with enough force to cause this fracture. Multiple bruises in a non-mobile infant are another major red flag. NPs are mandatory reporters — you are legally required to report to child protection if you have reasonable grounds to suspect abuse. Do not discharge the child without senior clinician review and a safety assessment. Document meticulously.

19. Q: A 3-year-old presents with her third episode of wheeze in 12 months, each triggered by viral URTIs. She has no wheeze between episodes and normal examination today. What is the most appropriate management?
    A: Diagnose asthma and start daily inhaled corticosteroid
    B: This is likely episodic viral wheeze — salbutamol MDI with spacer for acute episodes, reassess pattern at age 5
    C: Refer to respiratory paediatrician urgently
    D: Oral prednisolone course and chest X-ray
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Episodic (viral) wheeze is common in preschool children and is distinct from asthma. Children wheeze only with viral URTIs, are well between episodes, and most resolve by school age. Daily preventive ICS is not routinely recommended for episodic viral wheeze in children under 5. Salbutamol MDI with spacer is appropriate for acute episodes. Reassess at age 5 when spirometry becomes reliable. Refer if: severe or frequent episodes, wheeze triggers other than viruses, faltering growth, or persistent symptoms between episodes.

20. Q: Which vaccine is given at the 12-month visit on the Australian National Immunisation Program schedule?
    A: DTaP-IPV-HepB-Hib (hexavalent) only
    B: MMRV (measles-mumps-rubella-varicella) and MenACWY
    C: MMRV and PCV13 (pneumococcal)
    D: Influenza and hepatitis A
    Correct: B
    Explanation: At 12 months on the Australian NIP, children receive MMRV (measles-mumps-rubella-varicella combined vaccine) and MenACWY (meningococcal ACWY). This changed in 2024 with the addition of MenACWY at 12 months. Always check the current NIP schedule at immunise.health.gov.au and verify the child's AIR record before vaccinating — the schedule is updated periodically.
