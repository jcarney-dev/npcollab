## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Alcohol withdrawal seizure — diazepam IV/IM, call 000
- Delirium tremens — agitation, fever, hallucinations, autonomic instability — emergency transfer
- Opioid overdose — unresponsive, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression — naloxone, call 000
- Stimulant toxicity — chest pain, severe hypertension, arrhythmia — emergency transfer
- Wernicke's encephalopathy — confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia — IV thiamine urgently
- Acute alcohol poisoning — GCS impairment, aspiration risk, call 000
- Serotonin syndrome — agitation, clonus, hyperthermia, tachycardia — emergency transfer
- Suicidal ideation in intoxicated patient — immediate safety assessment

### Core Principles for NP Practice
Substance use disorders are common, undertreated, and highly stigmatised. NPs are well-positioned to provide non-judgmental, evidence-based care in primary care and community settings. Key principles:
- Use non-stigmatising language — "person with alcohol use disorder" not "alcoholic"
- Brief intervention is highly effective — even 5 minutes makes a difference
- Harm reduction is a valid and evidence-based approach
- Pharmacotherapy (buprenorphine, naltrexone, acamprosate) significantly improves outcomes
- Treat co-occurring mental health conditions — they are the rule, not the exception
- Recovery is possible — relapse is part of the process, not failure

### Alcohol

#### Screening
- AUDIT (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test) — 10 questions, comprehensive
- AUDIT-C — 3-question brief screen
- Single question: "How often do you have 6 or more standard drinks on one occasion?" — positive screen if any frequency >never
- Australian guidelines: no more than 4 standard drinks on any day, no more than 10 per week

#### Brief Intervention — FRAMES
- Feedback — give personalised feedback on risks
- Responsibility — emphasise personal responsibility for change
- Advice — give clear advice to change
- Menu — offer a range of options
- Empathy — use empathic counselling style
- Self-efficacy — reinforce belief in ability to change

#### Alcohol Use Disorder
Alcohol use disorder (AUD) encompasses hazardous, harmful, and dependent drinking. Pharmacotherapy options:
- **Naltrexone** (oral or depot): reduces craving and reward. Avoid if opioid-dependent or liver disease. PBS-listed.
- **Acamprosate**: reduces protracted withdrawal symptoms, best started after detox. PBS-listed.
- **Disulfiram**: aversion therapy — causes unpleasant reaction with alcohol. Requires commitment and monitoring.
- **Nalmefene**: reduces heavy drinking days — newer agent.

#### Alcohol Withdrawal
Risk of withdrawal in physically dependent drinkers who reduce or stop abruptly. CIWA-Ar scale assesses severity. Symptoms: tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea (mild); hallucinations (moderate); seizures, delirium tremens (severe — life-threatening).

Management: diazepam symptom-triggered protocol for moderate-severe withdrawal (10–20mg PO initially, repeat as needed using CIWA-Ar score). Thiamine 300mg IV before any glucose — prevents Wernicke's encephalopathy. Hospital admission for severe withdrawal, seizure history, comorbidities, or social issues.

#### Wernicke's Encephalopathy
Thiamine deficiency — medical emergency. Classic triad: confusion, ophthalmoplegia (nystagmus, lateral gaze palsy), ataxia. Only one-third have complete triad. Treat ANY suspected case with IV thiamine 500mg TDS for 3–5 days (do not wait for confirmation). Oral thiamine is poorly absorbed and inadequate for treatment.

### Opioids

#### Opioid Use Disorder
Opioid use disorder (OUD) can involve heroin, illicit fentanyl, or prescription opioids. Opioid agonist treatment (OAT) is the gold standard — significantly reduces mortality, crime, and blood-borne virus transmission.

**Buprenorphine-naloxone (Suboxone)**: First-line OAT. Sublingual formulation. Partial opioid agonist — ceiling effect on respiratory depression, safer than methadone. Monthly depot injection (Brixta, Sublocade) improves adherence.

**Methadone**: Full opioid agonist, higher overdose risk, more complex prescribing. Supervised dosing required. Requires specific prescriber authority.

NPs can prescribe buprenorphine in most Australian states with appropriate training and authority. This is a significant area of NP scope expansion that improves access to treatment.

#### Opioid Overdose
Recognise: unconscious or unresponsive, slow/shallow breathing or apnoea, pinpoint pupils, cyanosis, gurgling respirations.

Management:
1. Call 000
2. Stimulate — sternal rub, call name
3. Position recovery position if breathing, or airway management if not
4. Naloxone (Narcan) — 400mcg IM or intranasal. Repeat every 2–3 minutes up to 3 doses if no response. Duration shorter than most opioids — patient may re-sedate
5. Rescue breathing if apnoeic
6. Stay until ambulance arrives

Naloxone is available over the counter in Australian pharmacies. NPs should prescribe take-home naloxone to all patients on opioids and their carers.

### Stimulants

#### Methamphetamine
High rates of use in Australia — particularly in rural and remote areas. Presentations: acute intoxication (agitation, paranoia, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia), chronic use (dental erosion, skin picking, cognitive impairment, psychosis).

Management of acute methamphetamine toxicity: calm environment, benzodiazepines for agitation (diazepam or lorazepam), cooling for hyperthermia, antihypertensives for severe hypertension, antipsychotics for psychosis. No specific antidote.

Methamphetamine-induced psychosis: can be indistinguishable from primary psychosis. Treat with antipsychotics. Most resolves with abstinence — some persists.

#### Cocaine
Less prevalent in Australia than methamphetamine. Cardiovascular complications are the primary concern — myocardial infarction (even in young people), arrhythmias, aortic dissection. Avoid beta-blockers in acute cocaine toxicity — causes unopposed alpha-adrenergic stimulation worsening hypertension and coronary spasm.

### Cannabis
Most widely used illicit drug in Australia. Cannabis use disorder is common — characterised by dependence, tolerance, and withdrawal (irritability, anxiety, insomnia, appetite loss). Associated with psychosis risk (particularly high-THC products), cognitive impairment in adolescents, and respiratory disease from smoking. No approved pharmacotherapy — CBT and motivational interviewing are evidence-based. Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: cyclical vomiting in heavy cannabis users, relieved by hot showers — haloperidol effective acutely.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Substance Use History

#### Screening Questions
- Do you drink alcohol, and how much?
- Do you use any recreational drugs or substances?
- Do you use any medications not prescribed to you?
- Have you ever used intravenous drugs?

Use validated screening tools:
- AUDIT / AUDIT-C — alcohol
- DAST-10 (Drug Abuse Screening Test) — drugs
- CAGE questionnaire — alcohol dependence screen

#### For Each Substance Identified
- Type of substance
- Route of use — oral, inhaled, injected, intranasal
- Frequency and quantity
- Age of first use and duration of current use
- Last use
- Any periods of abstinence — what helped?
- Previous treatment attempts
- Withdrawal history — has the patient had withdrawal symptoms or seizures before?
- Dependence features — craving, tolerance, loss of control, withdrawal

#### Harm Assessment
- Sharing needles or injecting equipment — BBV risk
- Safe sex practices
- Driving under the influence
- Overdose history — when, where, was naloxone used?
- Current support network
- Employment and housing stability
- Child protection concerns — are there children in the household?

#### Mental Health Screen
- Depression — PHQ-9
- Anxiety — GAD-7
- PTSD — comorbidity extremely common in substance use disorders
- Psychosis symptoms
- Suicide and self-harm risk — always ask

#### Physical Examination
- General: nutritional state, hygiene, track marks, skin infections
- Vital signs: BP, HR (withdrawal features), temperature
- Neurological: tremor, confusion, nystagmus, ataxia (Wernicke's)
- Cardiovascular: murmurs (endocarditis in PWID)
- Abdomen: hepatomegaly, liver tenderness (alcohol)
- Mental state: intoxication, withdrawal, psychosis

#### Investigations
- FBE — anaemia (alcohol), infection (PWID)
- LFTs — GGT elevated in alcohol use, hepatitis
- EUC — electrolytes in alcohol withdrawal
- Blood glucose — hypoglycaemia in alcohol intoxication
- Urine drug screen — confirms substances present (not quantitative)
- Hepatitis B surface antigen and antibody
- Hepatitis C antibody and RNA
- HIV serology
- STI screen if relevant
- Breathalyser — if available and clinically indicated
- CIWA-Ar score — alcohol withdrawal severity assessment

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 42-year-old with Alcohol Use Disorder Seeking Help

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 42-year-old male presents requesting help to stop drinking.

History: Has been drinking heavily for approximately 15 years. Currently consuming approximately 15 standard drinks daily — starting in the morning to prevent shakiness. Attempted to stop twice before — experienced severe shaking, sweating, and on one occasion a seizure at day 2 of abstinence (12 months ago). Has not told his employer. His wife has threatened to leave. Two children aged 8 and 11 at home. Lost his driver's licence 6 months ago due to drink driving.

AUDIT Score: 36/40 (severe AUD)

Mental Health: Low mood, not sleeping well. PHQ-9: 14 (moderate depression). Denies active suicidal ideation currently — has had passive thoughts in the past. No psychosis.

Substances: Alcohol only. Has smoked 15 cigarettes per day for 20 years. No illicit drugs.

Patient Goals: Wants to stop completely. Motivated by family situation. Understands he cannot stop abruptly at home given seizure history.

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: BP 158/96 | HR 96 | Temp 37.1°C

General: Mildly tremulous hands at rest. Mildly diaphoretic. Alert and oriented. CIWA-Ar score: 8 (mild withdrawal — last drink 4 hours ago).

Abdominal: Mild hepatomegaly, non-tender. No ascites. No spider naevi.

Neurological: Mild peripheral tremor. No nystagmus. No ataxia. No asterixis.

Investigations:
- GGT: 248 (markedly elevated)
- ALT: 68, AST: 95 (elevated, AST:ALT >2:1 consistent with alcoholic liver disease)
- MCV: 102 (elevated — macrocytosis consistent with alcohol use)
- FBE: otherwise normal
- EUC: Na 136, K 3.4 (low-normal — replete), Cr normal
- Blood glucose: 5.8

**A — Assessment**
1. Severe alcohol use disorder with physical dependence — seizure history on previous withdrawal — HIGH RISK for complicated withdrawal
2. Likely alcoholic liver disease — elevated GGT, AST>ALT, macrocytosis
3. Moderate depression — likely partly alcohol-related but requires independent assessment after withdrawal
4. Thiamine deficiency risk — alcohol-dependent patient

**P — Plan**
Withdrawal Management:
- Given seizure history and severity of dependence — inpatient detoxification is strongly recommended and safest. Discussed and agreed.
- Refer to local hospital alcohol and drug service or inpatient detox unit today
- If inpatient unavailable — community-managed withdrawal with DAILY review and CIWA-Ar monitoring, family support, strict safety netting

Thiamine:
- Thiamine 300mg IV immediately (given in clinic today) — do not wait
- Arrange thiamine 100mg oral TDS ongoing after initial IV course
- Education to family regarding Wernicke's signs

Pharmacotherapy for Withdrawal (to be prescribed by detox team):
- Symptom-triggered diazepam protocol based on CIWA-Ar score
- Thiamine supplementation throughout

Pharmacotherapy for Maintaining Abstinence (post-detox):
- Discuss naltrexone or acamprosate once through withdrawal — start after medically stable
- Naltrexone 50mg daily — reduces craving; or acamprosate 666mg TDS — reduces protracted withdrawal

Mental Health:
- Depression assessment to be repeated at 4 weeks post-detox — much may be alcohol-related
- Low-dose antidepressant to be considered if depression persists after abstinence established

Referral:
- Alcohol and Drug Service for inpatient detoxification today
- Alcoholics Anonymous / SMART Recovery — peer support
- Family counselling — wife involved with consent

Safety Planning:
- Emergency contacts given — 24-hour alcohol helpline 1800 250 015
- Wife aware of Wernicke's warning signs
- Child protection not currently indicated — children not at immediate risk, wife present and capable

Review: After discharge from detox — within 1 week.

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A patient scores 18 on the AUDIT questionnaire. What does this indicate and what is the appropriate response?
   A: Normal drinking — no intervention needed
   B: Hazardous drinking — brief advice only
   C: Likely alcohol use disorder — comprehensive assessment, brief intervention, and discussion of treatment options including pharmacotherapy
   D: Immediate hospital admission required
   Correct: C
   Explanation: AUDIT scores: 0–7 = low risk, 8–15 = hazardous/harmful drinking (brief intervention), 16–19 = high-risk drinking (brief counselling and monitoring), 20–40 = likely dependence (referral to specialist). A score of 18 indicates high-risk drinking consistent with alcohol use disorder — requires comprehensive assessment including dependence features, withdrawal history, physical examination, and discussion of treatment options. Pharmacotherapy (naltrexone, acamprosate) significantly improves outcomes and should be discussed.

2. Q: A patient dependent on alcohol tells you they plan to stop drinking "cold turkey" tonight at home. They report a seizure during their last withdrawal attempt. What is the most appropriate response?
   A: Support their decision — stopping drinking is always safe and positive
   B: Advise against unsupervised abrupt cessation given seizure history — arrange urgent supervised withdrawal (inpatient or closely monitored community detox) and prescribe thiamine
   C: Prescribe diazepam to take at home without review
   D: Refer to AA — no medical management needed
   Correct: B
   Explanation: A history of withdrawal seizure is a significant risk factor for further seizures and delirium tremens with subsequent withdrawal attempts. Unsupervised home detoxification is dangerous in this context. Arrange inpatient or intensive community-managed withdrawal with daily clinical review, CIWA-Ar monitoring, and symptom-triggered diazepam protocol. Prescribe thiamine before any glucose administration to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy. If inpatient is unavailable, the patient must not attempt withdrawal alone — family or carer support is essential.

3. Q: A person who injects drugs (PWID) presents to your clinic for wound management. You notice track marks and old injection sites. What is the most important additional health check to offer?
   A: Drug urine screen — to confirm substance use
   B: Blood-borne virus (BBV) testing — hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV serology
   C: Chest X-ray for tuberculosis
   D: Psychiatric assessment only
   Correct: B
   Explanation: People who inject drugs have high rates of blood-borne virus (BBV) transmission from sharing injecting equipment. Hepatitis C affects approximately 50% of PWID in Australia. Hepatitis B and HIV are also prevalent. Offer BBV testing at every opportunity — HCV antibody and RNA (to confirm active infection), HBsAg/anti-HBs/anti-HBc, and HIV serology. HCV is now curable with DAA therapy (8–12 weeks, >95% cure). HBV vaccination if not immune. Needle and syringe program referral, naloxone prescription, and STI screening are also important harm reduction interventions.

4. Q: What is the recommended treatment for opioid use disorder in Australian primary care?
   A: Methadone is the only approved opioid agonist treatment — must be dispensed daily at a pharmacy
   B: Buprenorphine-naloxone (Suboxone) sublingual or monthly depot buprenorphine — NPs can prescribe with appropriate authority in most states
   C: Naltrexone is the only evidence-based treatment — start immediately without medically supervised withdrawal
   D: Residential rehabilitation is required before pharmacotherapy
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Opioid agonist treatment (OAT) is the gold standard for opioid use disorder — significantly reduces mortality, illicit drug use, crime, and HIV/HCV transmission. Buprenorphine-naloxone (Suboxone) is first-line: partial opioid agonist with ceiling effect on respiratory depression — safer than methadone. Monthly depot formulations (Brixta, Sublocade) improve adherence and reduce diversion. NPs can prescribe buprenorphine in most Australian states with appropriate training and prescriber authority. Naltrexone (full antagonist) can be used for patients who have completed withdrawal and want to remain opioid-free — must not be started until fully withdrawn.

5. Q: You recognise a patient as unresponsive with pinpoint pupils, slow respirations (4/min), and cyanosis in the waiting room. A companion says they used heroin 30 minutes ago. What are the immediate actions?
   A: Wait for paramedics to arrive before doing anything
   B: Call 000, administer naloxone 400mcg IM or intranasal, perform rescue breathing if needed, position in recovery position if breathing resumes
   C: Administer flumazenil — this is a benzodiazepine overdose
   D: IV access only — naloxone is only for hospital use
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Opioid overdose: call 000 immediately. Administer naloxone — 400mcg IM (preferred for fast onset) or intranasal (2mg if using 2mg/0.1mL formulation). Repeat every 2–3 minutes up to 3 doses if no response. Perform rescue breathing if apnoeic. Recovery position if breathing resumes. Stay until paramedics arrive — naloxone duration (60–90 min) is shorter than most opioids and the patient may re-sedate. All clinical settings should have naloxone available. Flumazenil is for benzodiazepine overdose — pinpoint pupils strongly suggest opioids.

6. Q: Which statement about take-home naloxone in Australia is correct?
   A: Naloxone is a Schedule 8 drug and can only be administered by healthcare professionals
   B: Naloxone is available over the counter at pharmacies without a prescription and NPs should prescribe it to all patients at risk of opioid overdose and their carers
   C: Naloxone is only appropriate for people who use illicit opioids
   D: Take-home naloxone increases opioid use by providing a safety net
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Naloxone is available over the counter in Australian pharmacies (no prescription needed in most states). NPs should proactively prescribe take-home naloxone to: anyone on regular or high-dose opioids (prescribed or illicit), anyone with history of overdose, and their household contacts/carers. Evidence shows take-home naloxone saves lives without increasing opioid use. Include training on recognition of overdose and administration technique. The MATES program and peer-based naloxone distribution programs have significantly reduced overdose deaths in Australia.

7. Q: A 38-year-old woman presents 36 hours after her last drink. She has been drinking 2 bottles of wine daily for 5 years. She is tremulous, diaphoretic, agitated, has a mild headache, and CIWA-Ar score is 14. HR 108, BP 154/96. No hallucinations. No seizures yet. What is the most appropriate management?
   A: Reassurance only — mild withdrawal is self-limiting
   B: Oral diazepam 20mg stat, repeat 10–20mg every 1–2 hours based on CIWA-Ar score; IV thiamine; admit or arrange hourly monitoring
   C: Haloperidol 5mg IM for agitation
   D: Oral naltrexone to reduce craving immediately
   Correct: B
   Explanation: CIWA-Ar score 14 indicates moderate alcohol withdrawal — significant risk of progression to seizures or delirium tremens. Management: symptom-triggered diazepam protocol (loading dose 20mg oral, repeat 10–20mg every 1–2 hours until CIWA-Ar <8). Diazepam's long half-life provides self-tapering. IV thiamine 300mg before any glucose. Admit if: CIWA-Ar >15, seizure history, hallucinations, unable to take oral medications, social isolation, or comorbidities. Monitor closely — CIWA-Ar every 1 hour. Haloperidol lowers seizure threshold — avoid in withdrawal.

8. Q: Which of the following correctly describes the mechanism and indication for acamprosate in alcohol use disorder?
   A: Acamprosate is an opioid antagonist that reduces the rewarding effects of alcohol
   B: Acamprosate modulates GABA and glutamate systems to reduce protracted withdrawal symptoms (dysphoria, anxiety, craving) — most effective when started after detoxification and continued for 6–12 months
   C: Acamprosate causes an unpleasant reaction when alcohol is consumed
   D: Acamprosate is a benzodiazepine used for acute withdrawal management
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Acamprosate (Campral) modulates GABA and NMDA glutamate receptors to normalise the neurochemical dysregulation that persists for months after alcohol cessation — reducing protracted withdrawal symptoms including dysphoria, anxiety, and cravings. It is most effective when started after detoxification is complete. Dose: 666mg (2 tablets) TDS, regardless of size/weight. PBS-listed. Continue for 6–12 months. Works best in motivated patients committed to abstinence. Naltrexone (opioid antagonist) is the alternative — reduces alcohol reward and is suitable for patients who may drink occasionally. Disulfiram causes the aversion reaction.

9. Q: A 22-year-old presents with acute agitation, paranoia, and seeing things that aren't there. His friend reports he smoked "ice" (methamphetamine) 3 hours ago. His BP is 172/108, HR 124, Temp 37.9°C. What is the most appropriate immediate management?
   A: Haloperidol 10mg IM immediately — antipsychotics are first-line
   B: Place in a calm, quiet environment; oral or IV benzodiazepine (diazepam) for agitation first-line; monitor vitals; call 000 if not settling or deteriorating
   C: Naloxone — this presentation is consistent with opioid toxicity
   D: Discharge with GP follow-up — stimulant effects will wear off
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Acute methamphetamine toxicity with agitation and psychosis: benzodiazepines (diazepam 5–10mg oral or IV) are first-line to reduce agitation and prevent complications (hyperthermia, seizure, rhabdomyolysis). Calm environment, low stimulation. If inadequate response to benzodiazepines — droperidol or olanzapine (antipsychotics). Avoid typical antipsychotics (haloperidol) as first-line — may lower seizure threshold. Monitor for hyperthermia (most dangerous complication) — active cooling if needed. BP management if severely hypertensive. Call 000 if significant hyperthermia (>39°C), seizure, arrhythmia, or chest pain.

10. Q: A patient with hepatitis C and active injecting drug use asks about treatment. They continue to inject heroin. Should treatment be offered?
    A: No — patients must be abstinent for 12 months before HCV treatment
    B: Yes — active drug use is not a contraindication to DAA therapy. Treat with appropriate support, harm reduction, and monitoring for reinfection
    C: Only if the patient is enrolled in a methadone program
    D: Defer until they stop injecting — reinfection makes treatment pointless
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Active drug use is NOT a contraindication to hepatitis C direct-acting antiviral (DAA) treatment. Current Australian guidelines and clinical practice strongly support treating PWID — they have the highest HCV burden and benefit most from treatment. Cure rates with DAAs are >95% regardless of drug use status. Provide treatment alongside harm reduction (NSP, naloxone, OAT), education about reinfection risk, and follow-up HCV RNA testing 3 months post-treatment to confirm sustained virological response (SVR = cure). Reinfection can occur and be retreated. NPs can prescribe DAA therapy under PBS authority.

11. Q: Which of the following statements about cannabis use disorder is correct?
    A: Cannabis is not addictive — dependence cannot develop
    B: Cannabis use disorder affects approximately 9% of people who use cannabis and is characterised by tolerance, withdrawal, and loss of control — CBT and motivational interviewing are first-line treatments
    C: There is approved pharmacotherapy for cannabis use disorder in Australia
    D: Occasional cannabis use has no health risks
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Cannabis use disorder develops in approximately 9% of all users and up to 17% of those who start in adolescence. Withdrawal syndrome: irritability, anxiety, insomnia, decreased appetite, restlessness — peaks 2–3 days after cessation, resolves in 1–2 weeks. No approved pharmacotherapy in Australia — treatment is psychological: CBT, motivational interviewing, contingency management. Risks of regular use: psychosis (2–3x increased risk, especially high-THC products), cognitive impairment particularly with adolescent-onset use, cannabis use disorder, and respiratory disease from smoking. Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome is a specific syndrome of cyclical vomiting in heavy users.

12. Q: A 35-year-old male presents with severe chest pain, palpitations, and agitation after using cocaine. ECG shows ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF. His BP is 196/114. Which class of medication should be AVOIDED in cocaine-induced chest pain?
    A: Benzodiazepines
    B: Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN)
    C: Beta-blockers
    D: Aspirin
    Correct: C
    Explanation: Beta-blockers are contraindicated in cocaine toxicity — they cause unopposed alpha-adrenergic stimulation resulting in paradoxical worsening of hypertension and coronary artery spasm. Management of cocaine-induced chest pain: benzodiazepines (reduce sympathetic drive and agitation), GTN (vasodilate coronary arteries), aspirin, and calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem — for coronary spasm). Coronary angiography as for any ACS. Cocaine-induced MI occurs even in young people with normal coronaries due to vasospasm and thrombosis. Urine drug screen confirms cocaine use.

13. Q: What is the CIWA-Ar scale used for in alcohol management?
    A: Screening for alcohol use disorder in the community
    B: Quantifying the severity of alcohol withdrawal symptoms to guide medication dosing — scores above 8 typically require pharmacotherapy
    C: Measuring blood alcohol concentration
    D: Assessing liver function in alcohol-related liver disease
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The CIWA-Ar (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, revised) is a 10-item validated scale assessing the severity of alcohol withdrawal: nausea, tremor, diaphoresis, anxiety, agitation, perceptual disturbances, headache, and orientation. Score 0–8 = mild (monitor, no medication), 8–15 = moderate (pharmacotherapy recommended), >15 = severe (urgent treatment, hospital admission). Symptom-triggered therapy guided by CIWA-Ar is superior to fixed-dose diazepam schedules — reduces total benzodiazepine use and duration of treatment. Reassess every 1–4 hours during active withdrawal.

14. Q: A patient on methadone maintenance treatment presents to your clinic with mild left leg cellulitis. They ask if their usual pharmacy dose needs to be adjusted. What is your approach?
    A: Stop methadone during the acute illness — opioids are immunosuppressive
    B: Continue methadone at current dose — coordinate with prescribing doctor and dispensing pharmacy; treat cellulitis with appropriate antibiotics; be aware of drug interactions
    C: Double the methadone dose for pain management
    D: Switch to buprenorphine during the acute illness
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Continue methadone at the prescribed dose — abrupt cessation or dose changes without specialist input can destabilise OAT and precipitate withdrawal, drug use relapse, and overdose risk. Contact the patient's OAT prescriber to inform them of the acute illness. Be aware of drug interactions: some antibiotics (e.g. fluconazole, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin) increase methadone levels — may need dose adjustment. If hospitalisation is required, ensure methadone continuation is arranged with the ward team — failure to continue OAT in hospital is a patient safety issue. Treat the cellulitis appropriately. Harm reduction considerations: assess BBV risk, injection site care.

15. Q: A mother of two young children presents asking for help reducing her alcohol consumption. She is drinking 6 standard drinks daily and is worried about losing her children. She is breastfeeding a 6-month-old infant. Which pharmacotherapy option is CONTRAINDICATED?
    A: Brief psychological intervention — safe in breastfeeding
    B: Acamprosate — limited data but generally considered lower risk
    C: Naltrexone — present in breast milk, breastfeeding not recommended
    D: Thiamine supplementation
    Correct: C
    Explanation: Naltrexone is present in breast milk and is not recommended during breastfeeding due to potential effects on the infant — breastfeeding should be discouraged if naltrexone is used. Acamprosate has limited data in breastfeeding — decision should be made on risk-benefit basis in consultation with a specialist. Disulfiram is also contraindicated in breastfeeding. Brief intervention and psychological support are safe and should be the primary approach. Thiamine supplementation is safe and important if nutritional deficiency is a concern. This case also raises child protection considerations — assess maternal functioning, parenting capacity, and children's safety.

16. Q: A 48-year-old man presents confused, with nystagmus and difficulty walking. He has been drinking heavily for years and has been unwell with vomiting for 3 days. Blood glucose is 3.2 mmol/L. What must be done BEFORE administering glucose?
    A: CT brain — to exclude intracranial haemorrhage
    B: IV thiamine 500mg — Wernicke's encephalopathy must be treated before glucose administration; glucose without thiamine can precipitate acute Wernicke's
    C: Blood alcohol level — to confirm intoxication
    D: Oral thiamine 100mg — adequate to prevent Wernicke's
    Correct: B
    Explanation: In any malnourished or alcohol-dependent patient who is confused and may require glucose, IV thiamine MUST be given before glucose. Administering glucose to a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate acute Wernicke's encephalopathy by depleting the limited remaining thiamine stores in glucose metabolism. This patient has the classic triad: confusion, nystagmus (ophthalmoplegia), and ataxia — treat as Wernicke's encephalopathy. IV thiamine 500mg TDS for 3–5 days. Oral thiamine is inadequate for treatment — poor absorption in malnourished/alcohol-dependent patients. Do not wait for blood levels or confirmation.

17. Q: Which of the following is considered a harm reduction intervention for people who inject drugs?
    A: Mandatory drug testing before accessing healthcare
    B: Needle and Syringe Programs (NSP), naloxone provision, hepatitis C treatment, and supervised injecting facilities
    C: Refusing treatment until the patient is abstinent
    D: Reporting all PWID to police as a public health measure
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Harm reduction is an evidence-based public health approach that aims to reduce the negative consequences of drug use without requiring abstinence. Key interventions: Needle and Syringe Programs (NSP) — reduce HCV and HIV transmission; take-home naloxone — reduces overdose deaths; hepatitis C treatment — curative even with ongoing use; supervised injecting facilities (available in some Australian cities); drug checking services; opioid agonist treatment; and hepatitis B vaccination. Harm reduction is cost-effective and saves lives. Withholding healthcare, mandatory testing, or punitive approaches increase harms and reduce engagement with services.

18. Q: A patient on buprenorphine-naloxone for opioid use disorder presents with a severe dental abscess requiring IV antibiotics. They are in significant pain. Which analgesic approach is most appropriate?
    A: Withhold all opioid analgesia — the patient is an addict and will abuse it
    B: Continue buprenorphine at current dose; add regular paracetamol, NSAIDs, and local anaesthetic. If opioid analgesia is required, higher doses may be needed due to buprenorphine cross-tolerance — consult with OAT prescriber
    C: Stop buprenorphine immediately — it will block all pain relief
    D: Double the buprenorphine dose for pain management
    Correct: B
    Explanation: People on buprenorphine OAT have the same right to adequate pain management as any other patient. Continue buprenorphine — stopping it risks withdrawal and destabilisation. For mild-moderate pain: non-opioid analgesics (paracetamol, NSAIDs). If opioid analgesia is needed for severe pain: higher doses of full opioid agonists may be required due to buprenorphine cross-tolerance and partial agonist competitive binding. Involve pain management or addiction medicine specialist if available. Never withhold pain management based on addiction history alone — this is discriminatory and harmful. Dental treatment under appropriate anaesthesia for the abscess.

19. Q: What is the recommended approach to supporting a patient who relapses to drinking after a period of abstinence?
    A: Discharge from your care — repeated relapse means treatment is not working
    B: Acknowledge the relapse non-judgementally, explore what happened, reinforce what went well, review the treatment plan, and continue pharmacotherapy and psychological support — relapse is a normal part of recovery
    C: Increase supervised dispensing of medications as punishment
    D: Refer back to detox immediately — another withdrawal is required
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Relapse is a normal and expected part of recovery from alcohol use disorder — it does not indicate treatment failure or that the patient is not trying. The model of addiction as a chronic relapsing condition means that relapse should be responded to in the same way as relapse in diabetes or hypertension — review, adjust the plan, and continue. Use motivational interviewing techniques: explore ambivalence, identify triggers, reinforce self-efficacy. Continue or restart pharmacotherapy. Review psychological support. Stigmatising responses to relapse cause disengagement from care and worse outcomes. Each attempt at sobriety builds skills and insight.

20. Q: A 19-year-old presents with nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. He reports cyclical episodes of vomiting that last 24–48 hours, occurring every 2–3 weeks for the past year. He mentions that hot showers relieve his symptoms dramatically. He smokes cannabis daily. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    A: Cyclical vomiting syndrome — refer to gastroenterology
    B: Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome — advise cannabis cessation; haloperidol effective acutely
    C: Peptic ulcer disease — H. pylori testing and PPI
    D: Functional dyspepsia — low FODMAP diet
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS) is characterised by cyclical nausea and vomiting in heavy cannabis users, with the pathognomonic feature of relief with hot showers or bathing (compulsive bathing behaviour). Often misdiagnosed for years. Prevalence is increasing with higher-potency cannabis products. The only curative treatment is cannabis cessation — symptoms typically resolve within days to weeks of stopping. Acute management: haloperidol has the best evidence for acute antiemetic effect in CHS (superior to ondansetron). Topical capsaicin cream to the abdomen also shows benefit. The hot shower relief is so characteristic that its presence should always prompt asking about cannabis use.
