# Onco-Haematology — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Febrile neutropenia — fever ≥38°C in patient with neutrophils <0.5 — call 000, emergency transfer
- Spinal cord compression — back pain with neurological deficit in cancer patient — dexamethasone 16mg immediately, emergency transfer
- Superior vena cava obstruction — facial swelling, dyspnoea, arm oedema — dexamethasone, emergency transfer
- Tumour lysis syndrome — AKI, hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia after chemotherapy
- Massive haemorrhage in thrombocytopenic patient — emergency transfer
- Hyperviscosity syndrome — confusion, visual disturbance, bleeding in myeloma/polycythaemia
- Acute leukaemia presentation — bruising, bleeding, infection, anaemia, lymphadenopathy
- Bone pain crisis in myeloma — pathological fracture risk

### The NP Role in Oncology and Haematology
NPs in oncology and haematology settings manage a highly complex patient population requiring expert symptom management, treatment monitoring, and urgent complication recognition. Key NP roles include:

- Chemotherapy assessment and pre-treatment review
- Toxicity monitoring and grading (CTCAE)
- Supportive care — nausea, fatigue, mucositis, peripheral neuropathy
- Complication recognition — febrile neutropenia, VTE, treatment toxicity
- Survivorship care and long-term follow-up
- Palliative symptom management
- Cancer screening and early detection in primary care
- Haematological disorder monitoring (anticoagulation, anaemia, thrombocytopenia)

### Cancer Screening in Australian Primary Care

NPs have a key role in cancer screening promotion and coordination:

**Bowel cancer**: National Bowel Cancer Screening Program — free iFOBT every 2 years from age 45. Positive result requires colonoscopy.

**Breast cancer**: BreastScreen Australia — free biennial mammography for women 50–74. Women 40–49 and 75+ can self-refer.

**Cervical cancer**: National Cervical Screening Program — 5-yearly HPV test replacing Pap smear, ages 25–74. Transition to self-collection improving access.

**Prostate cancer**: No national screening program. PSA testing with informed consent after discussing benefits and harms. Discuss with men over 50 (or 45 with family history or African ancestry).

**Skin cancer**: No formal national screening program — opportunistic skin checks. Annual full skin examination in high-risk patients.

**Lung cancer**: Low-dose CT screening trials ongoing — not yet a national program. High-risk individuals: heavy smokers aged 55–74.

### Key Oncological Conditions

#### Febrile Neutropenia
Oncological emergency. Defined as: temperature ≥38°C AND absolute neutrophil count <0.5 x10⁹/L (or expected to fall below this). Risk of bacterial sepsis with rapid deterioration. MASCC score risk stratifies. Emergency transfer — do not wait for blood results before calling 000. IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftazidime) within 60 minutes of presentation.

#### Common Chemotherapy Side Effects
- Nausea and vomiting: CINV — highly emetogenic (cisplatin, cyclophosphamide) vs moderately emetogenic. Prophylaxis: 5-HT3 antagonist (ondansetron) + dexamethasone + NK1 antagonist (aprepitant) for high risk.
- Mucositis: mouth ulcers, dysphagia. Management: regular mouthwash (saline, sodium bicarbonate), palifermin for high-risk regimens, pain management.
- Peripheral neuropathy: oxaliplatin, vincristine, paclitaxel. Dose modification may be required.
- Alopecia: common with many agents — temporary in most cases.
- Fatigue: most common complaint — exercise, activity pacing, sleep hygiene.
- Myelosuppression: anaemia (transfusion if symptomatic), thrombocytopenia (platelet transfusion if <10 or bleeding), neutropenia (G-CSF, febrile neutropenia protocol).

#### Myeloma (Multiple Myeloma)
Malignancy of plasma cells. Median age 70. CRAB criteria: hyperCalcaemia, Renal impairment, Anaemia, Bone lesions. Presents: back pain, fatigue, recurrent infections, renal failure. Diagnosis: serum protein electrophoresis (M-protein), serum free light chains, bone marrow biopsy, whole body low-dose CT or PET-CT. Treatment: triplet induction (bortezomib + lenalidomide + dexamethasone), autologous stem cell transplant in eligible patients. NP role: monitoring for treatment toxicity, bone protection (zoledronic acid), infection prophylaxis, VTE prevention.

#### Lymphoma
Hodgkin lymphoma (HL): young adults, bimodal peak. B symptoms: fever, night sweats, weight loss >10%. Reed-Sternberg cells. Highly curable with ABVD chemotherapy.

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL): heterogeneous group. Ranges from indolent (follicular, <5-year median survival without treatment but slow progression) to aggressive (diffuse large B-cell lymphoma — DLBCL — requires urgent treatment). Presentation: lymphadenopathy, B symptoms, extranodal disease.

#### Leukaemia
Acute leukaemia (AML/ALL): pancytopenia, circulating blasts. Urgent haematology referral — treatment delays are dangerous. Chronic leukaemia (CML/CLL): often incidental finding on FBE. CML: Philadelphia chromosome, imatinib (tyrosine kinase inhibitor). CLL: surveillance for early stage, chemoimmunotherapy for advanced.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Oncology and Haematology History

#### Cancer History
- Primary diagnosis: type, stage, date of diagnosis
- Current treatment: chemotherapy regimen, cycle number, last treatment date
- Previous treatments: surgery, radiation, prior chemotherapy
- Performance status: ECOG (0–5) or Karnofsky scale
- Treatment intent: curative vs palliative
- Current clinical trial participation

#### Symptom Assessment
- Pain: site, character, severity (NRS 0–10), current analgesia, breakthrough use
- Fatigue: severity, impact on function and ADLs
- Nausea and vomiting: frequency, severity, relationship to treatment
- Mucositis: grade (I–IV WHO), impact on eating
- Peripheral neuropathy: numbness, tingling, functional impact
- Appetite and weight: recent changes
- Bowel and bladder function
- Skin: rash, hand-foot syndrome, radiation dermatitis

#### Infection Risk Assessment
- Current neutrophil count and nadir timing post-chemotherapy
- Fever or rigor: temperature, onset
- Potential infection source: respiratory, urinary, skin, central line
- Previous febrile neutropenia episodes

#### Haematological History
- Bleeding: easy bruising, prolonged bleeding, spontaneous haemorrhage
- Thrombosis: DVT, PE, arterial events
- Anticoagulation: indication, agent, dose, monitoring
- Family history: clotting disorder, haematological malignancy

### Oncology Examination

#### General
- Performance status: ECOG score — is the patient better or worse than last visit?
- Nutritional status: weight trend, muscle wasting, cachexia
- Lymphadenopathy: cervical, axillary, inguinal, supraclavicular — size, consistency, tenderness
- Anaemia: pallor, tachycardia
- Bleeding: purpura, petechiae, ecchymoses

#### Treatment-Specific
- Central venous access device: PICC, port, tunnelled line — insertion site, integrity, patency
- Radiation field: skin assessment in radiation area
- Peripheral neuropathy: light touch, proprioception, reflexes in affected limbs
- Mucositis: oral cavity examination — grade

#### Investigations
- FBE and differential — neutrophil count critical (febrile neutropenia threshold)
- EUC — renal toxicity (cisplatin), electrolytes
- LFTs — hepatotoxicity monitoring
- CRP — infection, inflammation
- Blood cultures x2 — febrile neutropenia (before antibiotics)
- Tumour markers: PSA (prostate), CA-125 (ovarian), CEA (colorectal), AFP (hepatic/testicular)
- Serum protein electrophoresis — myeloma monitoring
- Serum free light chains — myeloma monitoring
- Coagulation and D-dimer — VTE assessment
- Imaging: CT, PET-CT, MRI — response assessment, complication workup

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: Febrile Neutropenia — Day 10 Post-Chemotherapy

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 58-year-old female with DLBCL (diffuse large B-cell lymphoma) on R-CHOP cycle 3, presenting day 10 post-chemotherapy with fever and rigors.

History: Developed fever (39.1°C at home) with rigors 2 hours ago. Mild productive cough — no pleuritic chest pain. No dysuria. No diarrhoea. PICC line in situ left arm — insertion site appears slightly red but no pus. No mouth ulcers. Had G-CSF (filgrastim) days 5–9 post-chemotherapy — last dose yesterday.

FBE last cycle nadir: neutrophils 0.3 (day 8 post-cycle 2). Expected nadir this cycle: day 7–14.

Performance Status: ECOG 1 at last clinic — working part-time.

Medications: Rituximab (IV — given on day 1), cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone. Ondansetron, dexamethasone (completed). Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (PCP prevention). Aciclovir prophylaxis. Pantoprazole. No current antibiotics.

Allergies: Penicillin — rash (documented).

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: BP 106/68 | HR 114 | Temp 39.4°C | RR 22 | SpO2 95% on air

General: Unwell, flushed, rigoring. Alert but distressed.

PICC site: mild erythema 1cm around insertion site, no purulent discharge, line flushing easily, no cord palpable.

Respiratory: Mild tachypnoea. Bibasal reduced air entry. No wheeze. No consolidation on percussion.

Abdomen: Soft. No organomegaly. No peritonism.

Mouth: No mucositis.

Point of Care:
- FBE (stat): WCC 0.6, Neutrophils 0.2 — febrile neutropenia confirmed
- Blood glucose 9.2 (dexamethasone effect)
- Blood cultures x2 drawn from PICC (one lumen) and peripheral vein — before antibiotics

**A — Assessment**
1. Febrile neutropenia — MASCC score: age <60 (+5), outpatient status (+3), mild dehydration not requiring IV fluids (+3), solid tumour/lymphoma (+4), no COPD (+4), no hypotension (+5) = 23/26 — LOW RISK technically, but penicillin allergy limits outpatient antibiotic options and clinical picture warrants admission
2. Likely source: respiratory vs PICC line (cannot exclude line infection)
3. Penicillin allergy documented — piperacillin-tazobactam contraindicated

**P — Plan**
Emergency Management (call 000 — emergency transfer):
- Oxygen: titrate to SpO2 >94% — 2L via nasal prongs commenced
- IV access: PICC line used — additional peripheral access obtained
- Blood cultures: x2 drawn (PICC + peripheral) — done
- Antibiotics within 60 minutes — PENICILLIN ALLERGY:
  - Ceftazidime 2g IV 8-hourly (cephalosporin — cross-reactivity <2% with penicillin rash history — confirmed safe per allergy history review)
  - Add vancomycin 25mg/kg IV (PICC line suspected source — empirical MRSA cover)
- IV fluids: 500mL normal saline — hypotensive on presentation
- Antipyretics: paracetamol 1g IV
- Continue cotrimoxazole and aciclovir prophylaxis (PCP and viral cover)

Documentation for Transfer:
- DLBCL, R-CHOP cycle 3, day 10
- Febrile neutropenia — neutrophils 0.2
- PENICILLIN ALLERGY — ceftazidime and vancomycin given
- Blood cultures x2 drawn pre-antibiotics
- PICC line left arm — line infection not excluded
- MASCC score 23 — low risk score but clinical judgement to admit

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 52-year-old female on cycle 4 of FOLFOX chemotherapy for colorectal cancer presents with a temperature of 38.4°C. Her most recent FBE shows neutrophils 0.3 x10⁹/L. What is this presentation called and what is the most critical immediate action?
   A: Grade 2 neutropenia — monitor and recheck FBE in 24 hours
   B: Febrile neutropenia — call 000, draw blood cultures immediately, and administer IV broad-spectrum antibiotics within 60 minutes regardless of the identified source
   C: Post-chemotherapy fever — paracetamol and review in clinic tomorrow
   D: Sepsis from a known source — treat the source only
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Febrile neutropenia (FN) = temperature ≥38°C AND neutrophils <0.5 x10⁹/L. This is an oncological emergency with mortality risk of 5–10% even with treatment. The most critical action is IV broad-spectrum antibiotics within 60 minutes — every hour of delay increases mortality. Draw blood cultures (x2 — peripheral and central line if present) before antibiotics but do not delay antibiotics waiting for cultures. Emergency transfer. First-line antibiotics: piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftazidime. The MASCC score risk stratifies — but most patients with FN are admitted regardless of score in Australian practice.

2. Q: A 65-year-old male with prostate cancer and known bone metastases presents with new onset back pain and difficulty walking. On examination, he has bilateral leg weakness, reduced sensation in both legs, and urinary incontinence. What is the most urgent diagnosis and immediate management?
   A: Spinal cord compression — dexamethasone 16mg IV/oral immediately, emergency transfer for urgent MRI and oncological/neurosurgical review
   B: Bone pain from metastases — increase opioid analgesia
   C: DVT causing leg weakness — anticoagulation
   D: Peripheral neuropathy from chemotherapy — gabapentin
   Correct: A
   Explanation: New back pain with bilateral leg weakness, sensory changes, and bladder dysfunction in a patient with known bone metastases is metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC) until proven otherwise. This is an oncological emergency — irreversible paralysis occurs within hours without treatment. Immediate dexamethasone 16mg IV or oral to reduce cord oedema. Emergency transfer for urgent MRI spine and oncology/neurosurgical review. Treatment: radiotherapy (most common), surgical decompression, or both. Neurological function at time of treatment is the strongest predictor of outcome — time is critical. Do NOT wait for imaging before giving dexamethasone.

3. Q: Which of the following is the most appropriate first-line antiemetic regimen for a patient receiving highly emetogenic chemotherapy (cisplatin-based)?
   A: Metoclopramide alone — dopamine antagonist is sufficient
   B: Ondansetron (5-HT3 antagonist) + dexamethasone + aprepitant (NK1 antagonist) — triple therapy for highly emetogenic chemotherapy
   C: Prochlorperazine 10mg orally before chemotherapy
   D: Ginger tablets — evidence-based natural antiemetic
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) prophylaxis is stratified by emetogenic potential. Highly emetogenic chemotherapy (cisplatin, AC combinations): triple therapy — 5-HT3 antagonist (ondansetron, granisetron) + dexamethasone + NK1 antagonist (aprepitant). This combination reduces acute and delayed CINV significantly. Olanzapine added as fourth agent in some highly emetogenic regimens. Moderately emetogenic: 5-HT3 + dexamethasone. Low emetogenic: single agent or none. Poorly controlled CINV leads to dehydration, dose delays, and poor quality of life — aggressive prophylaxis is essential. Breakthrough antiemetics (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine) for inadequate control.

4. Q: A 70-year-old woman presents to your primary care clinic with fatigue, back pain, and recurrent chest infections over 6 months. Blood tests show: Hb 92 g/L, calcium 2.95 mmol/L, creatinine 142 µmol/L. CXR shows no acute infiltrate but multiple lytic lesions are noted on the thoracic spine incidentally. What is the most likely diagnosis and next step?
   A: Osteoporosis — start bisphosphonate and calcium supplement
   B: Multiple myeloma — urgent haematology referral; serum protein electrophoresis, serum free light chains, and urine Bence Jones protein
   C: Metastatic breast cancer — arrange mammogram
   D: Hyperparathyroidism — parathyroid hormone level
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The CRAB criteria for myeloma: hyperCalcaemia (2.95), Renal impairment (Cr 142), Anaemia (Hb 92), Bone lesions (lytic lesions on spine) — this patient has 4 of 4. Multiple myeloma is strongly suspected. Urgent haematology referral. Investigations: serum protein electrophoresis (M-protein spike), serum free light chains (kappa/lambda ratio), urine Bence Jones protein, whole-body low-dose CT or PET-CT for skeletal survey, bone marrow biopsy (definitive diagnosis). Start treatment: modern triplet regimens (bortezomib + lenalidomide + dexamethasone). Zoledronic acid for bone protection. Do not start bisphosphonate without haematology review first.

5. Q: A patient with non-Hodgkin lymphoma is found to have a platelet count of 8 x10⁹/L on routine monitoring 10 days post-chemotherapy. He has no active bleeding. What is the threshold for prophylactic platelet transfusion in most oncology centres?
   A: Platelets <50 — transfuse any patient below this level
   B: Platelets <10 x10⁹/L in a non-bleeding, non-febrile patient — prophylactic platelet transfusion to reduce spontaneous haemorrhage risk
   C: Only transfuse if the patient is actively bleeding — no prophylactic transfusions
   D: Platelets <20 — transfuse all oncology patients at this level
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Prophylactic platelet transfusion threshold in haematology/oncology: <10 x10⁹/L in stable, non-febrile, non-bleeding patients. Higher thresholds apply for: active bleeding (transfuse to maintain >50), fever or infection (transfuse to maintain >20), invasive procedures (>50 for most, >100 for neurosurgery/ophthalmology), anticoagulation (>50). This patient at 8 x10⁹/L meets the prophylactic transfusion threshold — arrange hospital review. One adult therapeutic dose (ATD) of platelets typically raises the count by 20–40 x10⁹/L. Post-transfusion platelet count should be checked 1 hour and 24 hours post-transfusion.

6. Q: Which cancer screening test is recommended for women aged 25–74 in Australia under the National Cervical Screening Program as of 2024?
   A: Annual Pap smear (cervical cytology)
   B: 5-yearly HPV test — with option of self-collection for eligible women; abnormal results trigger colposcopy referral
   C: Annual HPV test regardless of age
   D: Colposcopy every 3 years for all women
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Australia replaced the 2-yearly Pap smear with the 5-yearly HPV test in December 2017 — one of the first countries to do so. The HPV test detects high-risk HPV strains (the cause of 99.7% of cervical cancers) with higher sensitivity than cytology. Self-collection (vaginal swab) is now available to eligible women who are overdue or have barriers to speculum examination — improving access for First Nations women, survivors of sexual violence, and others. Abnormal HPV result → cervical cytology reflex test → colposcopy if indicated. HPV vaccination (Gardasil 9) has dramatically reduced HPV prevalence — program likely to achieve cervical cancer elimination in Australia.

7. Q: A 45-year-old male with Hodgkin lymphoma has completed 6 cycles of ABVD and is in remission. He asks about long-term monitoring. Which late effect is he most at risk of?
   A: No significant long-term effects — ABVD is well-tolerated
   B: Secondary malignancies (breast cancer in females, lung cancer, thyroid cancer in radiation field), cardiac toxicity (doxorubicin), pulmonary toxicity (bleomycin), and infertility — long-term survivorship follow-up required
   C: Renal failure — dialysis likely within 5 years
   D: Peripheral neuropathy only — no other late effects
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Hodgkin lymphoma survivors have excellent overall survival (>85% 5-year) but significant late treatment toxicity: Secondary malignancies — breast cancer in women irradiated to the mediastinum (risk increases after 10 years, annual mammography from age 25 or 8 years post-radiation), lung cancer (smoking cessation critical), thyroid cancer (in radiation field). Cardiac: doxorubicin (cardiomyopathy), mediastinal radiation (coronary artery disease, valvular disease). Pulmonary: bleomycin toxicity (diffusion capacity monitoring). Fertility: gonadotoxicity, sperm banking should be offered pre-treatment. Survivorship care plans and long-term follow-up with a survivorship clinic are essential.

8. Q: A 68-year-old male with CLL (chronic lymphocytic leukaemia) presents with a WCC of 180 x10⁹/L on routine monitoring. He is asymptomatic, has no enlarged lymph nodes, and Hb and platelets are normal. His Rai stage is 0. What is the most appropriate management?
   A: Urgent chemotherapy — WCC >100 always requires immediate treatment
   B: Active surveillance (watch and wait) — early stage CLL without symptoms or cytopenias does not benefit from early treatment; monitor FBE, LFTs, and examine for lymphadenopathy every 3–6 months
   C: Stem cell transplant referral immediately
   D: Rituxan monotherapy as maintenance
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Early stage CLL (Rai 0–II, Binet A–B without symptoms) does not benefit from early chemotherapy — multiple trials have shown no survival benefit and increased toxicity from early treatment. Indications for treatment: constitutional symptoms (B symptoms), progressive lymphadenopathy or organomegaly, progressive anaemia (Hb <100) or thrombocytopenia (platelets <100), rapidly rising lymphocyte count (doubling time <6 months), or significant disease burden. Active surveillance with regular monitoring is the standard of care for asymptomatic early CLL. Educate patient about signs requiring urgent review (fever suggesting infection, increasing fatigue, new lumps).

9. Q: A patient with breast cancer is on trastuzumab (Herceptin) — a HER2-targeted monoclonal antibody. Which organ system requires specific monitoring throughout treatment?
   A: Renal function — nephrotoxicity is the primary concern
   B: Cardiac function — trastuzumab causes cardiomyopathy (LVEF reduction); echocardiogram or MUGA scan every 3 months during treatment
   C: Pulmonary function — interstitial lung disease risk
   D: Hepatic function — hepatotoxicity is dose-limiting
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Trastuzumab (Herceptin) causes cardiac toxicity — reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and cardiac failure. Mechanism: HER2 signalling is important for cardiac function. Monitoring: baseline LVEF assessment (echocardiogram or MUGA) before starting, then every 3 months during treatment. Hold trastuzumab if: LVEF drops >10 percentage points from baseline AND falls below lower limit of normal (usually 50%). Most cases are reversible on stopping treatment. Avoid concurrent doxorubicin (both cardiotoxic — additive risk). This is an important NP monitoring responsibility in breast cancer clinics.

10. Q: A 55-year-old woman with ovarian cancer on carboplatin + paclitaxel develops numbness and tingling in her feet and hands after cycle 3. She is having difficulty buttoning her shirt. What chemotherapy toxicity is this and what is the management?
    A: Chemotherapy-induced nausea — increase antiemetic dose
    B: Peripheral neuropathy (grade 2–3) — dose reduction or substitution of paclitaxel (primary neurotoxic agent); gabapentin or duloxetine for symptom management; physiotherapy
    C: Carpel tunnel syndrome — splinting only
    D: Cerebrovascular accident — urgent CT brain
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is caused by paclitaxel, oxaliplatin, vincristine, and bortezomib. Paclitaxel causes sensorimotor neuropathy — numbness, tingling, and functional impairment (difficulty with fine motor tasks). CTCAE grading: Grade 1 (asymptomatic), Grade 2 (limiting instrumental ADLs — driving, buttoning), Grade 3 (limiting self-care ADLs). Grade 2–3 CIPN: consider paclitaxel dose reduction or switch to nab-paclitaxel. Symptom management: duloxetine has the best evidence for CIPN (Level 1 evidence), gabapentin/pregabalin. CIPN may be permanent — early dose modification may prevent permanent damage. Physiotherapy and occupational therapy for functional impact.

11. Q: What is the ABCDE approach used for in dermatological assessment of skin lesions in cancer screening?
    A: Antibiotic, Biopsy, Culture, Dermoscopy, Excision — steps in managing infected skin lesions
    B: Asymmetry, Border, Colour, Diameter, Evolution — criteria for identifying potentially malignant melanoma
    C: Assessment, Bleeding, Crusting, Discharge, Erythema — wound assessment
    D: Age, BMI, Cardiovascular risk, Diabetes, Exercise — metabolic cancer risk factors
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The ABCDE criteria for melanoma detection: A = Asymmetry (one half unlike the other), B = Border (irregular, ragged, notched), C = Colour variation (multiple colours — brown, black, red, white, blue), D = Diameter >6mm (though melanomas can be smaller), E = Evolution (any change in size, shape, colour, or new symptoms). Evolution is arguably the most important — any changing lesion warrants urgent assessment. Australia has the world's highest melanoma rate. NPs in primary care should perform opportunistic skin checks and use dermoscopy if trained. Urgent referral or excision biopsy for any suspicious pigmented lesion — do not use liquid nitrogen on undiagnosed pigmented lesions.

12. Q: A patient completing treatment for colorectal cancer asks about surveillance after completing curative surgery and chemotherapy. What is the recommended follow-up schedule?
    A: No follow-up required after curative treatment
    B: CEA every 3–6 months for 3 years then 6 monthly to 5 years; CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis annually for 3–5 years; colonoscopy at 1 year post-surgery then every 3–5 years
    C: Annual blood tests only — imaging is not required
    D: Colonoscopy every 6 months for life
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Colorectal cancer surveillance after curative resection aims to detect local recurrence and metachronous lesions early. Australian guidelines recommend: CEA every 3–6 months for 3 years, then 6-monthly to 5 years (rising CEA may precede clinical recurrence). CT chest/abdomen/pelvis annually for 3–5 years (liver metastases are the most common site of recurrence — potentially resectable if detected early). Colonoscopy at 1 year post-surgery (or 3–6 months if incomplete pre-operative assessment), then every 3–5 years if normal. Liver surgery for isolated liver metastases can be curative. NPs in cancer survivorship clinics play a key role in surveillance coordination.

13. Q: A patient with lymphoma is commenced on R-CHOP chemotherapy. Which pre-treatment blood test is mandatory to check before starting rituximab (R)?
    A: Thyroid function — rituximab causes hypothyroidism
    B: Hepatitis B surface antigen and core antibody — rituximab causes hepatitis B reactivation which can be fatal; antiviral prophylaxis required if positive
    C: HIV serology — rituximab contraindicated in HIV
    D: Serum protein electrophoresis — baseline myeloma marker
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Hepatitis B reactivation is a potentially fatal complication of rituximab and other anti-CD20 antibodies and immunosuppressive chemotherapy. All patients must have hepatitis B serology (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs) before starting rituximab. If HBsAg positive OR anti-HBc positive (past infection) — antiviral prophylaxis with entecavir or tenofovir is required throughout treatment and for 12 months after completion. Failure to screen and prophylax can result in fulminant hepatitis B reactivation and death. This is a mandatory pre-treatment check in all Australian oncology centres. Hepatitis C screening is also recommended but reactivation with rituximab is less well established.

14. Q: A 60-year-old male with CML (chronic myeloid leukaemia) is on imatinib (Gleevec) — a tyrosine kinase inhibitor. Which response is monitored to assess treatment efficacy?
    A: FBE normalisation only — white cell count returning to normal
    B: BCR-ABL transcript levels by PCR — molecular response monitoring (MMR = major molecular response, defined as BCR-ABL <0.1% on IS); deep molecular response allows treatment-free remission trials
    C: CT scan every 6 months for spleen size
    D: Bone marrow biopsy every 3 months
    Correct: B
    Explanation: CML monitoring uses quantitative PCR for BCR-ABL1 transcripts (the Philadelphia chromosome fusion gene product). The International Scale (IS) standardises results across laboratories. Response milestones: Complete haematological response (CHR) by 3 months (WCC and platelets normal), Early molecular response (EMR: BCR-ABL ≤10% by IS) by 3 months, Major Molecular Response (MMR: BCR-ABL <0.1%) by 12 months, Deep Molecular Response (MR4/MR4.5) allowing potential treatment-free remission (TFR) trials. Imatinib has transformed CML from a disease requiring bone marrow transplant to one managed with daily oral therapy — most patients have normal life expectancy. NPs in haematology clinics monitor molecular responses and toxicity (fatigue, nausea, oedema, QT prolongation).

15. Q: A patient with myeloma is being treated with bortezomib. What specific toxicity requires monitoring and management with this agent?
    A: Cardiac toxicity — echocardiogram every cycle
    B: Peripheral neuropathy — grade regularly; dose modification required for grade 2+; consider subcutaneous formulation which has lower neuropathy risk than IV
    C: Renal toxicity — dialysis may be required
    D: Pulmonary fibrosis — PFTs every cycle
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Bortezomib (a proteasome inhibitor) causes peripheral neuropathy — the dose-limiting toxicity. Subcutaneous administration has significantly lower rates of peripheral neuropathy than IV administration (38% vs 53% any grade) with equivalent efficacy — subcutaneous is now preferred. Monitoring: CTCAE grade at each visit — sensory and motor assessment. Management: Grade 1 (asymptomatic) — continue with monitoring; Grade 2 (moderate symptoms, limiting instrumental ADLs) — reduce dose or extend interval; Grade 3 (severe, limiting self-care) — hold until recovery to ≤Grade 1 then resume at reduced dose. Antiviral prophylaxis (aciclovir/valaciclovir) is mandatory with bortezomib — significantly increases herpes zoster reactivation risk.

16. Q: A 75-year-old woman with metastatic pancreatic cancer on gemcitabine presents with fatigue, pallor, and dyspnoea. Hb 74 g/L. What is the threshold for red blood cell transfusion in most oncology settings?
    A: Transfuse any cancer patient with Hb <100 g/L
    B: Hb <70–80 g/L in a symptomatic patient, or Hb <80 g/L in patients with cardiac comorbidities — consider patient symptoms, not just number
    C: Never transfuse — cancer patients should receive erythropoiesis-stimulating agents only
    D: Hb <60 g/L only — transfuse as little as possible
    Correct: B
    Explanation: RBC transfusion in oncology: threshold-based approach considering symptoms, not just Hb number. Most guidelines: transfuse when Hb <70–80 g/L with symptoms (dyspnoea, dizziness, fatigue affecting function) or Hb <80 g/L in patients with cardiac disease (coronary artery disease, heart failure). In palliative patients, transfuse if likely to improve quality of life. Avoid unnecessary transfusion — risks include febrile reactions, alloimmunisation, transfusion-related complications, and iron overload. Target: post-transfusion Hb 80–100 g/L (not normalisation). ESAs (epoetin, darbepoetin) reduce transfusion requirements in chemotherapy-induced anaemia — specialist initiated.

17. Q: Which of the following correctly describes tumour lysis syndrome (TLS) and when it is most likely to occur?
    A: A paraneoplastic syndrome causing hypocalcaemia in bone metastases
    B: Rapid destruction of cancer cells releasing intracellular contents — causes hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperuricaemia, and AKI — most common after chemotherapy for highly proliferative tumours (Burkitt lymphoma, ALL, high-grade NHL)
    C: A complication of radiation therapy causing bowel perforation
    D: Lysis of healthy cells by chemotherapy causing bone marrow failure
    Correct: B
    Explanation: TLS occurs when rapid tumour cell death releases intracellular contents into circulation: potassium (hyperkalaemia — cardiac arrhythmia risk), phosphate (hyperphosphataemia — causes secondary hypocalcaemia — tetany, seizures), uric acid (hyperuricaemia — AKI, gout), and nucleic acids. Most common in highly proliferative tumours with large bulk: Burkitt lymphoma, ALL, high-grade NHL — particularly after first chemotherapy cycle. Prevention (high-risk patients): aggressive IV hydration pre-chemotherapy, allopurinol (reduces uric acid production), rasburicase (recombinant urate oxidase) for very high risk. Monitoring: EUC, uric acid, phosphate, calcium, LFTs every 6–8 hours during high-risk period.

18. Q: A 50-year-old woman with breast cancer on tamoxifen reports hot flushes, vaginal dryness, and mood changes. She asks if she can stop tamoxifen due to side effects. What is the most appropriate response?
    A: Stop tamoxifen immediately — quality of life takes priority over efficacy
    B: Discuss the significant survival benefit of completing 5–10 years of tamoxifen; manage side effects (SSRIs/SNRIs for hot flushes, vaginal oestrogen for dryness — discuss with oncologist); refer to breast oncology if considering discontinuation
    C: Switch to combined HRT — relieves symptoms and is safe in breast cancer survivors
    D: Reduce tamoxifen dose to every other day — maintains some benefit
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Tamoxifen reduces breast cancer recurrence and mortality by approximately 30–40% in ER-positive breast cancer — completing 5–10 years of treatment significantly improves survival. Non-adherence and early discontinuation are associated with increased recurrence. Side effect management: hot flushes — venlafaxine, SSRIs (avoid paroxetine with tamoxifen — CYP2D6 inhibition reduces tamoxifen efficacy), gabapentin, lifestyle measures; vaginal dryness — topical vaginal oestrogen (generally considered safe in ER+ breast cancer — discuss with oncologist), lubricants; mood — psychological support. Combinied HRT is generally contraindicated in ER-positive breast cancer survivors. Any consideration of discontinuation should involve the treating oncologist.

19. Q: A 40-year-old male smoker presents to your primary care clinic. He wants to discuss prostate cancer screening. His father had prostate cancer diagnosed at age 60. What is the recommended approach?
    A: PSA testing is not recommended for any man — it causes more harm than good
    B: Discuss the benefits and harms of PSA testing; given family history, offer PSA testing from age 45 with informed consent; provide written information; respect patient autonomy
    C: Order PSA immediately without discussion — family history makes it mandatory
    D: Refer directly to urology without primary care PSA testing
    Correct: B
    Explanation: There is no Australian national prostate cancer screening program. PSA testing is a shared decision — discuss benefits (early detection of aggressive cancers, potentially curative treatment) and harms (false positives leading to biopsy, overdiagnosis and overtreatment of indolent cancers, anxiety). RACGP recommends informed discussion from age 50 for average-risk men, or from age 45 for those with first-degree relative with prostate cancer diagnosed <65 years or men of African ancestry. If PSA testing is agreed after informed consent — document the discussion. A single PSA test is insufficient — baseline and interval testing are more meaningful. Provide PCFA patient decision aid.

20. Q: What is the role of G-CSF (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, e.g. filgrastim) in oncology?
    A: It is an antiemetic used to prevent chemotherapy-induced nausea
    B: It stimulates bone marrow to produce neutrophils — used to reduce the duration and severity of neutropenia and febrile neutropenia risk after myelosuppressive chemotherapy
    C: It replaces red blood cells in chemotherapy-induced anaemia
    D: It is used to treat established febrile neutropenia as the primary antibiotic
    Correct: B
    Explanation: G-CSF (filgrastim, pegfilgrastim) stimulates the bone marrow to produce and release neutrophils, reducing the nadir depth and duration of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. Primary prophylaxis: given after chemotherapy cycles with >20% febrile neutropenia risk (high-risk regimens), or when dose-dense regimens are used. Secondary prophylaxis: after a previous episode of febrile neutropenia to allow continuation of planned chemotherapy. G-CSF does NOT treat established febrile neutropenia — antibiotics remain the primary treatment. Side effects: bone pain (treated with paracetamol — avoid NSAIDs if thrombocytopenic), rare splenomegaly and splenic rupture. NPs in oncology clinics commonly administer and manage G-CSF.
