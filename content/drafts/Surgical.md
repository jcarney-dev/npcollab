## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Acute abdomen with peritonism — guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness — call 000
- Bowel obstruction — absolute constipation, distension, vomiting, colicky pain — emergency transfer
- Perforated viscus — sudden severe abdominal pain, board-like abdomen, free air on imaging
- Post-operative haemorrhage — hypotension, tachycardia, wound haematoma expanding — call 000
- Anastomotic leak — fever, tachycardia, deteriorating post-operative patient day 3–7
- Wound dehiscence — evisceration — cover with moist dressing, emergency transfer
- Necrotising fasciitis — rapidly spreading erythema, crepitus, systemic toxicity — call 000
- Post-operative pulmonary embolism — sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia

### The NP Role in Surgical Care
NPs work across the surgical spectrum — in pre-operative assessment clinics, surgical wards, day surgery units, post-operative follow-up clinics, and community wound care. Key NP competencies in surgical care include:

- Pre-operative assessment and optimisation
- Patient education and consent support
- Post-operative monitoring and early complication recognition
- Wound assessment and management
- Pain management including multimodal analgesia
- DVT and VTE prophylaxis management
- Discharge planning and community follow-up
- Surgical referral — knowing when to refer and with what urgency

### Common Surgical Conditions

#### Acute Abdomen
- Appendicitis — most common surgical emergency, right iliac fossa pain
- Acute cholecystitis (covered in GI module)
- Bowel obstruction — adhesions (most common post-surgery), hernia, volvulus, malignancy
- Perforated peptic ulcer
- Mesenteric ischaemia — acute onset, pain out of proportion to examination
- Diverticulitis — left iliac fossa pain

#### Hernia
- Inguinal hernia — most common; indirect vs direct
- Femoral hernia — more common in women, higher strangulation risk
- Umbilical and paraumbilical hernia
- Incisional hernia — complication of previous surgery
- Strangulated hernia — emergency: non-reducible, tender, signs of bowel obstruction

#### Wound Complications
- Superficial surgical site infection (SSI)
- Deep SSI
- Wound dehiscence
- Haematoma
- Seroma
- Necrotising fasciitis

#### Breast Surgery Context
- Breast lump assessment and referral
- Post-mastectomy wound care
- Lymphoedema management post-axillary surgery

#### Anorectal
- Haemorrhoids
- Anal fissure
- Anorectal abscess — urgent surgical referral
- Pilonidal disease

### Key Conditions

#### Appendicitis
Most common abdominal surgical emergency. Classic presentation: periumbilical pain migrating to RIF, nausea, anorexia, low-grade fever, rebound tenderness at McBurney's point. Rovsing's sign positive. Alvarado Score helps risk stratify. WCC elevated, CRP elevated. CT abdomen most accurate investigation (>95% sensitivity). Ultrasound preferred in children and pregnant women (no radiation). Treatment: urgent surgical referral for appendicectomy (laparoscopic). Perforation risk increases with time — do not delay.

#### Inguinal Hernia
Bulge in groin, often reducible, may have dragging discomfort, worsens with Valsalva. Distinguish: indirect (through deep inguinal ring — more common, can descend into scrotum) vs direct (through posterior wall of inguinal canal). Examination: reducible vs irreducible vs obstructed vs strangulated. Refer to surgery: symptomatic hernias, irreducible, or any suspicion of obstruction/strangulation. Strangulated hernia (non-reducible + tender + systemic signs) = emergency transfer.

#### Acute Diverticulitis
Inflammation of diverticula — most common in sigmoid colon. Presents: left iliac fossa pain, fever, change in bowel habit, WCC and CRP elevated. Uncomplicated acute diverticulitis: can be managed in primary care with clear liquids, analgesia, and antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate or cephalexin + metronidazole). Complicated (abscess, perforation, peritonitis, fistula) — hospital admission and surgical review. CT abdomen confirms diagnosis and complication. Refer to colorectal surgery after first episode — colonoscopy in 6–8 weeks to exclude malignancy.

#### Pre-operative Assessment
NPs in pre-operative clinics assess fitness for surgery and anaesthesia. Key components:
- Complete medical history — comorbidities, medications, allergies
- Functional capacity — can patient climb stairs, walk on flat?
- Cardiac risk — RCRI (Revised Cardiac Risk Index): ischaemic heart disease, CCF, cerebrovascular disease, insulin-dependent diabetes, renal impairment, high-risk surgery
- Respiratory risk — COPD, OSA (STOP-BANG), smoking
- Medication management — anticoagulants (when to stop/bridge), antiplatelet agents, diabetic medications on day of surgery, antihypertensives
- Pre-operative investigations based on risk: ECG, CXR, FBE, EUC, coagulation
- Patient education — fasting instructions, post-operative expectations, pain management, DVT prophylaxis

#### VTE Prophylaxis
Hospital-acquired VTE is a significant preventable cause of morbidity and mortality. Risk assess all surgical patients: low risk (minor surgery, mobile) — early mobilisation; moderate-high risk — LMWH (enoxaparin 40mg SC daily), compression stockings, early mobilisation. Duration: major orthopaedic surgery — 28–35 days; abdominal/pelvic surgery for cancer — 28 days; other — 7–10 days or until mobile. Caprini Score used in many surgical units.

#### Post-operative Complications Recognition

**Early (0–24 hours):**
- Haemorrhage — hypotension, tachycardia, wound haematoma
- Anaphylaxis — latex, drug
- Cardiac — MI, arrhythmia (highest risk 30–60 minutes post-op)
- Respiratory — atelectasis, aspiration, bronchospasm

**Days 1–3:**
- Atelectasis — most common fever cause post-op day 1–2
- Urinary retention — particularly after spinal/epidural anaesthesia
- Ileus — abdominal distension, no bowel sounds, no flatus

**Days 3–7:**
- Wound infection — fever, erythema, discharge, pain
- Anastomotic leak — fever, tachycardia, abdominal pain day 3–7 after bowel surgery
- DVT — calf pain, swelling
- Pneumonia — productive cough, consolidation

**Late (>1 week):**
- Wound dehiscence
- Incisional hernia
- Adhesions
- PE

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Pre-operative Assessment

#### History
- Planned procedure and indication
- Previous surgical and anaesthetic history — previous adverse events, family history of anaesthetic complications (malignant hyperthermia)
- Cardiovascular: angina, MI, CCF, palpitations, exercise tolerance
- Respiratory: asthma, COPD, OSA, smoking, recent URTI
- Renal and hepatic function
- Diabetes — management plan for perioperative period
- Anticoagulation and antiplatelet medications — cessation timing
- Regular medications — what to take on day of surgery
- Allergies — drug, latex, food
- Social: home support, transport, living situation

#### Functional Assessment
- Metabolic equivalents (METs): <4 METs (unable to climb stairs or walk on flat) = poor functional capacity
- STOP-BANG for OSA: Snoring, Tired, Observed apnoea, Pressure (hypertension), BMI >35, Age >50, Neck circumference >40cm, Gender male — ≥3 = high risk
- Frailty assessment in elderly surgical patients — Clinical Frailty Scale

### Post-operative Assessment

#### Systematic Post-operative Review
Use AAABCD approach:
- Airway — maintained and protected?
- Anaesthesia — type, residual effects (drowsiness, nausea)
- Analgesia — pain score, breakthrough use, side effects
- Breathing — RR, SpO2, oxygen requirement
- Circulation — BP, HR, fluid balance, wound drain output
- Drain/Dressing — wound appearance, drain volume and character

#### Wound Assessment
- Wound type: primary closure, secondary intention, dehisced
- Surrounding skin: erythema (measure and mark), warmth, oedema
- Wound bed: tissue type (viable, slough, necrotic), depth
- Exudate: amount, colour, consistency
- Signs of infection: purulent discharge, increasing pain, fever, spreading erythema
- Wound edges: approximated, dehisced, undermining

#### Surgical Site Infection Classification (CDC)
- Superficial incisional SSI: involves skin and subcutaneous tissue only; within 30 days of surgery
- Deep incisional SSI: involves fascia and muscle; within 30 days (or 90 days if implant)
- Organ/space SSI: infection within the body cavity opened or manipulated

#### Investigations Post-operatively
- FBE — WCC for infection, anaemia
- CRP — rising CRP on day 3–5 suggests complication
- EUC — fluid balance, AKI (common post-op)
- Blood cultures — if fever >38.5°C with tachycardia
- Wound swab — if signs of infection
- CXR — respiratory complication
- Ultrasound or CT — collection, abscess, anastomotic leak
- D-dimer ± ultrasound — DVT
- CTPA — PE

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: Post-operative Day 4 — Abdominal Wound Assessment After Laparoscopic Appendicectomy

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 28-year-old female, post-operative day 4 following laparoscopic appendicectomy for perforated appendicitis, presenting to surgical outpatient clinic for wound review.

History: Procedure performed laparoscopically, converted to open drain insertion for pelvic collection. Three port sites plus a small Pfannenstiel incision for drain removal yesterday. Reports increasing pain at the right port site over the past 24 hours — initially thought it was normal post-op pain but now rates it 6/10 and notices the area looks "more red and swollen." Low-grade fever at home last night (37.8°C). Nausea but no vomiting. Tolerating light diet. Bowels opened today — first time since surgery. Passing flatus. Urine output adequate.

Medications: Cefalexin 500mg QID (5-day course), paracetamol 1g QID, ibuprofen 400mg TDS with food. Enoxaparin 40mg SC daily (day 4 of 7-day course).

Concerns: "The wound looks really red and I am worried about infection."

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: BP 118/74 | HR 94 | Temp 37.9°C | RR 16 | SpO2 99%

Wound Assessment — Right port site (12mm):
- Surrounding erythema: 3cm radius from wound edge — marked
- Warmth and oedema present
- Wound edges: partially approximated — small area of gaping at inferior edge (~1cm)
- Exudate: small amount of serous-seropurulent discharge at inferior edge
- Surrounding skin: no tracking, no crepitus, no bullae
- Tenderness: localised to wound site — no peritonism on abdominal palpation

Other wounds: Left port site (5mm) healing well. Pfannenstiel incision — edges well approximated, no erythema, no discharge. Drain site healing well.

Abdomen: Soft. Mild generalised tenderness consistent with post-operative state. No guarding. No rigidity. Bowel sounds present.

Investigations:
- Wound swab collected from discharge at inferior edge — sent for MCS
- WCC: 13.4 (mildly elevated)
- CRP: 94 (elevated, up from 68 on day 2)
- CXR: not performed (no respiratory symptoms)

**A — Assessment**
1. Superficial surgical site infection — right laparoscopic port site. CDC criteria met: purulent discharge, erythema, warmth, and oedema within 30 days of surgery.
2. Small wound dehiscence at inferior edge — not deep, subcutaneous layer intact.
3. Perforated appendicitis — increased SSI risk (contaminated surgical field).
4. Otherwise clinically stable — no signs of deep SSI, anastomotic leak, or systemic sepsis.

**P — Plan**
Wound Management:
- Wound swab sent for MCS — review result and adjust antibiotics if organism resistant
- Extend antibiotic course: continue cefalexin 500mg QID for further 5 days (total 9-day course) — appropriate empirical coverage for SSI post-abdominal surgery
- Wound opening: gently open wound at dehisced edge to allow drainage of any collection — irrigate with normal saline, pack lightly with non-adherent dressing
- Wound dressing: wound pack daily until exudate resolving, then moisture-balanced dressing
- Mark erythema borders for monitoring progression

Pain Management:
- Continue paracetamol 1g QID regular
- Ibuprofen 400mg TDS with food — continue until review
- Oxycodone 5mg PRN 4-hourly if above insufficient — prescribe 5-day supply

VTE Prophylaxis:
- Continue enoxaparin 40mg SC daily — 3 days remaining (perforated appendicitis = high VTE risk)

Review and Safety Netting:
- Review in 48 hours — wound reassessment, erythema progression check
- Return immediately if: erythema expands significantly beyond marked border, bullae develop, crepitus, fever >39°C, haemodynamic instability, increasing abdominal pain, clinical deterioration
- If MCS shows resistant organism — contact patient to change antibiotics

Patient Education: Explained superficial SSI findings, importance of wound packing, dressing schedule, ongoing antibiotic course. Patient provided with wound care instructions. Written safety netting advice given.

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 22-year-old male presents with 18 hours of periumbilical pain that has migrated to the right iliac fossa. He has nausea, anorexia, low-grade fever (37.8°C), and rebound tenderness at McBurney's point. His Alvarado score is 8. What is the most appropriate management?
   A: Analgesia, reassurance, and review in 24 hours
   B: Urgent surgical referral — high probability appendicitis; CT abdomen if diagnosis uncertain; appendicectomy
   C: IV antibiotics only — appendicitis can be treated non-operatively in all cases
   D: Ultrasound pelvis to exclude ovarian pathology first
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Alvarado score 8 (out of 10) indicates high probability appendicitis — urgent surgical referral is required. Features in this case: migration of pain to RIF (+1), anorexia (+1), nausea (+1), RIF tenderness (+2), rebound tenderness (+1), temperature >37.3°C (+1) = 8/10. CT abdomen is the most accurate investigation if the diagnosis is uncertain (>95% sensitivity). Analgesics should be given — they do not mask peritonism and withholding them is cruel and unnecessary. Perforation risk increases significantly after 24–36 hours — do not delay referral.

2. Q: A patient presents to the ED 6 hours post-operatively following an elective right hemicolectomy. They are hypotensive (BP 86/52), tachycardic (HR 126), and the surgical drain is draining 400mL of blood-stained fluid in 2 hours. What is the most likely diagnosis?
   A: Postoperative ileus — give IV metoclopramide
   B: Post-operative haemorrhage — call 000/surgical team immediately; IV access, fluid resuscitation, urgent return to theatre likely required
   C: Anastomotic leak — start IV antibiotics
   D: Pulmonary embolism — CTPA
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Hypotension, tachycardia, and significant haemosanguineous drain output in the early post-operative period is post-operative haemorrhage until proven otherwise. This is a surgical emergency — escalate to the surgical team immediately. Establish IV access, commence fluid resuscitation (crystalloid initially), crossmatch and request packed red cells, notify anaesthetics for potential return to theatre. Haemoglobin may be falsely normal immediately after acute haemorrhage — do not be falsely reassured. Anastomotic leak typically presents days 3–7, not hours post-operatively.

3. Q: Which of the following correctly describes the grading of inguinal hernias and the urgency of management?
   A: All inguinal hernias require emergency surgery within 24 hours
   B: Reducible hernia — elective surgical referral; irreducible (incarcerated) — urgent referral; strangulated (non-reducible + tender + systemic signs) — emergency transfer
   C: Watchful waiting is recommended for all inguinal hernias — surgery only if severe pain
   D: Inguinal hernias in women are always treated conservatively
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Hernia management is graded by reducibility and perfusion: Reducible (returns to abdomen spontaneously or with gentle pressure) — symptomatic hernias should have elective surgical referral; watchful waiting acceptable for asymptomatic hernias in fit patients. Irreducible/incarcerated (cannot be reduced, bowel trapped but not ischaemic) — urgent surgical referral within hours. Strangulated (irreducible + tender + erythematous overlying skin + systemic signs — tachycardia, fever, nausea) — emergency transfer, bowel ischaemia and necrosis will occur without prompt surgery.

4. Q: What is the most common cause of fever on post-operative day 1–2?
   A: Wound infection
   B: Atelectasis — small airway collapse causing pyrexia; management is early mobilisation and incentive spirometry
   C: DVT
   D: Anastomotic leak
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The "Five Ws" of post-operative fever by timing: Wind (days 1–2, atelectasis), Water (days 3–5, UTI), Wound (days 5–7, SSI), Walking (days 5+, DVT/PE), Wonder drugs (any time, drug fever). Atelectasis (alveolar collapse from shallow breathing under anaesthesia) is the most common cause of early post-operative fever. Treatment: analgesia to enable deep breathing, early mobilisation, incentive spirometry, chest physiotherapy. Wound infection typically presents days 5–7 (not early post-op). Anastomotic leak: days 3–7.

5. Q: A post-operative patient on day 3 following bowel resection develops fever (38.6°C), tachycardia (HR 108), abdominal pain increasing, and declining urine output. CRP has risen from 80 to 240 overnight. What is the most concerning diagnosis?
   A: Ileus — give IV metoclopramide and reassess
   B: Anastomotic leak — urgent surgical review; CT abdomen with IV contrast; ICU involvement likely required
   C: UTI — urine MCS and antibiotics
   D: Atelectasis — chest physiotherapy
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Anastomotic leak is a life-threatening surgical complication typically occurring day 3–7 after bowel resection. Warning signs: fever, tachycardia, rising inflammatory markers (CRP, WCC), increasing abdominal pain, and declining urine output. The clinical picture of a deteriorating post-operative patient after bowel surgery must be treated as anastomotic leak until proven otherwise. CT abdomen with IV contrast is the investigation of choice (look for free air, free fluid, contrast extravasation). Urgent surgical review — most leaks require re-operation or radiological drainage. Delay is dangerous — septic shock develops rapidly.

6. Q: Which of the following patients requires deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis after surgery?
   A: 25-year-old having day surgery for ingrown toenail removal — no prophylaxis needed
   B: 68-year-old having elective total hip replacement — enoxaparin 40mg SC daily for 28–35 days plus compression stockings
   C: 45-year-old having laparoscopic cholecystectomy — no prophylaxis as procedure is minimally invasive
   D: All surgical patients receive identical VTE prophylaxis regardless of risk
   Correct: B
   Explanation: VTE prophylaxis is risk-stratified. Major orthopaedic surgery (hip and knee arthroplasty) carries the highest VTE risk — extended prophylaxis (28–35 days) with LMWH (enoxaparin 40mg daily) plus compression stockings is standard. Abdominal/pelvic surgery for cancer: 28 days LMWH. General surgical (laparoscopic cholecystectomy): 7–10 days if additional risk factors, otherwise until mobile. Minor surgery in young low-risk patients: early mobilisation only. The Caprini RAM (Risk Assessment Model) stratifies VTE risk and guides prophylaxis duration in surgical patients.

7. Q: A wound on post-operative day 7 shows: 4cm radius of erythema, increasing pain, and a small area of skin discolouration with surrounding crepitus on palpation. The patient is febrile (39.4°C) and tachycardic (HR 116). What is the most urgent concern?
   A: Superficial SSI — oral cefalexin and wound dressing
   B: Necrotising fasciitis — emergency transfer; surgical debridement is life-saving; do not delay
   C: Haematoma — aspirate and repack
   D: Seroma — reassure and observe
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Crepitus (gas in the tissues), rapid spread of erythema, skin discolouration (grey/blue skin), and systemic toxicity (fever, tachycardia) with a wound are hallmarks of necrotising fasciitis (NF) — a life-threatening surgical emergency. NF is caused by polymicrobial organisms producing gas and spreading along fascial planes with devastating tissue necrosis. Mortality is 20–40% even with treatment. Emergency transfer — wide surgical debridement is life-saving. IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam + clindamycin). Hyperbaric oxygen as adjunct in some centres. Every hour of delay increases mortality.

8. Q: A 55-year-old woman is being assessed pre-operatively for elective total knee arthroplasty. She takes warfarin for atrial fibrillation (CHA₂DS₂-VASc score 4). What is the most appropriate anticoagulation management perioperatively?
   A: Continue warfarin throughout — anticoagulation cannot be stopped for AF
   B: Cease warfarin 5 days pre-operatively; check INR on day of surgery (target <1.5); bridge with LMWH if high thrombotic risk; resume warfarin post-operatively
   C: Switch to aspirin 7 days before surgery
   D: Cease warfarin the night before — 24 hours is sufficient
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Warfarin management perioperatively: cease 5 days before surgery (half-life ~40 hours, 5 half-lives for clearance). Check INR on day of surgery — target <1.5 for most orthopaedic procedures. Bridging with LMWH is recommended for high thrombotic risk patients (mechanical heart valves, recent VTE, AF with CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥5 or recent stroke). Resume warfarin 12–24 hours post-operatively when haemostasis is confirmed. The specific bridging protocol (dose and timing) should be determined in consultation with the operating surgeon and anticoagulation specialist. DOACs require shorter cessation periods — typically 24–48 hours depending on drug and renal function.

9. Q: On post-operative day 4, a patient who underwent laparotomy for colorectal cancer has a temperature of 38.9°C. She has not had a bowel motion or passed flatus. Her abdomen is distended and tympanic. Bowel sounds are absent. What is the most likely diagnosis?
   A: Anastomotic leak — emergency re-operation
   B: Post-operative ileus — nasogastric decompression, IV fluids, withhold oral intake, mobilise; distinguish from mechanical obstruction with imaging if necessary
   C: Surgical site infection — wound swab and antibiotics
   D: Pulmonary embolism — CTPA
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Post-operative ileus (POI) is common after abdominal surgery — characterised by absence of bowel sounds, no flatus, abdominal distension, and nausea/vomiting. Causes: surgery itself, opioid analgesia, electrolyte imbalance (hypokalaemia), intra-abdominal inflammation. Management: NGT for decompression if vomiting, IV fluids and electrolyte replacement, NPO, early mobilisation, minimise opioids (use multimodal analgesia), consider alvimopan if available. Distinguish from mechanical obstruction (adhesions, internal hernia) with CT if not resolving — obstruction may require surgical intervention. Most POI resolves within 3–5 days.

10. Q: Which pre-operative investigation is recommended for a 65-year-old male with known ischaemic heart disease scheduled for elective total hip arthroplasty?
    A: No pre-operative investigations needed for elective orthopaedic surgery
    B: 12-lead ECG, FBE, EUC, coagulation, group and save, HbA1c if diabetic — and cardiology review if cardiac status has changed since last assessment
    C: Coronary angiogram to clear for surgery
    D: Echocardiogram only
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Pre-operative investigations should be targeted based on risk. For a 65-year-old with IHD having major orthopaedic surgery: 12-lead ECG (baseline, detect new changes), FBE (anaemia assessment — common in elderly), EUC (renal function for drug dosing, electrolytes), coagulation (baseline before anticoagulation), group and crossmatch (blood loss anticipated), HbA1c if diabetic (poor control increases SSI and poor wound healing). Cardiology review is indicated if: unstable symptoms, new or changed symptoms, recent MI (within 60 days), or poorly controlled heart failure. Coronary angiogram is not routine — indicated only if specific cardiac concerns identified.

11. Q: A 35-year-old male presents with a painful, non-reducible lump in the left groin. He is nauseated, vomiting, and the lump is erythematous and tender. What is the most appropriate management?
    A: Reassure — inguinal hernias are common and rarely serious
    B: Emergency transfer — strangulated hernia with likely bowel ischaemia
    C: Manual reduction and elective surgical referral
    D: IV antibiotics for groin cellulitis
    Correct: B
    Explanation: A non-reducible, tender, erythematous groin lump with systemic symptoms (nausea, vomiting) is a strangulated hernia until proven otherwise — a surgical emergency. Strangulation = compromised blood supply to the hernia contents (usually bowel). Without emergency surgery, bowel infarction and perforation will occur. Call 000 or arrange immediate emergency transfer. Do NOT attempt repeated forceful manual reduction of a strangulated hernia — risk of reducing infarcted bowel into the abdomen. IV access and analgesia while awaiting transfer.

12. Q: What is the correct management of a superficial surgical site infection on post-operative day 6?
    A: IV antibiotics and hospital admission for all SSIs
    B: Open and drain the wound (release sutures/staples), wound swab for MCS, oral antibiotics (cefalexin) for surrounding cellulitis, regular wound packing and dressing
    C: Apply topical antibiotic cream only — do not disturb the wound
    D: Reassure and observe — all post-operative wound changes are normal
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Superficial SSI management: open the wound to release purulent collection — primary closure retains infection. Remove sutures or staples over the infected area, irrigate with normal saline, and allow to heal by secondary intention (wound packing). Wound swab for MCS to guide antibiotic therapy. Oral antibiotics for surrounding cellulitis: cefalexin or flucloxacillin for Staph/Strep coverage (most common SSI organisms). IV antibiotics for: extensive cellulitis, systemic sepsis, immunocompromised, or deep SSI. Daily or alternate day wound review — most superficial SSIs heal within 2–4 weeks with appropriate management.

13. Q: A patient on post-operative day 8 after total knee replacement presents with left calf pain and swelling. The right leg is normal. Wells DVT score is 3. What is the next step?
    A: D-dimer test — if negative, DVT excluded
    B: Compression duplex ultrasound of left lower limb directly — high pre-test probability, D-dimer not useful
    C: Reassure — post-operative leg swelling is normal
    D: Start anticoagulation empirically without investigation
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Wells DVT score ≥2 = high pre-test probability — proceed directly to compression duplex ultrasound. D-dimer is only useful to EXCLUDE DVT in LOW probability cases — in high probability post-operative patients, D-dimer will be elevated (surgery causes hypercoagulability) and is not useful diagnostically. Post-operative patients undergoing major orthopaedic surgery are at high VTE risk. If proximal DVT confirmed: therapeutic anticoagulation (DOAC or LMWH) — duration typically 3 months. If the patient is already on enoxaparin prophylaxis (day 8), a DVT indicates treatment failure and therapeutic dosing is required.

14. Q: A 40-year-old woman is 48 hours post-operative following laparoscopic cholecystectomy for acute cholecystitis. She is febrile (38.2°C), tachycardic, and her abdominal pain has increased compared to yesterday. She has bile-stained fluid draining from her abdominal drain. What is the most likely complication?
    A: Post-operative ileus — withhold oral intake
    B: Bile leak — urgent surgical review and CT abdomen; ERCP may be required
    C: Wound infection — oral antibiotics
    D: UTI — urine MCS
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Bile-coloured drain output with fever, tachycardia, and increasing abdominal pain after laparoscopic cholecystectomy is a bile leak until proven otherwise — a recognised complication occurring in approximately 0.5–1% of cases. Causes: clip failure on cystic duct, injury to bile duct, leakage from liver bed. CT abdomen with IV contrast confirms bile collection. ERCP with biliary stenting is the definitive treatment for most bile leaks (reduces biliary pressure allowing the leak to seal). Urgent surgical review required. Bile peritonitis is life-threatening if not treated promptly.

15. Q: Which statement correctly describes wound healing by secondary intention?
    A: The wound edges are sutured closed — fastest healing
    B: The wound is left open and heals from the wound base upward through granulation tissue formation, contraction, and epithelialisation — used for infected or contaminated wounds
    C: A skin graft is applied immediately — primary closure is contraindicated
    D: Wound healing by secondary intention always requires antibiotics
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Three types of wound healing: Primary intention — wound edges sutured closed (surgical incisions, clean lacerations). Secondary intention — wound left open, heals from base upward through granulation, contraction, and epithelialisation (infected wounds, dehisced surgical wounds, pressure ulcers). Tertiary intention (delayed primary closure) — wound left open initially (for contamination control), sutured closed days later. Secondary intention healing is indicated for infected, contaminated, or poorly vascularised wounds where primary closure would trap infection. Takes longer than primary closure and produces a wider scar. Regular wound assessment and dressing changes are essential.

16. Q: What are the "sick day rules" for metformin in a surgical patient?
    A: Continue metformin on the day of surgery — blood glucose control is important perioperatively
    B: Withhold metformin on the day of surgery and hold for 48 hours post-operatively — resume when eating, drinking, and renal function is confirmed normal (AKI risk with contrast and anaesthesia)
    C: Halve the metformin dose on the day of surgery
    D: Switch to insulin on the day of surgery — metformin is always unsafe perioperatively
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Metformin should be withheld on the day of surgery and for 48 hours post-operatively. Reasons: surgery causes acute physiological stress with risk of AKI (contrast, hypotension, dehydration), and metformin accumulates in renal impairment increasing risk of lactic acidosis. Resume metformin when: the patient is eating and drinking normally, renal function is confirmed normal (EUC), and no contrast media was used (or 48 hours post-contrast). Insulin management perioperatively: continue long-acting insulin (often at reduced dose), withhold short-acting insulin if fasting, monitor blood glucose 1–2 hourly, target glucose 6–10 mmol/L perioperatively.

17. Q: A patient on post-operative day 2 following appendicectomy has a temperature of 37.9°C. Wound looks clean. Urine dipstick is negative. Chest is clear. What is the most likely cause of the fever and management?
    A: Wound infection — start antibiotics immediately
    B: Atelectasis — encourage deep breathing, early mobilisation, incentive spirometry, adequate analgesia
    C) DVT — start anticoagulation
    D: Drug fever — cease all medications
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Low-grade fever on post-operative day 1–2 with no obvious source is most commonly atelectasis — the most common cause of early post-operative fever. Post-operative pain limits deep breathing, leading to small airway and alveolar collapse. Management: adequate analgesia (to enable deep breathing), early mobilisation, incentive spirometry (10 deep breaths hourly while awake), chest physiotherapy. Wound infection typically presents day 5–7. DVT day 5+. Routine antibiotics are not indicated for post-operative atelectasis. If fever persists >72 hours or worsens — investigate for other sources.

18. Q: A 50-year-old male has a loop colostomy formed during emergency surgery for sigmoid volvulus. On post-operative day 3, the stoma output is 2,800mL over 24 hours of watery green effluent. He is tachycardic and his electrolytes show: Na 131, K 3.0, Cl 94. What is the complication and management?
    A: Normal stoma function — no intervention needed
    B: High output stoma — IV fluid and electrolyte replacement, anti-motility agents (loperamide), dietary modification, involve stomal therapy nurse
    C: Anastomotic leak — emergency surgery
    D: Stoma prolapse — manual reduction
    Correct: B
    Explanation: High output stoma (>2,000mL/24h) is a serious complication causing dehydration and electrolyte imbalance (hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia — classic pattern). Management: IV fluid replacement (normal saline with potassium), anti-motility agents (loperamide 2–4mg QID, may need very high doses), dietary modification (reduce hyperosmolar foods and drinks, small frequent meals, oral rehydration), magnesium supplementation, stomal therapy nurse involvement, nutritional support. Persistent high output — consider octreotide, codeine phosphate. Identify precipitating factors: infection, partial obstruction, short bowel. Oral intake restriction during acute phase.

19. Q: What is the Alvarado Score used for in surgical assessment?
    A: Grading the severity of pancreatitis
    B: Risk stratifying the probability of appendicitis to guide CT ordering and surgical referral
    C: Assessing post-operative ileus severity
    D: Calculating VTE risk in surgical patients
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The Alvarado Score (MANTRELS) stratifies the probability of appendicitis: Migration of pain to RIF (+1), Anorexia (+1), Nausea/Vomiting (+1), Tenderness in RIF (+2), Rebound tenderness (+1), Elevated temperature >37.3°C (+1), Leucocytosis (+2), Shift to left neutrophilia (+1). Maximum 10 points. Score 1–4: low probability — observation or discharge; 5–6: equivocal — CT imaging; 7–10: high probability — surgical referral. CT abdomen is more accurate but the Alvarado Score guides clinical decision-making, particularly in resource-limited settings or to triage CT urgency.

20. Q: A patient with an infected diabetic foot ulcer has surrounding erythema >4cm, deep tissue involvement to the tendon, and bone is visible at the wound base. X-ray shows bony destruction consistent with osteomyelitis. What is the appropriate management?
    A: Topical antiseptic dressing only — adequate for all diabetic foot ulcers
    B: Emergency hospital admission — IV antibiotics, urgent orthopaedic/vascular and podiatry review, MRI foot for osteomyelitis extent, likely surgical debridement or amputation planning
    C: Oral antibiotics and podiatry outpatient referral in 2 weeks
    D: Hyperbaric oxygen as primary treatment
    Correct: B
    Explanation: This presentation describes a severe diabetic foot infection (IDSA classification: Grade 3–4) with osteomyelitis — requires hospital admission. Osteomyelitis is confirmed or suspected: bone visible, bony destruction on X-ray, probe to bone test positive. Management: IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam or vancomycin + metronidazole for MRSA/anaerobic coverage based on wound swab), urgent orthopaedic or vascular surgery review, MDT approach (endocrinology, podiatry, vascular surgery), MRI foot for osteomyelitis extent (most sensitive imaging), glycaemic optimisation. Osteomyelitis often requires prolonged antibiotics (6 weeks) or surgical debridement — delayed treatment leads to amputation.
