## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Distal biceps tendon rupture — sudden pop with flexion, Popeye deformity, surgical referral urgent
- Elbow dislocation — deformity, neurovascular assessment essential — emergency transfer
- Open fracture — bone exposed through skin — call 000
- Compartment syndrome — tense forearm, severe pain on passive stretch — call 000
- Septic arthritis — hot swollen elbow with fever — emergency joint aspiration and IV antibiotics
- Medial epicondyle avulsion fracture with ulnar nerve injury in a child — urgent orthopaedic referral

### Key Conditions

#### Lateral Epicondylalgia (Tennis Elbow)
Most common elbow condition — affects 1–3% of adults. Now understood as tendinopathy (degenerative, not inflammatory) of the common extensor tendon origin at the lateral epicondyle. Presents: lateral elbow pain, worse with gripping activities, handshaking, lifting with the palm down. Tender at the lateral epicondyle. Positive Cozen's test and Mill's test. Management: load management (avoid provocative activities temporarily), progressive tendon loading (eccentric and heavy slow resistance exercises), physiotherapy, NSAIDs short-term. Corticosteroid injection — short-term benefit but worse long-term outcomes than physiotherapy (Coombes et al 2013). Most cases resolve in 12–18 months. Surgery rarely required.

#### Medial Epicondylalgia (Golfer's Elbow)
Tendinopathy of the common flexor-pronator tendon origin at the medial epicondyle. Medial elbow pain, worse with gripping and wrist flexion activities. Tender at the medial epicondyle. Important: always assess ulnar nerve — cubital tunnel syndrome frequently coexists (medial elbow, ring and little finger tingling). Management: similar to lateral epicondylalgia — progressive loading, physiotherapy, activity modification.

#### Cubital Tunnel Syndrome
Ulnar nerve compression at the medial elbow (cubital tunnel). Most common cause of ulnar neuropathy. Presents: medial elbow pain, ring and little finger numbness and tingling (ulnar 1.5 fingers), intrinsic hand muscle weakness (interossei, hypothenar). Tinel's sign positive over the cubital tunnel. Elbow flexion test positive (symptoms reproduced with maximal elbow flexion for 1 minute). Conservative: avoid prolonged elbow flexion (elbow pad at night), activity modification. Surgery (ulnar nerve transposition or decompression) for severe or progressive cases.

#### Olecranon Bursitis
Swelling over the posterior olecranon — bursa inflammation. Common causes: trauma (direct fall), repetitive pressure (leaning on elbow), septic (infected). Septic vs aseptic: septic bursitis shows erythema, warmth, tenderness, fever — aspire for culture and treat with flucloxacillin. Aseptic: compression, ice, NSAIDs, avoid pressure.

#### Radial Head Fracture
Most common adult elbow fracture — typically after a fall on outstretched hand (FOOSH). Lateral elbow pain, tenderness over radial head, painful and restricted forearm rotation. X-ray: may show fat pad sign (posterior fat pad visible = haemarthrosis = fracture even if not visible). Mason classification: Type I (undisplaced — sling and early mobilisation), Type II (partial articular displacement — surgery if >2mm or >30% articular involvement), Type III (comminuted — surgery). Aspiration of haemarthrosis and instillation of local anaesthetic can allow better examination and rotation assessment.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Elbow History
- Dominant or non-dominant arm
- Occupation and sport — repetitive gripping activities, overhead sport, racquet sports
- Onset — acute (trauma) vs insidious (overuse)
- Location — lateral (tennis elbow, LCL, radial head), medial (golfer's elbow, UCL, cubital tunnel), posterior (olecranon bursitis, triceps tendon), anterior (biceps tendon, brachialis)
- Triggering activities — lifting with palm down (lateral), gripping with wrist flexion (medial)
- Neurological symptoms — numbness, tingling (ulnar distribution = ring/little finger, median = thumb/index/middle)
- Locking or catching — loose bodies, osteochondritis dissecans
- Previous treatment

### Elbow Examination

#### Inspection
- Swelling — posterior olecranon (bursitis), lateral (radial head, effusion), diffuse (septic arthritis)
- Carrying angle — normal 5–15° valgus; increased = cubitus valgus (tardy ulnar nerve palsy)
- Muscle wasting — intrinsic hand muscles (ulnar nerve)

#### Range of Motion
- Flexion (normal 140–150°), extension (0°, loss of full extension = effusion or fracture)
- Pronation and supination (normal 80° each)
- Restricted supination ± pain over radial head = radial head fracture

#### Palpation
- Lateral epicondyle — tender at the common extensor origin (1cm distal and anterior to the lateral epicondyle)
- Medial epicondyle — common flexor-pronator origin
- Radial head — rotational crepitus with pronation/supination
- Olecranon — bursitis, tenderness
- Cubital tunnel — ulnar nerve Tinel's sign

#### Special Tests
- **Cozen's Test**: resisted wrist extension with elbow extended — positive = lateral epicondyle pain (lateral epicondylalgia)
- **Mill's Test**: passive wrist flexion with elbow extended — stretches the extensor origin — positive = lateral epicondyle pain
- **Medial Epicondyle Test**: resisted wrist flexion and pronation — positive = medial epicondyle pain
- **Elbow Flexion Test**: maximal elbow flexion for 1 minute — positive = ulnar distribution tingling (cubital tunnel syndrome)
- **Tinel's Sign Cubital Tunnel**: tap over the cubital tunnel behind the medial epicondyle — positive = ring and little finger tingling
- **Ligament Assessment**: valgus stress (UCL — medial elbow pain), varus stress (LCL)
- **Hook Test**: distal biceps — patient supines arm at 90°, examiner hooks a finger under the distal biceps tendon — absent cord = distal biceps rupture

#### Investigations
- X-ray elbow (AP and lateral): fracture, OA, loose bodies, fat pad sign (posterior fat pad = haemarthrosis)
- MRI: soft tissue — tendinopathy, UCL tear, loose bodies, OCD, nerve compression
- Ultrasound: tendinopathy, bursitis, partial tears — good first-line soft tissue investigation
- EMG/nerve conduction studies: cubital tunnel (ulnar nerve conduction velocity across elbow), median nerve, radial nerve

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 42-year-old with Lateral Epicondylalgia (Tennis Elbow)

**S — Subjective**
42-year-old female office administrator (right dominant). 4 months of right lateral elbow pain. Onset insidious — worse over the past 6 weeks. Pain over the lateral elbow, radiating into the forearm. Worse with gripping — lifting the kettle, opening jars, shaking hands, typing with the mouse. VAS 6/10 at worst. No numbness or tingling. No locking. Had one corticosteroid injection by GP 6 weeks ago — initial relief for 2 weeks then returned to baseline.

**O — Objective**
Tender at the lateral epicondyle and common extensor origin (1cm distal and anterior). No significant effusion. Wrist extension 5/5, grip strength mildly reduced right (dynamometer 26kg right vs 34kg left).

Cozen's Test: positive right — reproduces lateral elbow pain.
Mill's Test: positive right.
Elbow Flexion Test: negative bilaterally.
Tinel's Cubital Tunnel: negative.
Elbow ROM: full flexion and extension, full pronation and supination.

**A — Assessment**
Lateral epicondylalgia (tennis elbow) — right, 4 months duration. Corticosteroid injection provided only short-term benefit — consistent with evidence showing worse long-term outcomes with injection vs exercise.

**P — Plan**
- Physiotherapy referral — progressive tendon loading program (heavy slow resistance exercises for the wrist extensors): wrist extension with a weight, 3 sets of 15 repetitions, slow eccentric component, 3x/week. This is the most evidence-based intervention.
- Activity modification: avoid provocative activities during the acute loading program (first 4–6 weeks), then gradual reintroduction
- Ergonomic assessment: workstation, mouse positioning, keyboard height — common contributors in office workers
- NSAIDs: ibuprofen 400mg TDS with food for 2 weeks — for pain management during rehabilitation
- Avoid further corticosteroid injection — short-term relief only, worse long-term outcome vs physiotherapy
- Education: explained tendinopathy model, expected recovery timeline (12–18 months for full resolution), importance of adherence to loading program
- Review in 6 weeks — assess progress, adjust loading program

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 38-year-old builder has 3 months of right lateral elbow pain worse with gripping and lifting. Cozen's test is positive. He had a corticosteroid injection 8 weeks ago with only 3 weeks of relief. What is the most evidence-based next management step?
   A: Repeat corticosteroid injection immediately
   B: Progressive tendon loading program (heavy slow resistance exercises for wrist extensors) through physiotherapy — superior to corticosteroid injection for long-term outcomes
   C: Surgical release of the common extensor origin
   D: Complete rest for 6 weeks — activity is causing the problem
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The landmark Coombes et al (2013) trial showed that corticosteroid injection for lateral epicondylalgia provides better short-term pain relief but significantly worse long-term outcomes (12 months) compared to physiotherapy with progressive loading. Corticosteroid injection inhibits the natural healing response in tendinopathy. Progressive tendon loading (eccentric and heavy slow resistance exercises) is the most evidence-based treatment. Complete rest is counterproductive — tendons need appropriate mechanical loading to remodel. Lateral release surgery is reserved for truly refractory cases after >12 months of appropriate conservative management.

2. Q: What nerve is compressed in cubital tunnel syndrome and what are the characteristic symptoms?
   A: Median nerve — thumb, index, and middle finger numbness
   B: Ulnar nerve at the medial elbow — ring and little finger numbness and tingling, medial elbow pain, intrinsic hand muscle weakness (interossei, hypothenar)
   C: Radial nerve — dorsal hand numbness, wrist drop
   D: Anterior interosseous nerve — loss of pinch strength, no sensory symptoms
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Cubital tunnel syndrome — ulnar nerve compression at the medial elbow (most common site of ulnar neuropathy). Symptoms: medial elbow pain (especially with elbow flexion), sensory — ring finger (ulnar half) and little finger numbness and tingling, motor — weakness of intrinsic hand muscles (interossei, hypothenar, adductor pollicis — positive Froment's sign). Severe: clawing of ring and little finger (intrinsic wasting). Tests: Tinel's over cubital tunnel, elbow flexion test (90 seconds maximal elbow flexion reproduces symptoms). EMG/NCS confirms diagnosis (reduced ulnar nerve conduction velocity across the elbow). Compare with carpal tunnel syndrome (median nerve — thumb, index, middle finger, thenar wasting).

3. Q: Cozen's test is positive in a patient with lateral elbow pain. What does this test involve and what does a positive result indicate?
   A: Maximal elbow flexion for 1 minute — cubital tunnel syndrome
   B: Resisted wrist extension with the elbow extended — positive = lateral epicondyle pain reproduced, indicating lateral epicondylalgia (tennis elbow)
   C: Passive wrist flexion with elbow extended — stretches the flexor-pronator origin
   D: Hook test — assesses distal biceps tendon integrity
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Cozen's test: patient makes a fist, extends the wrist, the examiner resists wrist extension — this loads the common extensor tendon origin at the lateral epicondyle. Positive result: pain reproduced at the lateral epicondyle. High sensitivity for lateral epicondylalgia. Mill's test is complementary: examiner passively flexes the patient's wrist with the elbow extended — stretches the extensor origin. Both positive = strong support for lateral epicondylalgia. Note: radial tunnel syndrome can mimic lateral epicondylalgia — distinguish by tenderness location (radial tunnel is more distal, 3–4cm distal to the lateral epicondyle at the radial tunnel, not at the epicondyle itself).

4. Q: A 65-year-old male falls directly onto his elbow and develops posterior elbow swelling. He has full range of motion and no fever. The swelling is fluctuant and non-tender. X-ray shows no fracture. Diagnosis and management?
   A: Olecranon fracture — surgical fixation
   B: Olecranon bursitis (aseptic) — compression bandage, ice, NSAIDs, avoid direct pressure; aspiration only if very large or diagnostic uncertainty
   C: Septic bursitis — aspiration and antibiotics immediately
   D: Elbow effusion — joint aspiration
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Olecranon bursitis — fluctuant swelling over the posterior olecranon after direct trauma. Full ROM and no fever distinguish aseptic bursitis from septic and from joint effusion. Aseptic (traumatic) management: compression, ice, NSAIDs, avoid direct elbow pressure (elbow pads). Aspiration for diagnostic purposes if uncertain or if very large — send fluid for WCC, Gram stain, culture, crystals. Septic bursitis (erythema, warmth, tenderness, fever): aspirate and treat with flucloxacillin pending culture. Aspiration alone without antibiotics for aseptic bursitis — recurrence common, may introduce infection.

5. Q: A 25-year-old male rugby player felt a sudden "pop" in his arm while carrying a tackle bag and now has a visible defect in his distal upper arm with a "Popeye" deformity. What is the diagnosis and management?
   A: Biceps muscle contusion — ice and rest
   B: Distal biceps tendon rupture — urgent orthopaedic referral; surgical repair within 2–3 weeks provides best functional outcomes (restores supination strength)
   C: Proximal biceps tendon rupture — conservative management acceptable
   D: Brachialis muscle tear — physiotherapy
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Distal biceps tendon rupture: sudden pop with a flexion/supination mechanism, visible defect (Popeye sign — muscle belly retracts proximally), hook test negative (cannot hook a finger under the distal biceps tendon in the antecubital fossa). Distal ruptures cause significant loss of supination strength (30–40%) and elbow flexion strength — unlike proximal ruptures which are less functionally significant. Management: urgent surgical repair (within 2–3 weeks before tendon retracts and adhesions form) provides the best functional outcomes — restores supination strength. Conservative management is only for elderly or sedentary patients. MRI confirms the diagnosis if clinical assessment is uncertain.

6. Q: A 50-year-old female has medial elbow pain worse with gripping and forearm flexion activities. She is a keen golfer. Ring and little finger tingling is also present. What additional assessment is essential?
   A: X-ray to exclude OA
   B: Ulnar nerve assessment — cubital tunnel syndrome frequently coexists with medial epicondylalgia; Tinel's test over the cubital tunnel, elbow flexion test, and EMG/NCS if neurological symptoms present
   C: MRI immediately — urgent
   D: Corticosteroid injection — first-line for medial epicondylalgia
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Medial epicondylalgia and cubital tunnel syndrome frequently coexist — the ulnar nerve passes close to the medial epicondyle and can be irritated by the same activities. Ring and little finger tingling in a patient with medial elbow pain strongly suggests concurrent cubital tunnel syndrome. Assessment: Tinel's test over the cubital tunnel (tap posterior to the medial epicondyle — tingling into the ulnar fingers = positive), elbow flexion test (maximal elbow flexion for 90 seconds — ulnar distribution tingling = positive). EMG/NCS if neurological symptoms present — confirms ulnar neuropathy at the elbow. Management of both conditions may be required: progressive loading for the tendinopathy, elbow pad and night splinting for cubital tunnel.

7. Q: What does a posterior fat pad sign on a lateral elbow X-ray indicate in a patient with elbow pain after a fall?
   A: Normal finding — posterior fat pad is always visible
   B: Haemarthrosis — blood in the elbow joint displaces the fat pad posteriorly, making it visible; in the context of trauma this indicates an occult fracture (most commonly radial head) even if no fracture line is visible on X-ray
   C: Olecranon bursitis
   D: OA changes — routine finding in older patients
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The posterior fat pad is normally hidden in the olecranon fossa and is NOT visible on a normal lateral elbow X-ray. When a joint effusion or haemarthrosis is present, the fat pad is displaced posteriorly and becomes visible (fat pad sign). In the context of trauma, a positive posterior fat pad sign indicates a haemarthrosis and therefore an occult fracture — most commonly a radial head fracture not visible on plain X-ray (Mason Type I). Management: treat as radial head fracture — sling, analgesia, early mobilisation. CT or MRI if displacement or complex fracture suspected. The anterior fat pad (anterior to the humerus) is normally partially visible — a sail sign (large anterior fat pad) also indicates an effusion.

8. Q: A 12-year-old boy falls on an outstretched hand and has medial elbow swelling and pain. X-ray shows an avulsed medial epicondyle fragment. Why is this injury potentially serious in a child?
   A: AVN of the medial epicondyle — aggressive surgery needed
   B: The avulsed fragment can become trapped in the joint (incarcerated), and the ulnar nerve (which runs adjacent to the medial epicondyle) is at risk of injury — orthopaedic referral required
   C: Growth plate injury always requires immediate surgery
   D: This is a normal variant — medial epicondyle apophysis
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Medial epicondyle avulsion fracture in children: the medial epicondyle is an apophysis (growth centre) — avulsion can occur during valgus stress or dislocation. Two key concerns: 1) Incarcerated fragment — the avulsed piece can become trapped within the elbow joint, requiring surgical extraction; 2) Ulnar nerve injury — the ulnar nerve runs posterior to the medial epicondyle and is vulnerable. Always assess ulnar nerve function (ring/little finger sensation, intrinsic muscle strength). Urgent orthopaedic referral — surgical fixation for displaced fragments, incarcerated fragments, or ulnar nerve injury.

9. Q: Radial tunnel syndrome can mimic lateral epicondylalgia. What clinical feature best distinguishes radial tunnel syndrome from lateral epicondylalgia (tennis elbow)?
   A: They cannot be distinguished clinically — MRI always required
   B: Tenderness location — lateral epicondylalgia: tenderness at the lateral epicondyle and common extensor origin; radial tunnel syndrome: tenderness 3–4cm distal to the lateral epicondyle at the radial tunnel (mobile wad of muscles)
   C: Both have identical tenderness locations — only EMG distinguishes them
   D: Radial tunnel syndrome causes wrist drop; lateral epicondylalgia does not
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Distinguishing features: Lateral epicondylalgia — tenderness at or immediately distal/anterior to the lateral epicondyle (common extensor origin), Cozen's test positive (resisted wrist extension). Radial tunnel syndrome — tenderness 3–4 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle at the point where the radial nerve enters the radial tunnel (between the extensor carpi radialis brevis and supinator), pain with resisted middle finger extension (compresses the radial nerve against the ECRB), no true weakness (pure compression, not full posterior interosseous nerve palsy). Radial tunnel syndrome is a diagnosis of exclusion — often diagnosed when "tennis elbow" does not respond to treatment. EMG is usually normal in radial tunnel syndrome (it is a pain syndrome, not a true neuropathy).

10. Q: A 35-year-old cricket bowler has medial elbow pain that has persisted for 6 months. MRI shows a partial UCL (ulnar collateral ligament) tear. What is the UCL and why is it important in overhead athletes?
    A: The UCL is the lateral elbow ligament — prevents varus stress
    B: The UCL (medial collateral ligament of the elbow) is the primary stabiliser against valgus stress — it is critical in overhead throwing athletes; partial tears may respond to physiotherapy; complete tears in high-level athletes — UCL reconstruction (Tommy John surgery)
    C: The UCL stabilises the radiocapitellar joint
    D: UCL tears always require immediate surgery in all athletes
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The UCL (ulnar collateral ligament — also called MCL of the elbow) is the primary medial stabiliser against valgus stress — critical in throwing athletes who experience repetitive high valgus loads during the acceleration phase of throwing. UCL injuries: partial tears — physiotherapy, activity modification, platelet-rich plasma injection (emerging evidence); complete tears in competitive overhead athletes — UCL reconstruction (Tommy John surgery) using a tendon graft. Return to throwing: 12–18 months post-surgery. The diagnosis is often suspected clinically (medial pain with throwing, valgus stress test positive) and confirmed with MRI or MRI arthrogram.

11. Q: What is the evidence regarding PRP (platelet-rich plasma) injections for lateral epicondylalgia?
    A: PRP has strong evidence and is first-line treatment — superior to physiotherapy
    B: PRP has emerging evidence suggesting benefit over corticosteroid injection at 6–12 months, but is not yet established as superior to physiotherapy with progressive loading — it may be considered for refractory cases; not currently recommended as routine first-line treatment
    C: PRP is contraindicated for tendinopathy
    D: PRP requires daily injection for 4 weeks
    Correct: B
    Explanation: PRP for lateral epicondylalgia: Multiple studies show PRP superior to corticosteroid injection at 6–12 months (no early deterioration seen with corticosteroid). However, PRP vs physiotherapy with progressive loading — evidence is less clear. Current recommendation: physiotherapy with progressive tendon loading is still first-line. PRP may be considered for: refractory lateral epicondylalgia after adequate physiotherapy trial (>3 months), as an alternative to repeated corticosteroid injections. Cost, availability, and preparation standardisation are limitations. Not currently funded by Medicare in Australia. Emerging evidence continues — watch for updated guidelines.

12. Q: An 8-year-old girl falls on an outstretched arm and has lateral elbow pain and swelling. She cannot fully extend the elbow. X-ray shows a lateral condyle fracture. Why is this fracture significant in children?
    A: It is a minor injury — treat with a sling
    B: Lateral condyle fractures in children are intra-articular and involve the growth plate — they carry risk of non-union, AVN, cubitus valgus deformity, and tardy ulnar nerve palsy if not accurately reduced and fixed; orthopaedic referral required
    C: They always heal without surgery
    D: This is a Colles fracture — treat with reduction and cast
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Lateral condyle fractures in children: intra-articular, involve the lateral condyle growth plate (Salter-Harris type IV equivalent). The blood supply to the lateral condyle enters distally — disruption risks AVN. If displaced or the fracture gap widens — non-union is common (the fracture is under distraction from the extensors). Complications of missed/inadequately treated lateral condyle fractures: non-union, lateral condyle overgrowth, cubitus valgus deformity, and tardy ulnar nerve palsy (years later as the nerve is stretched over the valgus deformity). Urgent orthopaedic referral — many require ORIF (open reduction internal fixation). Do not manage as a simple bruised elbow in children — X-ray all elbow injuries and apply the CRITOE mnemonic for paediatric elbow ossification centres.

13. Q: A patient is referred for nerve conduction studies for suspected cubital tunnel syndrome. What findings confirm the diagnosis?
    A: Reduced median nerve conduction velocity across the wrist (carpal tunnel)
    B: Reduced ulnar nerve conduction velocity across the elbow (<50 m/s or >10 m/s slowing compared to the forearm segment), and/or reduced amplitude of ulnar nerve sensory and motor potentials
    C: Normal EMG excludes cubital tunnel syndrome
    D: Positive Phalen's test
    Correct: B
    Explanation: EMG/NCS findings in cubital tunnel syndrome: Nerve conduction studies: reduced ulnar nerve motor conduction velocity across the elbow (normal >50 m/s; slowing of >10 m/s compared to the distal forearm segment is significant), reduced compound motor action potential (CMAP) amplitude at abductor digiti minimi, reduced ulnar sensory nerve action potential (SNAP). EMG (needle): denervation changes (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves) and reinnervation in ulnar-innervated intrinsic hand muscles (interossei, hypothenar). Note: EMG/NCS can be normal in mild cubital tunnel syndrome — clinical diagnosis may be sufficient in typical presentations. False negatives occur because the nerve is compressed intermittently.

14. Q: A 55-year-old male with gout has an acute hot, swollen, extremely painful right elbow. WCC is 18.2, CRP 156, uric acid 0.62. The elbow is held in flexion and he refuses to move it. What is the priority management?
    A: Oral colchicine — acute gout treatment
    B: Emergency joint aspiration under sterile conditions — must exclude septic arthritis before treating as gout; send aspirate for WCC, Gram stain, culture, and crystals; treat septic arthritis empirically if aspirate turbid pending results
    C: Allopurinol immediately — start urate-lowering therapy during the acute attack
    D: NSAIDs only — adequate for acute gout
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The most important principle in acute monoarthritis: exclude septic arthritis before treating as gout or pseudogout. Elbow held in flexion, refusal to move, severe pain, elevated WCC and CRP — this could be septic arthritis or acute gout. Emergency joint aspiration: turbid fluid with WCC >50,000 = septic arthritis until proven; negatively birefringent crystals = gout. IV antibiotics (flucloxacillin) after aspiration if septic arthritis suspected. Gout treatment: NSAIDs or colchicine (do NOT start allopurinol during acute attack — can prolong or precipitate attacks). Elbow joint septic arthritis often requires surgical washout — urgent orthopaedic review if sepsis confirmed.

15. Q: What is the "hook test" used to assess in elbow examination?
    A: UCL integrity — valgus stress
    B: Distal biceps tendon integrity — patient supines arm at 90° flexion, supinated; examiner attempts to hook a finger under the distal biceps tendon in the antecubital fossa; absent cord = distal biceps rupture
    C: Cubital tunnel — ulnar nerve
    D: Radial head fracture
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Hook test for distal biceps tendon: With the elbow at 90° and the forearm supinated, the examiner tries to "hook" a finger around the distal biceps tendon from the lateral side in the antecubital fossa. Normal: a distinct cord (tendon) is palpable. Abnormal (rupture): no cord palpable — only soft tissue (retracted muscle). The hook test is 100% sensitive and specific for complete distal biceps rupture in studies. Combined with the clinical features (sudden pop, Popeye deformity, significant supination weakness), a positive hook test confirms the diagnosis without requiring MRI in most cases. MRI is useful if partial tear is suspected or diagnosis is uncertain.

16. Q: A 70-year-old male with RA and chronic elbow OA has bilateral elbow flexion contractures of 30° (cannot fully extend). He manages ADLs with difficulty. What are the options?
    A: Immediate elbow replacement bilaterally
    B: Physiotherapy — serial splinting and stretching for mild contractures; surgical release (open or arthroscopic) for significant contractures limiting ADLs; total elbow replacement for end-stage RA with severe pain and functional limitation
    C: Corticosteroid injection — resolves contractures
    D: Amputation — only option for severe bilateral elbow disease
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Elbow flexion contracture management: Mild (<30°): physiotherapy — static progressive splinting (low-load prolonged stretch), dynamic splinting, serial casting. These techniques apply sustained low-force stretch to gradually elongate the contracted capsule. Significant contractures (>30°, limiting ADLs): surgical release — arthroscopic or open capsulotomy. Total elbow replacement (TEA/TER): for end-stage RA or OA with severe pain and functional limitation not amenable to other measures. TEA has revision rate higher than hip/knee — typically reserved for low-demand patients (RA, elderly). Prosthesis longevity is a concern in younger/higher-demand patients.

17. Q: What is the CRITOE mnemonic used for in paediatric elbow assessment?
    A: Criteria for surgical intervention in elbow fractures
    B: Order of ossification of paediatric elbow centres — Capitellum, Radial head, Internal (medial) epicondyle, Trochlea, Olecranon, External (lateral) epicondyle — helps identify normal vs avulsed ossification centres on X-ray
    C: A scoring system for elbow OA
    D: Assessment of nerve injuries in elbow trauma
    Correct: B
    Explanation: CRITOE — the order in which the secondary ossification centres appear on X-ray of the paediatric elbow (approximate ages): Capitellum (1 year), Radial head (3 years), Internal (medial) epicondyle (5 years), Trochlea (7 years), Olecranon (9 years), External (lateral) epicondyle (11 years). Knowledge of this sequence is crucial for identifying avulsed ossification centres: if the medial epicondyle ossification centre appears in an unexpected location (e.g. within the joint) — this is an incarcerated fracture fragment, not a normal centre. X-ray comparison with the contralateral elbow helps identify subtle asymmetries.

18. Q: A physiotherapist asks for advice on which exercise has the strongest evidence for lateral epicondylalgia rehabilitation. What should be recommended?
    A: Stretching only — no strengthening exercises as they aggravate the tendon
    B: Heavy slow resistance (HSR) exercises for the wrist extensors — eccentric and concentric phases, high load (3 x 15 repetitions with weight progression), 3x/week; this promotes tendon collagen remodelling and is superior to eccentric-only protocols
    C: Ultrasound therapy — most evidence-based physiotherapy modality
    D: Ice massage only — reduce inflammation first
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The evidence for tendinopathy rehabilitation has evolved: Heavy slow resistance (HSR) training — loading through the full range of motion (concentric and eccentric), at high loads (patient determines load — 3x15 repetitions with 3 kg is a typical starting point), with progressive load increase. This is now preferred over eccentric-only protocols (which were previously advocated). Mechanism: mechanical load stimulates tenocytes to produce collagen, promotes healthy collagen organisation, and reduces the degenerative tendon changes. NSAIDs can be used short-term for pain management during the initial loading phase. Isometric exercises (sustained contraction, no joint movement) are also effective for immediate pain relief during the early rehabilitation phase.

19. Q: A 45-year-old male has persistent lateral elbow pain for 18 months that has not responded to physiotherapy, two corticosteroid injections, or PRP injection. He is unable to work as a plasterer. What are the surgical options?
    A: No surgical options exist — continue conservative management indefinitely
    B: Surgical options: open or arthroscopic debridement of the common extensor origin (resection of the degenerative tendon tissue); success rates approximately 80–90% in carefully selected patients; requires post-operative rehabilitation; typically reserved for >12 months failure of conservative management

    Explanation: Surgical management of refractory lateral epicondylalgia: Open or arthroscopic debridement — resection of the degenerative tendon tissue (ECRB — extensor carpi radialis brevis) at the lateral epicondyle. Some surgeons also denervate the lateral epicondyle. Success rates approximately 80–90% in appropriately selected patients (true refractory cases after adequate conservative treatment). Post-operative rehabilitation is critical — progressive loading program post-surgery. Return to work typically 3–6 months post-operatively for manual workers. Arthroscopic technique has shorter recovery than open surgery and allows assessment of the radiocapitellar joint (OCD, loose bodies). Surgery should not be offered until at least 12 months of adequate conservative management has failed.

20. Q: A 30-year-old rock climber has anterior elbow pain that is worse with resisted elbow flexion and forearm supination. There is point tenderness in the antecubital fossa and the hook test is mildly positive but a cord is palpable. MRI shows partial distal biceps tendon tear. What is the management?
    A: Immediate surgical repair — all biceps tears require surgery
    B: Conservative management for partial tears — activity modification (avoid loaded elbow flexion and heavy supination for 6–8 weeks), progressive rehabilitation with physiotherapy, consider PRP injection for healing support; surgical repair only if fails conservative management or worsens to complete rupture
    C: Corticosteroid injection into the biceps tendon — reduces inflammation
    D: Amputation of the forearm
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Partial distal biceps tendon tears: Controversy exists between conservative and surgical management. For most partial tears (<50% tendon involvement) in non-elite athletes: conservative management — rest from provocative loading (6–8 weeks), progressive strengthening starting with isometrics then through range, physiotherapy, possible PRP injection. MRI monitoring if symptoms not improving. Complete rupture during conservative management: surgical repair (acute repair within 2–3 weeks best). For high-demand athletes (rock climbers, competitive weightlifters) or tears >50%: lower threshold for surgical repair. Corticosteroid injection into the distal biceps tendon is CONTRAINDICATED — significant risk of precipitating complete rupture.
