# MSK — Knee — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Locked knee (inability to extend) with acute haemarthrosis — ACL rupture, meniscal bucket handle tear — emergency orthopaedic referral
- Septic arthritis — hot, swollen, severely painful joint with fever — emergency joint aspiration and IV antibiotics
- Acute knee dislocation — neurovascular injury risk, popliteal artery, call 000
- Open fracture — bone exposed through wound — call 000
- Compartment syndrome after trauma — severe pain, tense compartment, pain on passive stretch — call 000
- Suspected pathological fracture — bone pain in known malignancy — urgent imaging

### Common Knee Presentations

#### Acute Trauma
- ACL rupture — audible pop, acute haemarthrosis, instability
- Meniscal tear — twisting injury, joint line pain, locking or clicking
- MCL/LCL sprain — medial or lateral pain after valgus/varus force
- Patellar dislocation — lateral displacement, usually self-reducing
- Tibial plateau fracture — high-energy or low-energy in osteoporotic patients

#### Overuse and Degenerative
- Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS) — anterior knee pain, young active patients
- Iliotibial band syndrome (ITBS) — lateral knee pain in runners
- Patellar tendinopathy ("jumper's knee") — inferior pole patella pain
- Osteoarthritis — medial compartment most common
- Quadriceps or patellar tendon rupture (degenerative)

#### Inflammatory and Infectious
- Gout and pseudogout
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Septic arthritis — emergency
- Reactive arthritis

#### Bursitis
- Prepatellar bursitis ("housemaid's knee") — direct pressure
- Infrapatellar bursitis
- Pes anserine bursitis — medial knee, diabetes, obesity

### Key Conditions

#### ACL Rupture
Most common significant knee ligament injury. Audible pop, immediate haemarthrosis, instability feeling ("giving way"). Lachman test (most sensitive) and anterior drawer test positive. MRI confirms diagnosis and associated injuries (meniscal tear in 50–70%). Management: physiotherapy for functional stability OR surgical reconstruction depending on activity level and instability. Young active patients — reconstruction generally recommended. Older sedentary patients — may manage conservatively.

#### Meniscal Tear
Acute (twisting injury, young) or degenerative (older, minimal trauma). Joint line tenderness, McMurray test positive (pain/click on rotation), Thessaly test. Mechanical symptoms — locking, clicking, giving way — suggest bucket handle tear requiring surgical referral. MRI confirms tear. Management: acute traumatic tears in young athletes — surgical repair or meniscectomy. Degenerative tears in older patients — physiotherapy often equivalent to surgery (FIDELITY trial).

#### Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome
Most common cause of anterior knee pain in young active patients. Diffuse anterior knee pain, worse with stairs, squatting, sitting long periods ("theatre sign"). No specific structural abnormality. Clarke's test often positive. Management: quadriceps strengthening (VMO), hip strengthening, patellar taping, orthotic insoles. Excellent prognosis with appropriate rehabilitation.

#### Knee Osteoarthritis
Most common joint disease — affects approximately 16% of Australians >45 years. Medial compartment most commonly affected. Presents: activity-related pain, morning stiffness <30 minutes (distinguishes from inflammatory arthritis), crepitus, reduced ROM, bony enlargement. Varus deformity common in medial OA. Management: exercise therapy (first-line — land and water-based), weight loss, analgesia (paracetamol first-line, NSAIDs short-term), intra-articular corticosteroid injections, physiotherapy. Total knee replacement for severe refractory cases.

#### Patellar Tendinopathy
Overuse injury — repetitive jumping sports (basketball, volleyball). Inferior pole of patella pain — worse with jumping, running, stairs. Royal London Hospital test (tenderness disappears when quadriceps contracted). VISA-P questionnaire for severity. Management: eccentric quadriceps loading (most effective), reduce provocative activity, NSAIDs short-term. Avoid corticosteroid injection into the tendon (risk of rupture). Shockwave therapy for refractory cases.

#### Prepatellar Bursitis
Swelling directly over the patella — fluctuant, non-tender initially. Caused by repeated direct pressure (carpet layers, roofers, plumbers). Septic bursitis: erythema, warmth, tenderness, fever — aspirate for culture. Treat aseptic: ice, compression, avoid pressure. Septic: aspiration + antibiotics (flucloxacillin).

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Knee History
- Mechanism — twisting, direct blow, fall, overuse, atraumatic
- Onset — sudden (ACL, fracture) vs gradual (OA, tendinopathy)
- Location — anterior (PFPS, patellar tendon), medial (meniscus, MCL, OA), lateral (ITB, LCL), posterior (PCL, Baker's cyst)
- Swelling — acute (haemarthrosis = ligament/bone) vs delayed 12–24h (effusion = meniscal)
- Locking — inability to fully extend suggests bucket handle meniscal tear
- Giving way — instability suggests ligament injury (ACL, PCL) or PFPS
- Previous injuries — recurrent instability
- Activity level — sport, occupation
- Crepitus — OA

### Knee Examination

#### Look (Inspection)
- Effusion, swelling — location (joint vs bursa vs periarticular)
- Skin — erythema, bruising, wounds
- Muscle wasting — quadriceps, especially VMO
- Alignment — varus (OA), valgus, patellar tracking

#### Feel (Palpation)
- Joint line tenderness — medial (medial meniscus, MCL) and lateral (lateral meniscus, LCL, ITB)
- Patellar tenderness — inferior pole (patellar tendinopathy), superior pole, peripatellar
- Ligament tenderness
- Popliteal fossa — Baker's cyst
- Temperature — warm joint suggests inflammation or infection

#### Move (Range of Motion)
- Active and passive flexion/extension — full ROM or restricted?
- Measure deficit — degrees from full extension and flexion
- Pain arc — where in range does pain occur?

#### Special Tests
- **Lachman Test**: ACL — 20° flexion, stabilise femur, pull tibia anteriorly — positive = soft endpoint (most sensitive for ACL)
- **Anterior Drawer**: ACL — 90° flexion, pull tibia anteriorly
- **Posterior Drawer**: PCL — 90° flexion, push tibia posteriorly
- **Valgus Stress**: MCL — apply valgus force at 0° and 30° flexion
- **Varus Stress**: LCL — apply varus force
- **McMurray Test**: Meniscus — flex and rotate knee, extend — positive = pain/click at joint line
- **Thessaly Test**: Meniscus — stand on one leg at 20° flexion and rotate — positive = joint line pain
- **Clarke's Test**: Patellofemoral — compress patella while patient contracts quads — positive = pain (poor specificity)
- **Patellar Apprehension**: Patellofemoral instability — lateral displacement of patella causes alarm
- **Effusion Tests**: Bulge sign (small effusion), Ballottement/Patellar tap (large effusion)

#### Investigations
- X-ray knee (AP, lateral, skyline/Merchant view): OA (joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis), fracture, loose bodies
- Ottawa Knee Rules: X-ray indicated if: age ≥55, isolated tenderness of fibula head, isolated tenderness of patella, inability to flex to 90°, inability to weight bear (4 steps)
- MRI knee: ligament and meniscal injuries, chondral damage, bone marrow oedema — most sensitive
- Ultrasound: soft tissue — bursitis, tendinopathy, effusion, Baker's cyst
- Joint aspiration: haemarthrosis (blood ± fat globules = intra-articular fracture), septic arthritis (turbid — WCC >50,000, Gram stain, culture), gout (crystals)
- FBE, ESR, CRP, uric acid: inflammatory arthritis, septic arthritis, gout

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 28-year-old with ACL Rupture During Football

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 28-year-old male football player with acute right knee injury 3 hours ago.

History: Planted foot to change direction during a match — heard an audible "pop" and immediate severe pain. Unable to continue playing. Significant rapid knee swelling developed within 1 hour (haemarthrosis). Describes a feeling of "the knee giving way" and instability. Walked to the car with difficulty. No direct impact. No previous right knee injuries. Works as a personal trainer — high physical activity demands.

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: Stable. Temp afebrile.

Right knee:
- Significant effusion — patellar tap positive, ballottement positive
- Bruising medial and anterolateral aspects
- Temperature slightly increased compared to left
- ROM: flexion to 90° limited by swelling and pain; extension full
- No joint line tenderness medially or laterally
- No bony tenderness over fibula head, patella, or tibial plateau

Special Tests:
- Lachman test: positive — soft endpoint, 10mm anterior translation compared to left
- Anterior drawer: positive
- McMurray test: negative bilaterally
- Valgus/varus stress: stable at 0° and 30°

X-ray right knee (AP and lateral): No fracture. No loose bodies. Significant soft tissue swelling.

**A — Assessment**
1. Right ACL rupture — clinical examination highly consistent (haemarthrosis, positive Lachman, soft endpoint, mechanism)
2. No associated fracture on X-ray; Ottawa criteria did not mandate X-ray but obtained given mechanism and haemarthrosis
3. Concurrent medial meniscal tear possible — McMurray negative but haemarthrosis limits examination; MRI required

**P — Plan**
Immediate:
- RICE: Rest, Ice (20 min every 2 hours), Compression bandage, Elevation
- Crutches — partial weight-bearing as tolerated
- NSAIDs: naproxen 500mg BD with food for 7–10 days
- Paracetamol 1g QID regular for analgesia

Investigations:
- MRI right knee — confirm ACL rupture, exclude meniscal tears and chondral damage; arrange within 1 week

Referral:
- Sports medicine physician or orthopaedic surgeon referral — surgical reconstruction vs conservative management decision based on age, activity level, and MRI findings; this patient (28, high activity demands) is a likely candidate for ACL reconstruction
- Pre-operative physiotherapy (prehabilitation) — restore range of motion, reduce swelling, strengthen quadriceps before surgery if proceeding

Education:
- Explained ACL injury, likely surgical pathway, rehabilitation timeline (9–12 months return to competitive football)
- Return to play criteria: 9 months minimum, functional testing, psychological readiness

Follow-up: 1 week — MRI result review, specialist appointment confirmed

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 22-year-old male footballer has an acute knee injury — audible pop, immediate swelling, and instability. Lachman test shows a soft endpoint with 10mm anterior tibial translation. What is the most likely diagnosis?
   A: MCL sprain — valgus stress test positive
   B: ACL rupture — Lachman test is the most sensitive clinical test for ACL integrity
   C: Patellar dislocation — patellar apprehension positive
   D: Meniscal tear — McMurray test positive
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Acute haemarthrosis (immediate swelling), pop sensation, instability, and positive Lachman test (soft endpoint = absent ACL endpoint) is the classic presentation of ACL rupture. Lachman test is performed at 20° knee flexion — it is the most sensitive clinical test for ACL rupture (sensitivity 85%, specificity 94%). Anterior drawer test is less sensitive because hamstring spasm at 90° resists tibial translation. MRI confirms the diagnosis and identifies associated injuries (meniscal tears in 50–70% of ACL ruptures).

2. Q: A 65-year-old woman has medial knee pain worsening over 12 months. She describes morning stiffness for 10 minutes, crepitus, and pain after walking 500 metres. X-ray shows medial joint space narrowing, osteophytes, and subchondral sclerosis. What is the diagnosis and first-line management?
   A: Inflammatory arthritis — DMARDs immediately
   B: Knee osteoarthritis — first-line: land-based or aquatic exercise therapy and weight loss; paracetamol for pain; physiotherapy
   C: Septic arthritis — joint aspiration and IV antibiotics
   D: Meniscal tear — MRI and arthroscopy
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Medial knee OA — activity-related pain, brief morning stiffness (<30 minutes — distinguishes from inflammatory arthritis), crepitus, and X-ray showing medial joint space narrowing with osteophytes and subchondral sclerosis. First-line management: exercise therapy (strongest evidence — reduces pain and improves function as effectively as surgery in most patients), weight loss (every 1kg lost reduces knee load by 4kg), paracetamol for analgesia, physiotherapy. NSAIDs short-term for acute flares. Intra-articular corticosteroid injections for acute pain. Total knee replacement for severe refractory OA.

3. Q: The Ottawa Knee Rules are used to determine when knee X-rays are indicated. Which finding mandates X-ray?
   A: Pain after a sporting injury in anyone under 40
   B: Age ≥55 years, isolated fibular head tenderness, isolated patellar tenderness, inability to flex to 90°, or inability to weight bear (4 steps)
   C: Any knee swelling
   D: Positive McMurray test
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Ottawa Knee Rules — X-ray indicated if any of: age ≥55 years, isolated fibular head tenderness, isolated patellar tenderness (no other bony tenderness), inability to flex knee to 90°, inability to weight bear (4 steps) both immediately and in the ED. These rules have 98–100% sensitivity for clinically significant fractures — if no criteria present, X-ray can be safely omitted. Validated for adults; less validated in children. They do NOT apply when the mechanism is very high energy or the clinical picture is uncertain.

4. Q: A hot, swollen, severely painful knee with fever (38.8°C), WCC 19.2, CRP 168 is seen in a 55-year-old who takes immunosuppressive medication. Most urgent management?
   A: NSAIDs and X-ray as outpatient
   B: Emergency joint aspiration (WCC >50,000, Gram stain, culture) before antibiotics; then IV antibiotics (flucloxacillin); orthopaedic review for washout
   C: Oral colchicine — likely gout
   D: Physiotherapy and compression bandage
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Hot, swollen, severely painful joint with systemic features (fever, elevated WCC and CRP) in an immunosuppressed patient is septic arthritis until proven otherwise. Emergency joint aspiration: fluid sent for WCC (>50,000 = septic arthritis until proven), Gram stain, culture, and crystals (to exclude gout/pseudogout). Do not give antibiotics before aspiration if possible — cultures may be negative. IV antibiotics after aspiration: flucloxacillin (covers Staph aureus — most common) or vancomycin if MRSA risk. Urgent orthopaedic review — surgical washout (arthroscopy) is often required. Delayed treatment destroys articular cartilage.

5. Q: A 35-year-old female runner has 3 months of lateral knee pain that comes on at 3km into her run and worsens. Tenderness at the lateral femoral condyle (Noble compression test positive). No effusion. Diagnosis and management?
   A: LCL sprain — varus stress test
   B: Iliotibial band syndrome (ITBS) — reduce mileage, ITB stretching, hip abductor strengthening, running biomechanics assessment, physiotherapy
   C: Lateral meniscal tear — MRI
   D: Biceps femoris tendinopathy — eccentric loading
   Correct: B
   Explanation: ITBS — the most common cause of lateral knee pain in runners. The ITB (iliotibial band) rubs over the lateral femoral condyle during the footstrike phase of running. Classic features: lateral knee pain starting after a consistent distance, Noble compression test positive (tenderness at lateral femoral condyle at 30° flexion). No effusion. Management: reduce running volume, hip abductor strengthening (gluteus medius weakness is a key contributing factor), ITB stretching, running technique assessment (avoid crossover gait), physiotherapy. NSAIDs short-term. Corticosteroid injection at lateral femoral condyle for refractory cases. Avoid surgery unless truly refractory.

6. Q: A 16-year-old male basketball player has inferior pole patellar pain that is worse after jumping and running, and persists after training. VISA-P score 45/100. What is the diagnosis and most effective treatment?
   A: Osgood-Schlatter disease — rest until skeletally mature
   B: Patellar tendinopathy — eccentric quadriceps loading program (e.g. decline squat protocol) is the most evidence-based treatment; reduce provocative load temporarily
   C: PFPS — VMO strengthening and patellar taping
   D: Patellar fracture — X-ray and immobilisation
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Patellar tendinopathy (jumper's knee) — inferior pole patella pain in a jumping sport athlete, VISA-P score (lower = worse). Most effective treatment: eccentric loading — specifically the decline squat protocol (slow eccentric loading on a 25° decline board, 3x15 reps twice daily). This applies controlled mechanical load to the tendon promoting collagen remodelling. NSAIDs short-term for pain. Avoid corticosteroid injection into the tendon — significant risk of tendon rupture. Shockwave therapy for chronic refractory cases. VISA-P monitoring guides return to full training. Osgood-Schlatter involves the tibial tuberosity (not inferior patellar pole) and occurs at the tibial apophysis in adolescents.

7. Q: A 50-year-old woman develops acute severe knee pain, significant swelling, and warmth. She is not febrile. Joint aspiration reveals positively birefringent rhomboid crystals. What is the diagnosis and treatment?
   A: Gout — negatively birefringent, needle-shaped crystals; colchicine
   B: Pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease, CPPD) — positively birefringent, rhomboid crystals; NSAIDs or colchicine for acute attack; intra-articular corticosteroid
   C: Septic arthritis — IV antibiotics
   D: Rheumatoid arthritis — DMARD therapy
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Crystal arthropathy identification: Gout — negatively birefringent, needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals (MSU); Pseudogout — positively birefringent, rhomboid-shaped calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals. Pseudogout most commonly affects the knee. Predisposing conditions: haemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesaemia — screen if recurrent. Acute management: NSAIDs or colchicine (same as gout), intra-articular corticosteroid injection. X-ray may show chondrocalcinosis (calcification of knee menisci and articular cartilage). No equivalent of allopurinol for pseudogout — no urate-lowering therapy.

8. Q: A 45-year-old male presents with medial knee pain 3 days after a twisting injury playing tennis. Joint line tenderness medially, McMurray test produces a click and medial joint line pain on external rotation. No effusion (onset delayed >24 hours). Diagnosis and management?
   A: MCL sprain — valgus stress test and physiotherapy
   B: Medial meniscal tear — MRI; physiotherapy for degenerative tears, surgical repair or meniscectomy for mechanical symptoms or acute tears in young patients
   C: Pes anserine bursitis — ice and NSAIDs
   D: ACL rupture — Lachman test
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Medial joint line tenderness + positive McMurray test (click/pain on medial joint line with external rotation and extension) is highly suggestive of medial meniscal tear. Delayed effusion (>24 hours) suggests meniscal rather than ligamentous injury (haemarthrosis from ACL rupture is immediate). MRI confirms the tear. Management: physiological tears — degenerative meniscal tears in older patients often respond to physiotherapy as well as surgery (FIDELITY trial). Mechanical symptoms (locking, giving way) — surgical referral for arthroscopic repair or meniscectomy. Acute bucket handle tears causing locking — urgent orthopaedic referral.

9. Q: An 18-year-old female has diffuse anterior knee pain for 4 months, worse with stairs, squatting, and sitting for prolonged periods. Examination shows peripatellar tenderness, mild quadriceps wasting, and VMO weakness. No joint line tenderness. X-ray normal. Diagnosis and management?
   A: Patellar tendinopathy — eccentric loading
   B: Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS) — VMO and hip strengthening, patellar taping (McConnell technique), orthotics if overpronation, activity modification
   C: Meniscal tear — MRI
   D: OA — X-ray confirms diagnosis at 18 years
   Correct: B
   Explanation: PFPS is the most common cause of anterior knee pain in young active females. Features: diffuse anterior knee pain, worse with stairs and prolonged sitting (theatre sign), peripatellar tenderness, VMO weakness, positive Clarke's test (pain on patellar compression with quads contracted). No joint line tenderness (distinguishes from meniscal pathology). Management: VMO strengthening (short-arc quads, leg press), hip abductor and external rotator strengthening (reduces valgus loading), patellar taping (McConnell), orthotics for overpronation, activity modification during rehabilitation. Excellent prognosis with appropriate physiotherapy.

10. Q: Which clinical test is MOST sensitive for ACL integrity?
    A: Anterior drawer test at 90° flexion
    B: Lachman test at 20° flexion — most sensitive (sensitivity ~85%) due to reduced hamstring resistance
    C: McMurray test
    D: Valgus stress test
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Lachman test (20° flexion) is the most sensitive and clinically useful test for ACL rupture. At 90° (anterior drawer), hamstring contraction resists anterior tibial translation — reducing sensitivity. At 20° (Lachman), hamstrings are more relaxed allowing better assessment of ACL integrity. Positive result: increased anterior translation compared to the contralateral side with a soft or absent endpoint (ACL normally provides a firm endpoint). Pivot shift test is most specific for rotational instability but is painful — better performed under anaesthesia. Always compare to the contralateral knee.

11. Q: A 70-year-old male with severe bilateral knee OA undergoes total knee replacement (TKR) of the right knee. He presents at the 6-week outpatient review with increasing right knee redness, swelling, and pain — worse than at 4 weeks. Temperature 38.2°C. WCC 16.8. What is the most urgent concern?
    A: Normal post-operative inflammation — reassure
    B: Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) — urgent orthopaedic review, joint aspiration for WCC and culture before antibiotics, likely removal of prosthesis required
    C: DVT — start anticoagulation
    D: Wound dehiscence — wound care and antibiotics
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) after total knee replacement is a devastating complication — increasing pain, swelling, and erythema with fever at 6 weeks post-TKR. Diagnosis: joint aspiration (elevated WCC, positive culture — most important). Treatment: prolonged IV antibiotics AND prosthesis removal (debridement alone rarely sufficient for established PJI — two-stage revision is the gold standard). Early recognition is critical — delayed diagnosis worsens outcomes. Risk factors: diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression, prior infection. Always aspirate the joint before starting antibiotics — cultures may be negative if antibiotics are given first.

12. Q: A 60-year-old female with knee OA asks about intra-articular corticosteroid injections. What is the evidence and appropriate advice?
    A: Corticosteroid injections are curative — one injection per year is sufficient to prevent joint replacement
    B: Corticosteroid injections provide short-term pain relief (weeks to months) for knee OA flares; may be repeated; high-quality evidence shows no long-term benefit over exercise; repeated injections may accelerate cartilage loss — use judiciously
    C: Corticosteroid injections are contraindicated in knee OA
    D: Corticosteroid injections replace the need for exercise therapy
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Intra-articular corticosteroid (IACS) injections for knee OA: Provide short-term pain relief — typically 4–12 weeks. May be repeated — generally no more than 3–4 times per year. Recent evidence suggests repeated IACS injections may accelerate cartilage loss — use for acute flares rather than regular maintenance. IACS do NOT replace exercise therapy — exercise has greater long-term benefit. Before each injection, consider: current symptoms, timing since last injection, alternatives. Hyaluronic acid injections — conflicting evidence, not recommended in most Australian guidelines as first-line. PRP (platelet-rich plasma) — promising early evidence, not yet standard of care.

13. Q: A 35-year-old male carpet layer has a painless swelling directly over the patella that has developed over several months of kneeling on hard floors. The swelling is fluctuant and transilluminates. The knee joint ROM is full and painless. Diagnosis and management?
    A: Joint effusion — knee aspiration and examination
    B: Prepatellar bursitis — avoid pressure (knee pads), aspiration if large, NSAIDs; if erythematous and warm — aspire and send for culture (septic bursitis — antibiotics if positive)
    C: Patellar fracture — X-ray
    D: Patellar tendinopathy — eccentric loading
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Prepatellar bursitis (housemaid's knee) — fluctuant swelling OVER the patella (not in the joint), full painless knee ROM (joint not involved). Caused by repetitive direct pressure on the prepatellar bursa. Aseptic: NSAIDs, avoid pressure (knee pads), aspiration if very large. Septic bursitis (erythema, warmth, fever): aspire bursa and send for WCC, Gram stain, culture — flucloxacillin if Staph aureus confirmed. Distinguish from joint effusion: effusion is in the joint (patellar tap positive, ballottement), prepatellar bursitis swelling is superficial to the joint (patellar tap negative). Full pain-free ROM helps distinguish bursitis from intra-articular pathology.

14. Q: A 55-year-old female with knee OA and BMI 34 asks about the most effective non-surgical interventions. What does evidence show?
    A: Topical diclofenac only — most effective with fewest side effects
    B: Exercise therapy (any form — walking, aquatic, strength) and weight loss are the most effective interventions; GLA:D program (education + neuromuscular exercise) is a validated Australian program
    C: Bed rest and joint offloading — activity damages OA cartilage
    D: Glucosamine and chondroitin — best evidence for symptom relief
    Correct: B
    Explanation: For knee OA, exercise therapy is the most effective non-pharmacological intervention — reduces pain and improves function comparably to surgery in most patients. ALL exercise forms are beneficial — walking, cycling, aquatic exercise, strength training. Weight loss: every 1kg reduces knee joint load by 4kg — significant impact in overweight patients. GLA:D (Good Life with osteoArthritis in Denmark) — an Australian-adapted education and neuromuscular exercise program with strong evidence. Glucosamine and chondroitin — conflicting evidence, not recommended in most Australian guidelines. Topical NSAIDs (diclofenac gel) — effective for symptomatic relief with fewer systemic effects than oral NSAIDs, particularly in elderly.

15. Q: A 17-year-old female gymnast lands awkwardly from a vault and her left patella dislocates laterally, then reduces spontaneously. She presents in pain with medial knee tenderness and mild effusion. What is the immediate management and investigation?
    A: MRI immediately — only after MRI should treatment begin
    B: X-ray to exclude associated fracture (osteochondral fracture occurs in ~40%); RICE; immobilisation in extension initially; then physiotherapy for VMO and hip strengthening; MRI if surgical consideration (significant osteochondral fragment requiring fixation)
    C: Surgical patellar stabilisation immediately — first-time dislocation always requires surgery
    D: Ignore — spontaneous reduction is reassuring, no further treatment needed
    Correct: B
    Explanation: First-time patellar dislocation management: X-ray (AP, lateral, skyline) — exclude osteochondral fracture (tear of medial patellar retinaculum can avulse a bony fragment). Initial management: RICE, immobilisation in extension (brace/cylinder cast) for 2–4 weeks. Physiotherapy is the mainstay — VMO and hip abductor strengthening, patellar taping, proprioception. MRI if: significant effusion (haemarthrosis — suggests osteochondral injury requiring surgery), or surgical stabilisation consideration (MPFL reconstruction for recurrent dislocations — typically after ≥2 episodes or in high-risk anatomy). First-time dislocations — recurrence risk approximately 15–44%; surgery generally reserved for recurrent cases.

16. Q: A Baker's cyst (popliteal cyst) is found on ultrasound in a 60-year-old male with knee OA and medial joint line tenderness. What is the appropriate management?
    A: Surgical excision of the cyst is required
    B: Treat the underlying knee pathology (OA, meniscal tear) — Baker's cysts are secondary to intra-articular pathology and usually resolve when the primary condition is managed; aspiration and corticosteroid injection if symptomatic
    C: Anticoagulate — DVT must be excluded
    D: No treatment — Baker's cysts are always asymptomatic
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Baker's (popliteal) cyst is a fluid-filled synovial cyst in the posterior knee — a direct communication with the knee joint. It is almost always secondary to intra-articular pathology (OA, meniscal tear, RA) — the joint effusion fills the cyst. Management: treat the underlying pathology (manage OA or repair/meniscectomy for meniscal tear) — the cyst usually resolves when intra-articular inflammation is controlled. Direct aspiration and corticosteroid injection of the cyst provides temporary relief. Ruptured Baker's cyst: acute posterior calf pain and swelling — mimics DVT (perform ultrasound to distinguish — important differential). Surgical excision rarely required.

17. Q: What is the mechanism and typical presentation of a PCL (posterior cruciate ligament) rupture?
    A: Non-contact twisting injury with valgus force — same as ACL
    B: Dashboard injury — direct blow to proximal tibia with knee flexed (MVA); posterior sag of tibia at 90° flexion; positive posterior drawer test; posterior tibial translation
    C: Hyperextension injury — same mechanism as LCL tear
    D: Jumping and landing — same as patellar tendinopathy
    Correct: B
    Explanation: PCL rupture mechanism: direct blow to the proximal tibia with knee flexed (dashboard injury in MVA) or forced hyperflexion. Clinical examination: posterior sag sign (tibia sags posteriorly at 90° flexion when compared to contralateral knee), positive posterior drawer test (tibia can be pushed posteriorly), absent posterior endpoint. MRI confirms diagnosis. PCL ruptures are less common than ACL. Most isolated PCL ruptures can be managed conservatively with physiotherapy (especially quadriceps strengthening). Surgical reconstruction for combined ligament injuries (PCL + ACL or PCL + posterolateral corner) or significant functional instability.

18. Q: A 72-year-old female with advanced bilateral knee OA has severe pain despite physiotherapy, weight loss, and multiple intra-articular injections. She asks about total knee replacement (TKR). What are the key criteria for TKR?
    A: TKR should be performed as soon as OA is diagnosed on X-ray
    B: Severe OA with significant pain and functional limitation NOT adequately controlled by conservative management (exercise, weight loss, analgesia, injections) — X-ray confirms moderate-severe OA; patient must understand rehabilitation requirements
    C: TKR is only for patients under 65 — elderly patients are not suitable
    D: BMI >35 is an absolute contraindication to TKR
    Correct: B
    Explanation: TKR indications: severe knee OA causing significant pain and functional limitation (inability to walk reasonable distances, disturbed sleep, impaired ADLs) that has not responded to adequate conservative management (exercise, weight loss, physiotherapy, analgesia, injections). X-ray shows moderate-severe OA (joint space narrowing, osteophytes, deformity). TKR in appropriately selected patients has excellent outcomes — 90–95% satisfaction, 15–20 year implant survival. Key pre-operative requirements: BMI <40 ideally (higher BMI increases complications but is not absolute contraindication — shared decision-making), smoking cessation, blood sugar optimisation (DM), prehabilitation physiotherapy. The decision should be shared between the patient and orthopaedic surgeon.

19. Q: An 8-year-old boy has anterior knee pain centred over the tibial tuberosity, worsened by running and jumping. The tibial tuberosity is tender and slightly prominent. What is the diagnosis?
    A: Patellar tendinopathy — eccentric loading
    B: Osgood-Schlatter disease — apophysitis of the tibial tuberosity; activity modification, quadriceps stretching, NSAIDs; usually self-limiting at skeletal maturity
    C: PFPS — VMO strengthening
    D: Septic arthritis — joint aspiration
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Osgood-Schlatter disease is apophysitis of the tibial tuberosity — occurs in active adolescents during growth spurts (ages 10–15). The patellar tendon pulls on the tibial apophysis during the growth spurt causing inflammation and sometimes avulsion. Classic: tibial tuberosity tenderness and prominence in an active adolescent. Management: activity modification (reduce impact activities), quadriceps and hamstring stretching, NSAIDs for acute pain, physiotherapy, patellar tendon strap. Usually self-limiting — resolves at skeletal maturity (growth plate closure). Rarely, a painful ossicle at the tibial tuberosity persists into adulthood requiring excision.

20. Q: A patient asks whether arthroscopy is appropriate for their degenerative medial meniscal tear and knee OA. They are 58 years old with medial knee pain. MRI shows a degenerative medial meniscal tear with moderate OA changes. What does current evidence show?
    A: Arthroscopic meniscectomy is the gold standard for all meniscal tears — recommend surgery immediately
    B: For degenerative meniscal tears in middle-aged and older patients with OA, arthroscopic meniscectomy is NOT superior to physiotherapy and exercise — conservative management should be first-line
    C: Surgery is contraindicated in the presence of OA — only TKR is appropriate
    D: MRI-confirmed tears always require surgery regardless of age
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The FIDELITY trial (2013) and subsequent studies demonstrated that arthroscopic partial meniscectomy for degenerative meniscal tears in middle-aged and older patients is NOT superior to physiotherapy for pain relief and function at 1 and 5 years. Exercise-based physiotherapy is equally effective. This changed practice significantly — arthroscopy for degenerative meniscal tears in OA should not be routine first-line treatment. Conservative management: physiotherapy, exercise, weight loss, analgesia. Arthroscopy may still be appropriate for: mechanical symptoms (locking from bucket handle tear), acute traumatic tears in young patients, or failure of conservative management after adequate trial.
