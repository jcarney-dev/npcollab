# MSK — Back — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Cauda Equina Syndrome — Call 000
- Bilateral leg weakness or paralysis
- Saddle anaesthesia (numbness around perineum, genitals, inner thighs)
- Bladder or bowel dysfunction — urinary retention, incontinence, faecal incontinence
- This is a surgical emergency — emergency transfer for urgent MRI and neurosurgical review

### Other Red Flags Requiring Urgent Investigation
- Back pain with fever — discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, epidural abscess
- Back pain in known malignancy — metastatic spinal cord compression
- Progressive neurological deficit — foot drop, progressive weakness
- Back pain with unexplained weight loss — malignancy
- Onset after significant trauma in older or osteoporotic patient — vertebral fracture
- Back pain in IV drug user — septic discitis
- Back pain unrelieved by rest — inflammatory or malignant cause

### Common Back Pain Presentations

#### Non-Specific Low Back Pain (NSLBP)
The most common musculoskeletal presentation in primary care. Most episodes resolve within 6 weeks. Active management is the evidence base — avoid bed rest, encourage early return to normal activities.

#### Specific Low Back Pain
- Lumbar disc herniation with radiculopathy (sciatica)
- Lumbar spinal stenosis — neurogenic claudication
- Vertebral fracture — osteoporotic compression fracture
- Spondylolisthesis
- Inflammatory back pain — ankylosing spondylitis / axial spondyloarthropathy

### Key Conditions

#### Non-Specific Low Back Pain
Biopsychosocial model: mechanical pain + psychosocial factors (fear-avoidance, catastrophising, depression, work dissatisfaction). Yellow flags predict chronicity. Management: education, staying active, short-term NSAIDs, physiotherapy. Avoid bed rest and opioids.

#### Lumbar Disc Herniation with Radiculopathy
Disc material compresses a nerve root producing sciatica — radicular pain radiating below the knee in a dermatomal pattern. L4–L5 and L5–S1 most common. Positive SLR at <70° reproduces leg pain. Majority resolve within 6–12 weeks conservatively. MRI if considering surgery or red flags. Surgical referral (microdiscectomy) for persistent pain >6–12 weeks, progressive neurological deficit, or cauda equina (emergency).

#### Lumbar Spinal Stenosis
Narrowing of the spinal canal causing neurogenic claudication — bilateral leg pain and heaviness on walking, relieved by sitting or leaning forward (flexion). Distinguished from vascular claudication: neurogenic relieved by flexion, vascular by stopping walking. Management: physiotherapy, exercise. Surgical decompression for severe cases.

#### Osteoporotic Vertebral Fracture
Acute severe back pain after minimal trauma in osteoporotic patient. Midline tenderness. X-ray shows vertebral height loss. Management: pain management, mobilisation, osteoporosis treatment (zoledronic acid, denosumab), calcium and vitamin D.

#### Ankylosing Spondylitis (Axial Spondyloarthropathy)
Inflammatory back pain in young adults (<45). Features: insidious onset, morning stiffness >30 min, improves with exercise, worsens with rest, alternating buttock pain. HLA-B27 positive >90%. MRI sacroiliac joints. Refer to rheumatology. NSAIDs highly effective. Biologics for severe disease.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Back Pain History
- Onset — sudden vs gradual
- Location — lumbar, thoracic; localised vs diffuse
- Character — dull aching (muscular), sharp shooting (radicular), burning (neuropathic)
- Radiation — below the knee = significant radiculopathy
- Duration — acute (<6 weeks), subacute (6–12 weeks), chronic (>12 weeks)
- Aggravating and relieving factors — flexion vs extension, rest vs activity, night pain

#### Red Flag Screen
- Bladder or bowel dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia, bilateral leg symptoms
- Fever, weight loss, history of malignancy, IV drug use
- Age <20 or >50 with new onset, significant trauma, corticosteroid use

#### Yellow Flags (Psychosocial)
- Fear-avoidance beliefs, catastrophising, depression, anxiety
- Work dissatisfaction, compensation claims, poor social support

### Back Examination

#### Inspection and Range of Motion
- Posture, scoliosis, loss of lumbar lordosis
- Flexion (fingertip to floor), extension, lateral flexion, rotation

#### Palpation
- Midline spinous process tenderness (fracture, discitis)
- Paravertebral muscle spasm
- SI joint tenderness

#### Neurological Examination
| Level | Weakness | Sensory | Reflex |
|-------|----------|---------|--------|
| L3–L4 | Knee extension | Inner calf | Knee jerk |
| L4–L5 | Ankle dorsiflexion (foot drop) | Dorsum foot, outer calf | None |
| L5–S1 | Plantar flexion | Sole, outer foot | Ankle jerk |

#### Special Tests
- SLR (Straight Leg Raise): positive if radicular pain reproduced at <70°
- Crossed SLR: highly specific for large disc herniation
- FABER test: SI joint pain
- Stork test: spondylolysis

#### Investigations
- X-ray: NOT first-line for NSLBP; indicated for red flags, trauma, inflammatory arthritis
- MRI: gold standard; order for red flags, neurological deficit, persistent radiculopathy >6 weeks, pre-surgery
- FBE, ESR, CRP: inflammatory or infectious cause
- HLA-B27: suspected ankylosing spondylitis
- DEXA: after vertebral fracture

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 38-year-old with Acute L4–L5 Disc Herniation and Right L5 Radiculopathy

**S — Subjective**
38-year-old male warehouse worker. 3 weeks of low back pain after lifting, now predominantly right buttock pain radiating to dorsum of right foot. Sharp shooting pain 7/10. Worse with sitting, bending, coughing. Developed right foot weakness — catching on stairs. No bladder or bowel dysfunction. No saddle anaesthesia. PHQ-9 score 8 (mild depression). Fear-avoidance beliefs present. Workers compensation claim in progress.

**O — Objective**
Antalgic lean left. Flexion severely restricted. Right paravertebral muscle spasm L4–S1. Midline non-tender.

Neurological right LL: ankle dorsiflexion 4/5 (mild foot drop), reduced sensation dorsum of right foot, ankle jerk mildly reduced.

SLR right: positive at 40° reproducing right leg pain and foot tingling. SLR left: negative at 80°.

**A — Assessment**
1. Right L4–L5 disc herniation with L5 nerve root compression — foot drop present
2. Yellow flags — fear avoidance, work stress, mild depression

**P — Plan**
- Urgent MRI lumbar spine — progressive neurological deficit (foot drop)
- Analgesia: ibuprofen 400mg TDS + paracetamol 1g QID; oxycodone 5mg PRN short-term for night pain only
- Encourage gentle walking daily — activity is beneficial
- Urgent spinal surgery/neurosurgery referral — foot drop = progressive neurological deficit
- Physiotherapy — active rehabilitation when pain allows
- PHQ-9 8 — monitor depression, offer counselling, address fear-avoidance
- Safety netting: return IMMEDIATELY if bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia, bilateral weakness, worsening foot drop

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 45-year-old male has 2 weeks of acute low back pain after lifting. No leg pain, no red flags, normal neurology. What is the most appropriate initial management?
   A: MRI lumbar spine immediately
   B: Reassurance; encourage staying active; short-term NSAIDs; physiotherapy if not improving; NO bed rest
   C: Bed rest for 1 week
   D: Opioid analgesia
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Non-specific low back pain without red flags does not require imaging. Evidence-based management: reassurance (recovery is expected), staying active (strongest evidence), short-term NSAIDs or paracetamol, physiotherapy for persistent cases. Bed rest delays recovery. Opioids have poor evidence for NSLBP and significant harm.

2. Q: A 30-year-old presents with 4 months of back pain worse after rest, improves with exercise, morning stiffness 1 hour, alternating buttock pain. ESR 45. Most likely diagnosis?
   A: Non-specific mechanical low back pain
   B: Axial spondyloarthropathy — refer rheumatology; HLA-B27; MRI sacroiliac joints
   C: Lumbar disc herniation
   D: Fibromyalgia
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Inflammatory back pain features: age <45, insidious onset, improves with exercise not rest, night pain, alternating buttock pain. Elevated ESR. Strongly suggests axial spondyloarthropathy. HLA-B27 positive >90%, MRI SI joints for early sacroiliitis. NSAIDs highly effective. Biologics for severe disease.

3. Q: A 70-year-old woman with osteoporosis has acute severe thoracic back pain after bending. Midline T8 tenderness. Most likely diagnosis and management?
   A: Pulled muscle — rest
   B: Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture — X-ray, analgesia, mobilisation, optimise osteoporosis treatment
   C: Disc herniation — MRI
   D: Metastatic cord compression — dexamethasone
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Acute midline back pain after minimal trauma in osteoporotic patient is vertebral compression fracture. X-ray confirms vertebral height loss. Analgesia, mobilisation, osteoporosis treatment (zoledronic acid, denosumab), calcium and vitamin D.

4. Q: Which finding warrants urgent MRI in a back pain patient?
   A: Pain lasting more than 2 weeks
   B: Cauda equina symptoms (bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia, bilateral leg weakness) — emergency same-day MRI
   C: All patients with buttock radiation
   D: Patient request
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Cauda equina syndrome is a neurosurgical emergency requiring same-day MRI and urgent surgical decompression. Other MRI indications: red flags, progressive neurological deficit, radiculopathy not improving after 6 weeks. Routine NSLBP does not need imaging.

5. Q: A 65-year-old male has bilateral calf and thigh pain after walking 200m, relieved by sitting or leaning on a shopping trolley. ABI 1.0. Diagnosis?
   A: Peripheral arterial disease
   B: Lumbar spinal stenosis causing neurogenic claudication — MRI lumbar spine; physiotherapy; surgical decompression if severe
   C: Bilateral disc herniations
   D: Peripheral neuropathy
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Neurogenic claudication: bilateral leg symptoms, relieved by sitting/flexion (not just stopping), worse going downhill, normal ABI. Vascular claudication: relieved by stopping, worse going uphill, abnormal ABI. ABI 1.0 excludes significant PAD.

6. Q: A patient describes saddle anaesthesia and difficulty initiating urination with bilateral leg weakness. Most urgent action?
   A: MRI outpatient in 1 week
   B: Call 000 — cauda equina syndrome, neurosurgical emergency
   C: Catheterise and arrange physiotherapy
   D: Oral corticosteroids
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Cauda equina syndrome requires emergency MRI and surgical decompression within hours. Features: bilateral leg weakness, saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention, bowel dysfunction. Permanent disability results from delayed treatment.

7. Q: A 55-year-old with breast cancer has new onset severe thoracic back pain. What must be excluded?
   A: Osteoporotic fracture only
   B: Metastatic spinal cord compression — dexamethasone 16mg if neurological signs; urgent MRI whole spine
   C: Musculoskeletal strain
   D: Disc herniation
   Correct: B
   Explanation: New back pain in any cancer patient is MSCC until proven otherwise. Dexamethasone 16mg immediately if neurological signs present. Urgent MRI whole spine. Treatment: radiotherapy, surgery. Neurological function at time of treatment predicts outcome.

8. Q: A 25-year-old on bed rest for 4 weeks with NSLBP — what key message?
   A: Continue bed rest for further 4 weeks
   B: Bed rest delays recovery — staying active is the most effective treatment; return to normal activities as soon as possible
   C: MRI now — 6 weeks warrants imaging
   D: Strong opioids to rest more comfortably
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Bed rest is harmful for NSLBP — causes deconditioning, fear-avoidance, and chronicity. Exercise is the best treatment. Return to work and normal activities improves outcomes. Activity is safe; pain does not mean damage.

9. Q: A 42-year-old with 3 months LBP has PHQ-9 16 and high pain catastrophising. Most important management?
   A: Urgent MRI
   B: Address psychosocial yellow flags — treat depression, pain neuroscience education, CBT, graded activity
   C: Strong opioids — her pain is clearly severe
   D: Psychiatric referral only
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Yellow flags (depression, catastrophising, fear-avoidance) are the strongest predictors of chronic disabling back pain. Address psychosocial factors directly — antidepressant, CBT, pain neuroscience education, graded activity. These interventions have stronger evidence than passive treatments or opioids.

10. Q: What constitutes a positive Straight Leg Raise (SLR) test?
    A: Hamstring tightness at 45°
    B: Radicular pain reproduced below the knee at <70° of hip flexion
    C: Back pain at 60°
    D: Buttock pain at 90°
    Correct: B
    Explanation: SLR tests neural tension in lumbosacral nerve roots. Positive = reproduction of radicular pain (dermatomal, below the knee) at <70°. Back pain or hamstring tightness alone is NOT positive. Sensitivity ~80% for L4–S1 disc herniation.

11. Q: A 50-year-old with L4–L5 disc herniation and right L5 radiculopathy, no neurological deficit, managing with NSAIDs and physiotherapy. When should surgery be considered?
    A: Surgery immediately — disc herniations always require surgery
    B: If conservative management fails after 6–12 weeks, symptoms significantly impact quality of life, or neurological deficit develops — 90% resolve without surgery
    C: Never — always conservative
    D: MRI showing disc herniation means surgery needed
    Correct: B
    Explanation: 90% of disc herniations with radiculopathy resolve with conservative management within 6–12 weeks. Surgery produces faster relief but similar 1-year outcomes. Indications: failure of 6–12 weeks conservative management, intolerable pain, progressive neurological deficit, or cauda equina (emergency).

12. Q: A 68-year-old on long-term prednisolone has a fragility vertebral fracture. Priority management beyond analgesia?
    A: Vertebroplasty immediately
    B: DEXA scan; initiate anti-osteoporotic treatment (zoledronic acid, denosumab, teriparatide); calcium and vitamin D; fall prevention
    C: Stop prednisolone immediately
    D: Spine surgery for fusion
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Fragility fracture on corticosteroids = glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP). DEXA scan, anti-osteoporotic treatment (zoledronic acid annually or denosumab 6-monthly), calcium 1200mg/day and vitamin D 1000–2000 IU/day, fall prevention. All patients on prednisolone ≥7.5mg/day >3 months should receive prophylactic anti-osteoporotic therapy.

13. Q: What are "yellow flags" in back pain assessment?
    A: MRI findings requiring surgery
    B: Psychosocial risk factors predicting transition to chronic disability — fear-avoidance, catastrophising, depression, poor job satisfaction, passive coping
    C: Red flags for serious pathology
    D: Signs of malingering
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Yellow flags are psychosocial risk factors for chronicity. Key flags: beliefs that pain = damage, fear-avoidance, depression, anxiety, catastrophising, work dissatisfaction, compensation claims, poor social support. Identification allows targeted psychosocial intervention — CBT, pain education, graded activity.

14. Q: What distinguishes radicular pain from discogenic referred pain?
    A: They are the same
    B: Radicular pain radiates below the knee in a dermatomal pattern with possible neurological deficit; discogenic pain is localised to back ± buttock, non-dermatomal, no neurological deficit
    C: Discogenic always requires surgery; radicular does not
    D: Sciatica is only from piriformis
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Discogenic/somatic pain: localised to lumbar region ± referred to buttock/posterior thigh — not below knee, not dermatomal. Radicular pain: dermatomal below the knee with possible weakness, sensory loss, reduced reflexes. The distinction guides management — radiculopathy warrants neurological assessment and may need MRI and surgical consultation.

15. Q: A 19-year-old cricketer (fast bowler) has back pain worsening with extension and unilateral stork test. Suspected diagnosis?
    A: Lumbar disc herniation
    B: Spondylolysis (pars interarticularis stress fracture) — CT or SPECT more sensitive than X-ray; activity modification; physiotherapy; bracing
    C: Ankylosing spondylitis
    D: Non-specific LBP
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Spondylolysis is common in young athletes with repetitive lumbar extension — fast bowlers, gymnasts, rowers. Stork test (standing extension on one leg) reproduces pain. X-ray may miss it — CT most specific, MRI detects stress reaction. Activity modification, physiotherapy, 3–6 months to return to sport.

16. Q: Which is NOT recommended for non-specific low back pain?
    A: Encouraging staying active
    B: Short-term NSAIDs
    C: Prolonged opioid therapy (>4 weeks)
    D: Physiotherapy for persistent cases
    Correct: C
    Explanation: Opioids are NOT recommended for NSLBP — minimal pain relief, do not improve function, significant risks (dependence, overdose, hyperalgesia). Australian guidelines recommend against routine opioid prescribing for NSLBP. If short-term opioids used, limit to <1 week.

17. Q: What is the evidence-based psychological intervention for fear-avoidance in chronic back pain?
    A: Psychoanalysis
    B: Pain neuroscience education (PNE) and CBT — explaining pain neuroscience, challenging fear-avoidance, graded exposure, coping skills
    C: Hypnotherapy
    D: Reassure pain is not real
    Correct: B
    Explanation: PNE teaches patients about pain neuroscience — why pain does not equal tissue damage, central sensitisation, role of thoughts and emotions. Combined with CBT (challenging maladaptive cognitions, graded exposure) has strong evidence for chronic LBP with psychosocial contributors. More effective than passive treatments.

18. Q: A 55-year-old with back pain has: history of colon cancer (3 years remission), long-term corticosteroids, elevated PSA 18 ng/mL, haematuria, weight loss 6kg. Which red flags are present?
    A: No red flags
    B: History of malignancy AND corticosteroid use (osteoporosis/fracture risk) AND elevated PSA AND haematuria AND weight loss — urgent cancer workup required
    C: Smoking only
    D: COPD only
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Multiple red flags: prior malignancy (bone mets concern), long-term steroids (osteoporotic fracture), elevated PSA (prostate cancer), haematuria, weight loss. Urgent investigation: X-ray, MRI if concerned, FBE, CRP, ALP, PSA trend, protein electrophoresis, urology referral. Do NOT manage as NSLBP.

19. Q: What is the role of epidural steroid injections in back pain?
    A: First-line for all back pain
    B: Targeted treatment for radiculopathy — provides short-term relief (6–12 weeks) when conservative management insufficient; does not alter long-term outcomes
    C: Curative — permanently resolves disc herniations
    D: Only for non-radicular back pain
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Epidural steroid injections deliver corticosteroid adjacent to the affected nerve root. Provides short-term radicular pain relief — helps engage with rehabilitation. Not effective for non-radicular NSLBP. Does not change long-term outcomes. Indication: radiculopathy with inadequate response to conservative management.

20. Q: A 60-year-old male has back pain, haematuria, PSA 18 ng/mL, weight loss 6kg, night sweats. Most appropriate management?
    A: Reassure — common at this age
    B: Urgent investigation for prostate cancer with bone metastases — urgent urology referral; staging imaging; do NOT dismiss as musculoskeletal
    C: MRI lumbar spine for disc herniation
    D: Physiotherapy and NSAIDs
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Multiple red flags for malignancy — prostate cancer with bone metastases until proven otherwise. Prostate cancer is the most common cancer to metastasise to bone. Urgent urology referral, prostate biopsy, bone scan or whole body MRI for staging. Haematuria also requires urological investigation for bladder cancer.
