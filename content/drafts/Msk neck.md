# MSK — Neck — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Neck pain following trauma with neurological deficit — cervical spinal cord injury, call 000, immobilise
- Progressive myelopathy — bilateral hand clumsiness, gait disturbance, bladder dysfunction — urgent MRI and neurosurgical referral
- Neck pain with fever and difficulty swallowing — retropharyngeal abscess, emergency transfer
- Neck stiffness with fever and photophobia — meningitis, call 000
- New onset severe thunderclap headache — subarachnoid haemorrhage, call 000
- Rapidly expanding neck mass — lymphoma, abscess, vascular emergency
- Neck pain after MVA with altered consciousness — assume cervical fracture until excluded

### Common Neck Presentations

#### Mechanical Neck Pain
- Acute wry neck (torticollis) — muscle spasm, sudden onset on waking
- Cervical spondylosis — degenerative disc and facet joint disease
- Cervical disc herniation with radiculopathy
- Work-related or postural neck pain
- Whiplash associated disorder (WAD)

#### Referred and Neurological
- Cervical radiculopathy — arm pain, weakness, sensory loss
- Cervical myelopathy — spinal cord compression
- Thoracic outlet syndrome
- Tension headache referred from cervicogenic source
- Shoulder pathology referring to neck

### Key Conditions

#### Cervical Spondylosis
Degenerative changes in the cervical spine — disc degeneration, osteophyte formation, facet joint arthropathy. Most common in adults >40. Presents: neck pain, stiffness, reduced range of motion, headache. Often asymptomatic — incidental finding on imaging. Management: exercise, physiotherapy, NSAIDs. Imaging only if red flags or neurological deficit.

#### Cervical Radiculopathy
Nerve root compression — disc herniation or osteophyte at the neural foramen. Presents: neck pain radiating to the arm in a dermatomal pattern, with associated weakness, sensory changes, and reduced reflexes. C5–C6 and C6–C7 most common levels. Spurling's test reproduces radicular arm pain. Majority resolve conservatively in 6–12 weeks. MRI if persistent or neurological deficit. Surgical referral for progressive deficit or persistent pain >3 months.

#### Cervical Myelopathy
Spinal cord compression from cervical spondylosis or disc herniation. Serious — can cause permanent disability. Presents: bilateral hand clumsiness (difficulty with buttons, writing), gait disturbance (wide-based, unstable), lower limb spasticity, bladder dysfunction. Upper motor neurone signs: hyperreflexia, Babinski sign, clonus. Lhermitte's sign (electric shock sensation down the spine on neck flexion). MRI cervical spine urgently. Neurosurgical referral — surgical decompression often required.

#### Whiplash Associated Disorder (WAD)
Acceleration-deceleration injury to the neck — most common in rear-end MVAs. WAD graded I–IV: Grade I (neck complaint, no physical signs), Grade II (neck complaint + musculoskeletal signs), Grade III (neck complaint + neurological signs), Grade IV (fracture or dislocation). Management: early active mobilisation is key — avoid collar (delays recovery), encourage normal movement, physiotherapy, NSAIDs. Address psychosocial factors (yellow flags). Prognosis: most Grade I–II resolve within 3 months with active management.

#### Acute Wry Neck (Torticollis)
Sudden onset neck pain and muscle spasm with head tilted to one side — typically on waking. Common in young adults. May be from disc or facet joint origin. Management: analgesia (NSAIDs + paracetamol), heat, gentle mobilisation, reassurance (usually resolves in days to weeks). Physiotherapy for persistent cases.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Neck Pain History
- Onset — sudden vs gradual; associated trauma; waking vs activity
- Location — posterior neck, lateral, radiating to arm or head
- Character — dull aching (mechanical), sharp shooting (radicular), burning (neuropathic)
- Radiation — arm pain in dermatomal pattern suggests radiculopathy
- Duration — acute, subacute, chronic
- Mechanism — trauma (MVA, fall), occupation, posture, sport

#### Red Flag Screen
- Trauma — MVA, fall from height
- Fever, weight loss, night sweats — infection, malignancy
- History of malignancy
- Progressive neurological deficit — bilateral limb symptoms
- Bladder or bowel dysfunction
- Signs of myelopathy — clumsiness, gait disturbance
- Thunderclap headache onset — SAH

#### Radiculopathy Screen
- Arm pain, numbness, tingling — which distribution?
- Weakness — grip, shoulder abduction
- Symptom worsening — with extension, rotation toward affected side (Spurling's manoeuvre)

### Neck Examination

#### Inspection and Range of Motion
- Posture, head tilt, muscle asymmetry
- Flexion, extension, lateral flexion, rotation — assess what limits motion (pain, spasm, bony block)

#### Palpation
- Cervical spinous processes — midline tenderness
- Paravertebral muscles — spasm, trigger points
- Lymph nodes — cervical, supraclavicular

#### Neurological Examination — Cervical Levels
| Level | Weakness | Sensory | Reflex |
|-------|----------|---------|--------|
| C5 | Shoulder abduction, elbow flexion | Lateral arm | Biceps |
| C6 | Wrist extension, elbow flexion | Thumb, index | Brachioradialis |
| C7 | Elbow extension, wrist flexion | Middle finger | Triceps |
| C8 | Finger flexion, intrinsics | Ring, little finger | None |
| T1 | Hand intrinsics (interossei) | Inner forearm | None |

#### Special Tests
- Spurling's Test: axial compression + extension + lateral rotation toward affected side — positive if radicular arm pain reproduced
- Distraction Test: manual traction relieves radicular symptoms — positive
- Lhermitte's Sign: electric shock down spine on neck flexion — suggests myelopathy or MS
- Upper limb tension test: neural tension — similar to SLR for the arm
- Hoffman's Sign: flick the middle finger — flexion of thumb and index = UMN sign (myelopathy)

#### Investigations
- X-ray cervical spine: trauma assessment, spondylosis. NOT first-line for mechanical neck pain.
- MRI cervical spine: gold standard — disc herniation, stenosis, myelopathy, cord signal change. Order for: neurological deficit, myelopathy features, red flags, persistent radiculopathy >6 weeks.
- CT cervical spine: bony detail — fractures, trauma. NEXUS or Canadian C-Spine Rule for post-trauma imaging decision.
- FBE, ESR, CRP: infection or inflammatory arthritis
- EMG/nerve conduction studies: confirm radiculopathy vs peripheral neuropathy

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 48-year-old with Right C6 Radiculopathy

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 48-year-old female with 6 weeks of right neck and arm pain.

History: Insidious onset right-sided neck pain progressing to pain radiating down the right arm to the thumb and index finger. Burning and aching quality, 6/10. Paraesthesia (tingling) thumb and index finger. Some weakness noticed — difficulty opening jars (grip). Worsens with looking up and turning head to the right. Improves slightly with arm elevated above head. No bilateral symptoms, no gait problems, no bladder/bowel dysfunction. No trauma. Works at a desk — prolonged computer use.

**O — Objective**
Cervical ROM: Extension and right lateral rotation restricted and reproduce right arm pain. Flexion and left rotation normal.

Neurological right upper limb:
- Wrist extension: 4/5 (mild weakness)
- Elbow flexion: 5/5
- Grip: slightly reduced right
- Sensation: reduced light touch thumb and index finger right
- Brachioradialis reflex: reduced right; biceps reflex normal; triceps normal

Spurling's Test: positive right — reproduces right arm tingling to thumb.
Distraction Test: positive — relief of arm symptoms with manual traction.
Hoffman's sign: negative bilaterally (no myelopathy features).

**A — Assessment**
1. Right C6 radiculopathy — clinical features consistent with C5–C6 disc herniation or osteophyte compressing C6 nerve root
2. No myelopathy features
3. Occupational postural contributor — prolonged computer use

**P — Plan**
- MRI cervical spine — confirm level and severity of compression; guide management decision
- NSAIDs: naproxen 500mg BD with food for 2 weeks; paracetamol 1g QID
- Physiotherapy: cervical traction (if MRI confirms disc herniation at C5–C6), neural mobilisation, postural correction, ergonomic assessment
- Workstation ergonomics review — refer to occupational health
- Cervical collar NOT recommended — does not aid recovery
- Surgical referral if: no improvement at 6–12 weeks, progressive weakness, significant functional limitation
- Safety netting: return urgently if bilateral arm symptoms, leg weakness, gait disturbance, bladder dysfunction (myelopathy)

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 55-year-old presents with bilateral hand clumsiness, difficulty buttoning shirts, and unsteady gait. Cervical spine X-ray shows multilevel spondylosis. Hoffman's sign is positive bilaterally. What is the most likely diagnosis and management?
   A: Carpal tunnel syndrome bilaterally — nerve conduction studies and splints
   B: Cervical myelopathy — urgent MRI cervical spine; neurosurgical referral for surgical decompression
   C: Peripheral neuropathy — nerve conduction studies
   D: Essential tremor — propranolol
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Bilateral hand clumsiness, gait disturbance, and positive Hoffman's sign (UMN sign) in the context of cervical spondylosis indicates cervical myelopathy — spinal cord compression. This is a serious condition that can lead to permanent disability without treatment. Urgent MRI cervical spine to assess cord compression and signal change. Neurosurgical referral — surgical decompression (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, or posterior laminoplasty) prevents further deterioration. Do not manage as peripheral neuropathy — the UMN signs distinguish myelopathy.

2. Q: Which cervical nerve root level is associated with reduced brachioradialis reflex and sensory loss over the thumb and index finger?
   A: C5 — lateral arm, biceps reflex
   B: C6 — thumb and index finger, brachioradialis reflex
   C: C7 — middle finger, triceps reflex
   D: C8 — ring and little finger, no reflex
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Cervical radiculopathy localisation: C5 — shoulder abduction weakness, lateral arm sensory loss, reduced biceps reflex; C6 — wrist extension weakness, thumb and index finger sensory loss, reduced brachioradialis reflex; C7 — elbow extension weakness, middle finger sensory loss, reduced triceps reflex; C8 — finger flexion and intrinsic hand muscle weakness, ring and little finger sensory loss. Memorising this table is essential for NPs assessing cervical radiculopathy.

3. Q: Spurling's test is performed on a patient with right neck and arm pain. The test is positive. What does this indicate?
   A: Cervical myelopathy — immediate surgical referral
   B: Cervical nerve root compression on the right side — supports diagnosis of cervical radiculopathy; further investigation with MRI if neurological deficit or not improving
   C: Meningism — lumbar puncture required
   D: Temporomandibular joint dysfunction
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Spurling's test: axial compression + extension + lateral rotation toward the symptomatic side — narrows the neural foramen, compressing the nerve root. Positive result: reproduction of radicular arm pain (in a dermatomal distribution). Sensitivity approximately 50%, specificity approximately 90% — highly specific for cervical nerve root compression. A positive Spurling's test combined with radicular symptoms and neurological examination findings strongly supports cervical radiculopathy.

4. Q: A patient involved in a rear-end MVA presents with neck pain but no neurological symptoms. Vitals are stable. Which assessment tool guides the decision to obtain cervical spine X-rays?
   A: X-ray all patients with post-trauma neck pain regardless of clinical findings
   B: Canadian C-Spine Rule or NEXUS criteria — if low-risk criteria met, imaging can be safely avoided
   C: MRI is always required after any MVA
   D: CT head only — cervical spine X-ray not useful
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The Canadian C-Spine Rule and NEXUS criteria are validated tools to safely identify patients who do NOT need cervical imaging after trauma. Canadian C-Spine Rule: high-risk factors (age ≥65, dangerous mechanism, paraesthesia in extremities) require imaging; if none, low-risk factors are assessed (simple rear-end MVA, sitting in ED, ambulatory, delayed onset neck pain, no midline C-spine tenderness) — if any low-risk factor present and able to actively rotate neck 45° bilaterally, imaging can be avoided. NEXUS: imaging not required if 5 criteria met (no posterior midline tenderness, no intoxication, alert, no focal neurological deficit, no painful distracting injury).

5. Q: A 35-year-old wakes with acute neck pain and head tilted to the left — unable to straighten the head. No trauma. No neurological symptoms. What is the diagnosis and management?
   A: Meningitis — lumbar puncture urgently
   B: Acute wry neck (torticollis) — analgesia (NSAIDs + paracetamol), heat, gentle mobilisation, reassurance (usually resolves in days to weeks)
   C: Cervical fracture — immobilise and emergency transfer
   D: Cervical myelopathy — urgent MRI
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Acute wry neck (acute torticollis) — sudden onset neck pain and muscle spasm with head held in a tilted position, typically on waking in young adults. No trauma, no neurological symptoms. Caused by minor disc or facet joint irritation. Self-limiting — usually resolves in days to weeks. Management: analgesia, heat packs, gentle progressive mobilisation, reassurance. Cervical collar delays recovery — avoid. Physiotherapy for persistent cases. Distinguish from meningism (fever, photophobia, severe headache) and fracture (significant trauma history).

6. Q: A 28-year-old was rear-ended 4 weeks ago and has ongoing neck pain with Grade II WAD. She has been wearing a cervical collar continuously. What is the most important management change?
   A: Continue collar — protects the spine during healing
   B: Remove the collar immediately — early active mobilisation is the evidence-based management for WAD; collar use delays recovery
   C: MRI to confirm injury severity before changing management
   D: Refer to neurosurgery — WAD always requires surgical assessment
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The most important evidence-based change in WAD management: early active mobilisation, NOT immobilisation. Cervical collar use beyond the first few days is associated with worse outcomes — prolonged collar use leads to deconditioning, fear-avoidance, and chronicity. Management of WAD Grade I–II: early active mobilisation (from day 1), reassurance of expected recovery, physiotherapy, NSAIDs for short-term pain relief, address psychosocial yellow flags. Prognosis is good for Grade I–II with active management — most resolve within 3 months.

7. Q: Which finding in a neck pain patient is MOST consistent with cervical myelopathy rather than radiculopathy?
   A: Unilateral arm pain in a C6 dermatomal distribution
   B: Bilateral hand weakness and spasticity, hyperreflexia in the lower limbs, and Lhermitte's sign
   C: Reduced biceps reflex on one side
   D: Positive Spurling's test on the right
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Distinguishing myelopathy from radiculopathy: Radiculopathy — unilateral arm pain, weakness, sensory loss in a single dermatomal distribution, reduced ipsilateral reflex (LMN signs). Myelopathy — BILATERAL symptoms, UMN signs (hyperreflexia, spasticity, Babinski, Hoffman's, clonus), Lhermitte's sign (electric shock on neck flexion), gait disturbance, bladder dysfunction. Myelopathy involves the spinal cord itself (not just a nerve root) — it is a more serious diagnosis requiring urgent MRI and often surgical intervention.

8. Q: A 40-year-old has cervical radiculopathy with right C7 nerve root compression confirmed on MRI. Conservative management has been optimised for 10 weeks with physiotherapy and NSAIDs. Symptoms persist and there is progressive weakness in right elbow extension. What is the next step?
   A: Continue conservative management for another 6 months
   B: Refer to neurosurgery for anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) — progressive neurological deficit is an indication for surgical intervention
   C: Repeat MRI in 3 months before considering surgery
   D: Epidural steroid injection as definitive treatment
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Indications for surgical referral in cervical radiculopathy: failure of 6–12 weeks conservative management, progressive neurological deficit (worsening weakness), intolerable pain, or myelopathy (emergency). Progressive weakness in elbow extension (C7) after 10 weeks of adequate conservative management is a clear indication for surgical referral. ACDF (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion) or ACDR (disc replacement) are effective surgical options with good outcomes for cervical radiculopathy. Do not delay surgical referral for progressive neurological deficit.

9. Q: A 50-year-old presents with neck pain and headache. The headache is unilateral, occipital, and radiates to the frontal area. It is worsened by neck movement and improved by cervical spine manual therapy. What type of headache is this?
   A: Migraine — sumatriptan
   B: Cervicogenic headache — originating from the cervical spine structures; physiotherapy (manual therapy and exercise) is first-line; cervical nerve blocks for refractory cases
   C: Cluster headache — oxygen and sumatriptan
   D: Subarachnoid haemorrhage — CT head urgently
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Cervicogenic headache: unilateral headache (same side as neck pain), provoked or worsened by neck movement or sustained postures, reduced cervical range of motion, confirmed response to cervical diagnostic block. Often confused with migraine — key distinction is that cervicogenic headache is consistently provoked by neck movement and often responds to cervical manual therapy. Management: physiotherapy (manual therapy + exercise has the best evidence), posture correction, addressing workstation ergonomics. Cervical nerve blocks (C2/C3 medial branch) for refractory cases. NSAIDs for acute pain.

10. Q: What is Lhermitte's sign and what does it indicate?
    A: Radicular arm pain on neck extension — cervical disc herniation
    B: An electric shock sensation radiating down the spine and into the limbs on forward neck flexion — indicates cervical cord pathology (myelopathy, MS, cervical spondylosis with cord compression)
    C: Facial pain on neck rotation — TMJ dysfunction
    D: Shoulder pain on neck abduction — rotator cuff pathology
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Lhermitte's sign (or phenomenon): patient bends the neck forward and experiences an electric shock or tingling sensation radiating down the spine into the arms and/or legs. It is a clinical sign of cervical cord dysfunction — not specific for a single diagnosis but indicates pathology within or affecting the spinal cord. Causes: cervical spondylotic myelopathy (most common in older adults), multiple sclerosis (most common in younger patients), radiation myelopathy, B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration). Urgent MRI cervical spine is required when Lhermitte's sign is present.

11. Q: A patient with chronic neck pain and bilateral tingling in both arms asks about the safety of cervical manipulation by a chiropractor. What should the NP advise?
    A: Cervical manipulation is completely safe — no risks
    B: Cervical manipulation (high-velocity thrust) carries a rare but serious risk of vertebral artery dissection causing stroke — bilateral neurological symptoms warrant MRI cervical spine before any manipulation; advise the patient to discuss with their treating team
    C: Chiropractic manipulation is the most effective treatment for cervical radiculopathy
    D: Cervical manipulation is contraindicated for all neck pain
    Correct: B
    Explanation: High-velocity cervical manipulation (rotation thrust) carries a rare but serious risk of vertebral artery dissection, which can cause posterior circulation stroke. Risk is estimated at approximately 1 in 1–3 million manipulations but the consequences can be devastating. In patients with bilateral arm symptoms suggesting possible myelopathy, vertebral artery testing, or any neurological deficit — MRI should be obtained before manipulation to exclude cord compression or vascular anomaly. Advise patients to inform their chiropractor or manual therapist of all neurological symptoms. Mobilisation (lower force techniques) has a better safety profile than high-velocity manipulation.

12. Q: Thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) can mimic cervical radiculopathy. Which feature favours TOS over cervical radiculopathy?
    A: Positive Spurling's test
    B: Symptoms provoked by overhead arm elevation or carrying heavy objects; vascular symptoms (swelling, colour change, pulse changes); positive Adson's or Wright's test — compression between clavicle and first rib or scalene muscles
    C: Reduced triceps reflex
    D: MRI showing disc herniation at C7–T1
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Thoracic outlet syndrome involves compression of the brachial plexus (neurogenic TOS — most common), subclavian artery (arterial TOS), or subclavian vein (venous TOS) between the clavicle, first rib, and scalene muscles. Features distinguishing TOS from cervical radiculopathy: symptoms provoked by overhead activities or arm elevation, vascular symptoms (arm swelling, pallor, colour change), positive provocative tests (Adson's — radial pulse obliterated on head rotation and breath hold; Wright's — symptoms reproduced with arm abducted 90° externally rotated). Neurogenic TOS involves the lower brachial plexus (C8–T1) — intrinsic hand muscle wasting and weakness. Normal MRI cervical spine. Management: physiotherapy, postural correction, scalene stretching; surgical first rib resection for refractory cases.

13. Q: A 65-year-old female with known rheumatoid arthritis presents with neck pain and new onset bilateral hand weakness, imbalance, and bladder urgency. What is the most urgent concern and investigation?
    A: Cervical radiculopathy — MRI elective
    B: Atlanto-axial subluxation causing cervical myelopathy — urgent MRI cervical spine including flexion-extension views; neurosurgical referral; avoid aggressive cervical manipulation
    C: Peripheral neuropathy — nerve conduction studies
    D: Stroke — CT brain
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Atlanto-axial (C1–C2) subluxation is a serious complication of rheumatoid arthritis — pannus formation and ligamentous laxity from synovitis can cause C1 to slip forward on C2, compressing the spinal cord. RA patients with neck pain should be assessed for myelopathy — particularly before any procedures requiring intubation or neck positioning. Presentation: myelopathy (bilateral weakness, gait disturbance, bladder dysfunction) ± occipital headache (C2 compression). Urgent MRI including dynamic views. Neurosurgical referral — posterior C1–C2 fusion may be required. All RA patients with significant neck symptoms need cervical spine imaging before anaesthesia.

14. Q: A 22-year-old male presents with neck stiffness, severe headache of sudden onset ("worst headache of my life"), and photophobia. Temperature 38.8°C. What is the most urgent diagnosis to exclude?
    A: Cervical muscle strain — analgesia
    B: Subarachnoid haemorrhage (sudden severe headache) and/or meningitis (fever, meningism) — call 000; both are medical emergencies; CT head before LP if SAH suspected
    C: Migraine — sumatriptan and dark room
    D: Acute wry neck — heat and NSAIDs
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Two emergencies must be considered: SAH — thunderclap headache (sudden severe "worst of life") requires CT head immediately (sensitive within 6 hours), then LP if CT negative (xanthochromia). Meningitis — fever + neck stiffness + photophobia = meningism. Both can coexist. Call 000. Do NOT perform LP before CT head if papilloedema, focal neurological deficit, or immunocompromise. Bacterial meningitis — do not wait for CT/LP before giving antibiotics if immediate threat to life (purpuric rash, rapid deterioration). Neck stiffness from cervical pathology: no fever, no photophobia, no headache of sudden onset — very different clinical picture.

15. Q: What is the recommended management for occupational neck pain related to prolonged computer use?
    A: Cervical collar during work hours
    B: Ergonomic workstation assessment and modification; regular breaks and movement; stretching exercises; physiotherapy; NSAIDs for acute pain; address posture and screen height
    C: Immediate surgical referral — work-related neck pain requires surgery
    D: Complete work cessation — return when pain free
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Occupational neck pain is common in desk workers. Management: ergonomic assessment (monitor at eye level, keyboard position, chair height, lumbar support), regular break every 30–60 minutes to move and stretch, specific cervical and thoracic mobility exercises, physiotherapy for acute episodes. NSAIDs short-term for acute pain. Cervical collar is not appropriate — delays recovery. Complete work cessation is rarely appropriate and risks chronicity through deconditioning. Work modification and early return to work with ergonomic adjustments is the evidence-based approach. Prolonged sitting with forward head posture increases cervical muscle load significantly.

16. Q: Which investigation is most sensitive for detecting early cervical cord signal change in myelopathy?
    A: Plain X-ray cervical spine
    B: MRI cervical spine with T2-weighted sequences — shows cord compression and hyperintense signal change (myelomalacia) indicating cord damage
    C: CT cervical spine
    D: EMG/nerve conduction studies
    Correct: B
    Explanation: MRI is the gold standard for cervical myelopathy assessment. T2-weighted MRI shows: degree of cord compression (narrowed canal, flattening of cord), hyperintense signal change within the cord (myelomalacia — indicates cord damage and worse prognosis), disc herniation and osteophyte formation, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. T2 hyperintensity correlates with severity of myelopathy and surgical urgency. CT shows bony changes well but cannot assess cord signal. Plain X-ray cannot assess the cord. EMG confirms peripheral neuropathy but cannot diagnose myelopathy.

17. Q: A physiotherapist requests a cervical spine X-ray before starting manipulation for a patient with RA and neck pain. Is this appropriate?
    A: No — X-rays are never needed before physiotherapy
    B: Yes — RA patients with neck pain require cervical imaging (X-ray or MRI) before manipulation to assess for atlanto-axial subluxation or significant instability that would make manipulation dangerous
    C: X-ray is sufficient — MRI not needed for RA neck pain
    D: Only if the patient requests it
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Atlanto-axial subluxation in RA can cause catastrophic neurological injury with cervical manipulation. Any RA patient with significant neck pain, new neurological symptoms, or who requires procedures involving neck positioning (intubation, manipulation) should have cervical imaging. X-ray (lateral in flexion-extension) assesses the atlanto-dental interval (ADI) — ADI >3mm in adults suggests subluxation. MRI provides additional information about cord compression. If significant instability is confirmed — cervical manipulation is absolutely contraindicated. This is a patient safety issue that the NP should actively communicate.

18. Q: A 45-year-old male presents with neck pain and bilateral hand intrinsic muscle wasting. His symptoms are worse at night and when the arms are above his head. Which diagnosis should be considered in addition to cervical pathology?
    A: Carpal tunnel syndrome only — most common cause of hand paresthesia
    B: Thoracic outlet syndrome (neurogenic) involving C8–T1 — intrinsic hand muscle wasting is characteristic; provocation with arm elevation; assess with Wright's test and Adson's test
    C: Multiple sclerosis
    D: Stroke
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Neurogenic TOS involves the lower brachial plexus (C8–T1) causing intrinsic hand muscle wasting (interossei, thenar, hypothenar), ulnar-sided hand and forearm sensory symptoms, and pain exacerbated by overhead arm elevation. Distinguishing from cervical C8–T1 radiculopathy: TOS symptoms are provoked by arm elevation (not neck movement), Spurling's test is negative, MRI cervical spine is normal, and provocative tests for TOS (Wright's, Adson's, EAST test) are positive. Both conditions can coexist — "double crush" phenomenon. EMG and nerve conduction studies may help differentiate. Management: physiotherapy, scalene stretching, postural correction; surgical first rib resection for refractory neurogenic TOS.

19. Q: A 32-year-old pregnant woman (28 weeks) presents with neck and right arm pain with C6 distribution symptoms. NSAIDs are contraindicated after 20 weeks. What analgesic options are safe?
    A: High-dose ibuprofen — safe throughout pregnancy
    B: Paracetamol — safe throughout pregnancy; avoid NSAIDs after 20 weeks (premature ductus arteriosus closure risk); physiotherapy, heat, cervical traction; short-term opioids (tramadol cautiously) if inadequate — discuss with obstetric team
    C: Aspirin 600mg TDS — safe in second trimester
    D: Codeine — completely safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Pain management in pregnancy: Paracetamol remains first-line throughout pregnancy. NSAIDs are contraindicated after 20 weeks gestation — risk of premature closure of the ductus arteriosus and oligohydramnios. Non-pharmacological: physiotherapy, cervical traction (carefully in pregnancy), heat, activity modification. Short-term opioids (tramadol — limited data, use cautiously): discuss with obstetric team. Avoid codeine in late pregnancy and breastfeeding — CYP2D6 ultrametabolisers can produce excessive morphine causing neonatal toxicity. Gabapentin and pregabalin — avoid in pregnancy (teratogenic risk). Always involve the obstetric team in pain management decisions during pregnancy.

20. Q: A patient with cervical myelopathy is scheduled for elective anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF). As the NP seeing the patient preoperatively, what are the key topics to cover in the pre-operative assessment and education?
    A: Confirm the diagnosis — this is the only pre-operative task
    B: Comprehensive pre-operative assessment: cardiac and respiratory risk, medication management (anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, diabetes medications on day of surgery), airway assessment (neck extension for intubation may be limited), consent understanding, post-operative expectations (collar use, mobilisation timeline, wound care), neurological baseline documentation, VTE prophylaxis plan, and discharge planning
    C: Anaesthetic review only — NP has no role in surgical pre-assessment
    D: Order post-operative medications only
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Pre-operative NP assessment for ACDF: Medical optimisation — cardiac risk (RCRI), respiratory function (smoking cessation 8+ weeks pre-surgery), BP control, glycaemic control. Medication management — anticoagulants (warfarin 5 days pre-op; DOACs 24–48 hours; antiplatelet agents per surgeon instruction), metformin (hold day of surgery), antihypertensives (continue most). Airway note — limited neck extension in myelopathy may complicate intubation; document and communicate to anaesthetics. Neurological baseline — document pre-operative neurological status for post-operative comparison. Patient education — collar use post-ACDF (6–12 weeks typically), activity restrictions, wound care, dysphagia (common post-ACDF — swallowing education). VTE prophylaxis — enoxaparin timing per surgeon.
