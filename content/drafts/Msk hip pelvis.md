# MSK — Hip & Pelvis — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Act Urgently
- Acute hip fracture — elderly patient, fall, unable to weight bear, shortened externally rotated leg — call 000
- Septic arthritis of the hip — fever, severe hip pain, refusal to weight bear — emergency transfer
- Avascular necrosis — acute severe hip pain in at-risk patient (steroids, alcohol, SCD) — urgent MRI
- Hip dislocation — after trauma or arthroplasty — emergency transfer
- Acute-on-chronic hip pain with weight loss or night pain in known malignancy — bone metastasis
- Pelvic fracture — high-energy trauma, haemodynamic instability — call 000

### Common Hip and Pelvis Presentations

#### Traumatic
- Hip fracture — neck of femur, intertrochanteric
- Hip dislocation — posterior (most common, MVA dashboard)
- Acetabular fracture
- Pelvic ring fracture

#### Degenerative and Inflammatory
- Hip osteoarthritis — most common cause of groin pain in adults >50
- Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) — young active adults
- Labral tear
- Rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis

#### Periarticular and Referred
- Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (GTPS) — lateral hip pain
- Iliopsoas bursitis/tendinopathy — anterior hip/groin
- Meralgia paraesthetica — lateral thigh numbness
- Referred lumbar spine — L3 root, hip distribution
- Pubic symphysis dysfunction — pelvic girdle pain in pregnancy

### Key Conditions

#### Hip Osteoarthritis
Most common cause of hip pain in adults over 50. Groin-predominant pain radiating to the anterior thigh and knee. Activity-related, morning stiffness <30 minutes, reduced internal rotation and flexion (early signs). FADIR test (Flexion, ADduction, Internal Rotation) — positive in hip OA and FAI. Trendelenburg sign — gluteus medius weakness. X-ray: superior joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis. Management: exercise (land and water-based), weight loss, NSAIDs, physiotherapy. Total hip replacement (THR) for severe refractory OA — excellent outcomes.

#### Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome (GTPS)
Previously called trochanteric bursitis — now recognised as primarily gluteal tendinopathy. Lateral hip pain over the greater trochanter, worse with stairs, lying on affected side, and prolonged standing. Predominantly affects middle-aged women. Positive "FADER" test. Avoid postures that load the tendon (crossing legs, hip adduction). Management: education, load modification, gluteal strengthening (avoid hip adduction exercises initially), physiotherapy. Corticosteroid injection for short-term relief — not long-term solution.

#### Femoroacetabular Impingement (FAI)
Abnormal contact between the femoral head and acetabulum — cam type (aspherical femoral head), pincer type (overcoverage of acetabulum), or mixed. Young active patients. Deep groin/anterior hip pain, worse with prolonged sitting, hip flexion activities, pivoting. FADIR test positive. MRI arthrogram for labral tear assessment. Management: physiotherapy (activity modification, movement control), analgesia. Surgical arthroscopy for labral tear repair or cam/pincer correction in appropriate patients.

#### Hip Fracture
Significant cause of morbidity and mortality in elderly. Fall is the precipitant — low-energy in osteoporotic bone. Types: intracapsular (neck of femur — at risk of avascular necrosis), extracapsular (intertrochanteric, subtrochanteric). Clinical signs: shortened, externally rotated leg, inability to weight bear, groin pain. X-ray confirms in most cases — MRI if X-ray negative but high suspicion (occult fracture). Surgical fixation (hemi- or total arthroplasty for displaced intracapsular, dynamic hip screw for extracapsular) within 24–48 hours. Orthogeriatric co-management improves outcomes.

#### Meralgia Paraesthetica
Entrapment neuropathy of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN). Burning, tingling, numbness over the anterolateral thigh — no hip joint involvement, no weakness. Common in: obesity, pregnancy, tight clothing, prolonged hip flexion. Inguinal ligament tenderness at the ASIS. Management: address precipitating factors (weight loss, loose clothing), reassurance (self-limiting in most). Corticosteroid injection at the ASIS for refractory cases. Surgery (decompression) rarely required.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Hip History
- Location — groin (intra-articular: OA, FAI, labral tear), lateral hip (GTPS), anterior (iliopsoas, FAI), buttock (referred lumbar, SI joint, piriformis)
- Onset — acute (fracture, dislocation) vs gradual (OA, FAI)
- Aggravating factors — sitting (FAI, labral tear), walking (OA), lying on hip (GTPS), stairs
- Limp — antalgic (pain) vs Trendelenburg (gluteus medius weakness)
- Weight bearing — inability suggests fracture or septic arthritis

### Hip Examination

#### Inspection
- Gait — antalgic, Trendelenburg, short leg
- Alignment — leg length discrepancy, pelvic tilt

#### Range of Motion
- Flexion (normal 120°), extension (20°), abduction (45°), adduction (30°), internal/external rotation
- Loss of internal rotation — early OA sign
- Capsular pattern: flexion > abduction > internal rotation most limited

#### Special Tests
- FADIR (Flexion, ADduction, Internal Rotation): hip impingement — OA, FAI — positive = groin pain reproduced
- FABER (Flexion, ABduction, External Rotation): SI joint pain, hip pathology — positive = groin or SI pain
- Trendelenburg Test: single leg stance — pelvis drops on opposite side = gluteus medius weakness
- Thomas Test: hip flexor tightness — flatten lumbar spine, assess opposite hip extension
- GTPS Tests: tenderness over greater trochanter, pain with resisted abduction (gluteal tendon)

#### Investigations
- X-ray hip (AP pelvis + lateral): first-line — OA (joint space narrowing), fracture, FAI morphology, Paget's
- MRI hip: soft tissue (labral tear, avascular necrosis, occult fracture, GTPS, gluteal tendinopathy)
- Ultrasound: GTPS, bursitis, tendinopathy, guided injection
- CT: complex fractures, FAI surgical planning
- FBE, CRP, ESR: septic arthritis, inflammatory arthritis
- Bone density (DEXA): after fragility fracture

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 68-year-old with Right Hip OA

**S — Subjective**
68-year-old female with 18 months of right groin and anterior thigh pain. Activity-related — worsens with prolonged walking (>500m) and climbing stairs. Morning stiffness 15–20 minutes. Notices right leg feels stiff after sitting. Difficulty putting on shoes and socks. No night pain at rest. BMI 31. Has tried paracetamol with partial relief. No previous physiotherapy.

**O — Objective**
Antalgic gait favouring right. Reduced ROM right hip: flexion 90° (left 120°), internal rotation 10° (left 35°), abduction 25° (left 40°). FADIR test: positive right — groin pain reproduced. FABER: negative. Trendelenburg: mildly positive right (gluteus medius weakness).

X-ray right hip: superior joint space narrowing 2mm, moderate osteophytes medially, mild subchondral sclerosis. Consistent with moderate OA.

**A — Assessment**
Right hip OA — moderate severity. Significant functional limitation. Inadequate current management.

**P — Plan**
- Physiotherapy referral — hip strengthening program (gluteals, hip abductors, quadriceps); GLA:D program if available
- Weight loss — target 5–10% reduction; refer dietitian
- NSAIDs: naproxen 500mg BD with food for 4 weeks (recheck renal function and BP — elderly patient)
- Paracetamol 1g QID regular — continue
- Orthopaedic referral for THR assessment — moderate-severe OA with significant functional limitation
- Education: OA management, activity pacing, appropriate footwear

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: An 80-year-old woman falls at home and is unable to weight bear. Her right leg is shortened and externally rotated. What is the most likely diagnosis and immediate management?
   A: Hip OA flare — analgesia and physiotherapy
   B: Hip fracture (neck of femur) — call 000; immobilise; analgesia; emergency transfer for surgical fixation within 24–48 hours
   C: Hip dislocation — manual reduction in clinic
   D: Lumbar disc herniation — MRI spine
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Shortened, externally rotated leg with inability to weight bear after a fall in an elderly patient is a hip fracture until proven otherwise. Call 000. Analgesia (IV morphine or nerve block in ED). X-ray confirms — AP pelvis and lateral hip. MRI if X-ray negative but clinical suspicion high (occult fracture). Surgical fixation within 24–48 hours (intracapsular = hemiarthroplasty or THR; extracapsular = dynamic hip screw). Orthogeriatric co-management, DVT prophylaxis, early mobilisation post-surgery improve outcomes and reduce mortality.

2. Q: A 45-year-old female runner has lateral hip pain directly over the greater trochanter, worse with stairs and lying on that side. Tenderness on palpation of the greater trochanter. What is the diagnosis and management?
   A: Hip OA — X-ray and physiotherapy
   B: Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (GTPS/gluteal tendinopathy) — load modification (avoid hip adduction/crossing legs), gluteal strengthening, physiotherapy; corticosteroid injection for short-term relief
   C: Iliotibial band syndrome — ITB stretching
   D: Meralgia paraesthetica — lateral LFCN entrapment
   Correct: B
   Explanation: GTPS — lateral hip pain over the greater trochanter, worse with stairs and lying on the side. Now understood to be primarily gluteal tendinopathy (not primarily bursitis). Compressive loading of the gluteal tendons (crossing legs, hip adduction, prolonged side-lying) worsens symptoms. Management: education (avoid compressive positions), load modification, gluteal tendon loading program (progressive resistance), physiotherapy. Corticosteroid injection provides short-term relief but not superior long-term. Shockwave therapy for refractory cases. Common in perimenopausal women — oestrogen decline affects tendon biology.

3. Q: A 30-year-old male soccer player has deep groin pain worse with prolonged sitting and pivoting. FADIR test is positive. MRI arthrogram shows an anterior labral tear. Diagnosis and management options?
   A: Hip OA — TKR planning
   B: Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) with labral tear — physiotherapy and activity modification first-line; hip arthroscopy for labral repair/debridement and cam/pincer correction if conservative management fails
   C: Groin strain — ice and rest
   D: Meralgia paraesthetica — LFCN injection
   Correct: B
   Explanation: FAI with labral tear — young active male, deep groin pain worse with sitting and pivoting, positive FADIR, MRI arthrogram confirms labral tear. Management: physiotherapy first (activity modification, core and hip strengthening, movement control) for 3–6 months. If inadequate response and significant labral tear — hip arthroscopy for labral repair and cam/pincer correction. Outcomes of arthroscopy for FAI are generally good in appropriately selected patients. FAI is the most common cause of hip pathology in young active adults.

4. Q: A 55-year-old female has burning, tingling numbness over the anterolateral thigh — no hip joint pain, no weakness, no back pain. Tenderness at the ASIS. What is the diagnosis?
   A: L3 radiculopathy — MRI lumbar spine
   B: Meralgia paraesthetica — LFCN entrapment at the inguinal ligament; address precipitating factors; most cases self-limiting
   C: Hip OA — FADIR test and X-ray
   D: DVT — ultrasound legs
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Meralgia paraesthetica — lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) entrapment at the inguinal ligament. Pure sensory symptoms (burning, tingling, numbness) over the anterolateral thigh — no motor deficit, no joint involvement. Tenderness at the ASIS where the LFCN passes under the inguinal ligament. Causes: obesity, pregnancy, tight belts, prolonged hip flexion. Management: address precipitating factors, reassurance (self-limiting in most within months), corticosteroid injection at ASIS for refractory cases. LFCN is a pure sensory nerve — weakness excludes this diagnosis.

5. Q: X-ray of the hip in OA characteristically shows which pattern of joint space narrowing?
   A: Diffuse concentric narrowing — same as rheumatoid arthritis
   B: Superior (superolateral) joint space narrowing — the area of greatest load bearing; this pattern distinguishes OA from RA (concentric) and avascular necrosis
   C: Inferior narrowing only
   D: Medial narrowing only
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Hip OA characteristically shows superior (superolateral) joint space narrowing — the area of greatest mechanical load. Additional X-ray features: osteophytes (medial "teardrop" and superolateral margin), subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cysts. RA shows concentric (global) joint space narrowing. Avascular necrosis shows crescent sign (subchondral fracture) and collapse of the femoral head. Identifying the pattern of joint space narrowing on X-ray helps differentiate the cause of hip pain.

6. Q: After total hip replacement (THR), a patient develops sudden severe hip pain at 6 weeks after bending to pick up an object. The hip appears shortened and internally rotated. What is the most likely complication?
   A: Periprosthetic infection
   B: Hip dislocation — emergency transfer for closed or open reduction
   C: Femoral stem loosening
   D: DVT
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Post-THR hip dislocation — typically posterior, occurring with hip flexion + internal rotation + adduction (precaution movements taught pre-operatively). Signs: sudden severe pain, shortened and internally rotated limb (posterior dislocation). Emergency transfer for reduction under anaesthesia (closed reduction). If irreducible — open reduction. Recurrent dislocators may require revision surgery (constrained liner, dual mobility cup). THR dislocation precautions: avoid hip flexion >90°, avoid internal rotation, avoid crossing legs — taught to all THR patients pre-operatively and for 6–12 weeks post-op.

7. Q: What is the Trendelenburg test and what does a positive result indicate?
   A: Internal rotation of the hip on passive movement — hip OA
   B: Single-leg stance test — pelvis drops on the non-stance side = weakness of the gluteus medius on the stance side; seen in hip OA, GTPS, hip fracture, superior gluteal nerve palsy
   C: Reproduction of groin pain on hip flexion — FAI
   D: Lateral thigh numbness — meralgia paraesthetica
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Trendelenburg test: patient stands on one leg — observe the pelvis. Normal: pelvis remains level or rises slightly on the non-stance side (gluteus medius on the stance side holds the pelvis level). Positive (Trendelenburg sign): pelvis drops on the non-stance side — indicates gluteus medius weakness on the stance side. Causes: hip OA (pain inhibition of gluteus medius), GTPS (gluteal tendinopathy), hip fracture, superior gluteal nerve palsy, leg length discrepancy. A positive Trendelenburg is often visible as a lateral trunk lean (Trendelenburg gait) — the patient leans over the affected hip to reduce the mechanical demand on the gluteus medius.

8. Q: A 72-year-old male on long-term corticosteroids for COPD develops severe right hip pain acutely. X-ray shows no fracture. MRI shows subchondral crescentic signal change in the right femoral head. What is the diagnosis?
   A: Hip OA — physiotherapy
   B: Avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head — urgent orthopaedic referral; management depends on stage — early (pre-collapse): core decompression; late (collapse): THR
   C: Septic arthritis — aspiration and antibiotics
   D: Stress fracture — activity modification
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head — subchondral crescent sign on MRI indicates subchondral fracture (pre-collapse). Risk factors: corticosteroids (most common), alcohol excess, SCD, radiation, dysbaric osteonecrosis (divers), trauma. Early AVN — pre-collapse: core decompression (drilling to relieve intraosseous pressure) may prevent progression. Once collapse occurs — THR is required. X-ray is often normal in early AVN — MRI is the gold standard for early diagnosis. Screen high-risk patients (on long-term corticosteroids) with MRI if hip pain develops.

9. Q: Pelvic girdle pain (PGP) affects many pregnant women. What is the most common site of pain and first-line management?
   A: Lumbar spine — bed rest
   B: Sacroiliac joints and/or pubic symphysis — physiotherapy (pelvic stabilisation exercises, education, pelvic support belt), activity modification, analgesia (paracetamol)
   C: Greater trochanter — corticosteroid injection
   D: Hip joint — X-ray and orthopaedic referral
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Pelvic girdle pain (PGP) in pregnancy — pain over the sacroiliac joints (posterior) and/or pubic symphysis (anterior), often radiating to the buttock and posterior thigh. Caused by relaxin-mediated ligamentous laxity and altered load distribution. Positive ASLR (Active Straight Leg Raise) test — unable to raise leg off plinth without significant difficulty. Management: physiotherapy specialising in women's health/pelvic floor (pelvic stabilisation, core stability), pelvic support belt, activity modification (avoid asymmetric loading — stairs one step at a time), paracetamol. Avoid NSAIDs after 20 weeks. Most resolves postpartum — referral for persistent cases.

10. Q: Which statement correctly describes the difference between intracapsular and extracapsular hip fractures?
    A: They are managed identically — dynamic hip screw for all
    B: Intracapsular (neck of femur) fractures risk avascular necrosis of the femoral head due to disruption of the femoral head blood supply — displaced fractures treated with arthroplasty (hemi or total); extracapsular (intertrochanteric) fractures have intact blood supply — treated with dynamic hip screw or intramedullary nail
    C: Extracapsular fractures are more serious and require arthroplasty
    D: Intracapsular fractures always heal without surgery
    Correct: B
    Explanation: The blood supply to the femoral head (medial femoral circumflex artery) runs along the femoral neck — intracapsular fractures (subcapital, transcervical) disrupt this supply, risking AVN of the femoral head. Displaced intracapsular fractures in elderly patients: hemiarthroplasty (replaces femoral head only) or THR (replaces both surfaces). Undisplaced intracapsular: internal fixation (cannulated screws) — lower AVN risk. Extracapsular fractures (intertrochanteric, subtrochanteric): blood supply intact — treated with internal fixation (dynamic hip screw or intramedullary nail). Understanding this distinction is important for NPs managing post-operative hip fracture patients.

11. Q: A 55-year-old female presents with right groin and anterior thigh pain radiating to the knee. Hip examination shows FADIR positive. Lumbar spine examination is normal. What investigation is most useful?
    A: MRI lumbar spine — referred pain most likely
    B: X-ray hip (AP pelvis and lateral) — first-line for hip pain; then MRI hip if X-ray inconclusive
    C: Ultrasound knee — pain may originate there
    D: EMG — radiculopathy assessment
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Groin pain radiating to the anterior thigh and knee with positive FADIR and normal lumbar examination suggests intra-articular hip pathology. X-ray hip (AP pelvis + lateral frog-leg) is first-line — shows OA, FAI morphology, fracture, Paget's. If X-ray normal or inconclusive — MRI hip for labral tear, AVN, occult fracture, stress fracture, cartilage damage. Hip pain commonly refers to the thigh and knee (L3 distribution) — always examine the hip in patients with knee pain (especially medial knee pain) as referred hip pain is commonly misattributed to the knee. The FADIR test distinguishes intra-articular hip pathology from spinal referred pain.

12. Q: An elite 20-year-old female gymnast has groin pain and limited hip flexion. X-ray shows a cam deformity (aspherical femoral head-neck junction). What is the most likely diagnosis and what additional finding on MRI would confirm significant pathology?
    A: Hip OA — joint space narrowing on X-ray
    B: Cam-type FAI — MRI arthrogram finding of anterior labral tear at the chondrolabral junction confirms the impingement mechanism is causing significant pathology warranting consideration of surgery
    C: Stress fracture — MRI shows bone marrow oedema
    D: Iliopsoas tendinopathy — MRI shows tendon thickening
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Cam FAI — aspherical femoral head-neck junction (reduced alpha angle) causes jamming against the acetabulum during hip flexion and internal rotation. Young active patients. MRI arthrogram confirms: anterior superior labral tear (most common location), chondrolabral junction damage, acetabular cartilage injury. Alpha angle >55° on MRI confirms cam morphology. Conservative management (physiotherapy) is first-line. Surgical arthroscopy for labral repair and cam correction (femoroplasty) when conservative management fails — good outcomes in appropriately selected patients, particularly those without significant OA.

13. Q: A 65-year-old male with known prostate cancer presents with acute severe left hip pain and inability to weight bear. X-ray shows a lytic lesion in the femoral neck. What is the diagnosis and management?
    A: Hip OA — physiotherapy and NSAIDs
    B: Pathological fracture from bone metastasis — do NOT attempt to weight bear; emergency orthopaedic referral for prophylactic fixation or arthroplasty; pain management; oncology referral for systemic treatment and radiotherapy
    C: Stress fracture — walking boot
    D: AVN — core decompression
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Lytic lesion in the femoral neck with acute severe pain in a patient with known prostate cancer (which commonly metastasises to bone) is a pathological fracture or impending pathological fracture. Do NOT allow weight bearing — fracture through the femoral neck is likely. Emergency orthopaedic referral: prophylactic intramedullary nail or arthroplasty before fracture completes. Radiotherapy post-fixation for local disease control. Oncology referral for systemic treatment (hormone therapy, chemotherapy, bone-targeted agents — zoledronic acid or denosumab). Bone scan or whole-body imaging to assess other skeletal metastases.

14. Q: What are the standard post-operative hip precautions after posterior approach total hip replacement?
    A: No restrictions — patient can move freely from day 1
    B: Avoid hip flexion >90°, avoid internal rotation of the hip, avoid crossing the legs — typically for 6–12 weeks post-operatively to prevent posterior dislocation
    C: Non-weight bearing for 3 months
    D: Avoid hip extension and external rotation — risk of anterior dislocation
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Posterior approach THR precautions (to prevent posterior dislocation): Avoid hip flexion >90° (no sitting in low chairs, no bending to put on shoes — use long-handled aids), avoid internal rotation of the hip, avoid crossing the legs/knees. These precautions are maintained for 6–12 weeks while the posterior capsule and short external rotators heal. NPs in post-operative follow-up must confirm patients understand and are complying with these precautions. Some surgeons using anterior or anterolateral approaches may have different or fewer restrictions — always clarify with the operating surgeon. Occupational therapy is essential for home assessment and adaptive equipment.

15. Q: A 40-year-old male is admitted after a high-speed MVA with a posterior hip dislocation. The femoral head is displaced posteriorly out of the acetabulum. What is the most time-critical concern?
    A: DVT — anticoagulation immediately
    B: Avascular necrosis risk — closed reduction within 6 hours reduces AVN risk; urgent orthopaedic review; check sciatic nerve function (commonly injured in posterior dislocation)
    C: Hip fracture — dynamic hip screw
    D: Ligamentous injury — MRI
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Hip dislocation (posterior is most common — dashboard injury) requires urgent reduction. The longer the femoral head remains dislocated, the higher the risk of AVN of the femoral head — reduction within 6 hours significantly reduces this risk. Closed reduction under anaesthesia (Allis manoeuvre) is standard. Check sciatic nerve function before and after reduction — the sciatic nerve is adjacent and commonly stretched or injured in posterior dislocation (foot drop, sensory loss). Post-reduction: CT hip to exclude associated acetabular fracture, MRI for labral/cartilage assessment. Protected weight bearing post-reduction.

16. Q: What is the FABER test (Patrick's test) and what conditions does it assess?
    A: Assessment of hip flexor strength
    B: Flexion, Abduction, and External Rotation of the hip in supine — positive (groin or SI joint pain reproduced) suggests hip intra-articular pathology (groin pain) or sacroiliac joint pathology (posterior SI pain) — also stretches the piriformis
    C: Assessment of ACL integrity
    D: Test for meralgia paraesthetica
    Correct: B
    Explanation: FABER test (Patrick's test): patient supine, hip and knee flexed, foot placed on opposite knee (figure-4 position). Examiner applies gentle downward pressure on the knee. Positive results: Groin pain — suggests intra-articular hip pathology (OA, labral tear, FAI); Posterior SI joint pain — suggests sacroiliac joint dysfunction; Anterior inguinal pain — iliopsoas pathology. The test also stretches the piriformis — posterior buttock pain may indicate piriformis syndrome. Compare bilaterally. FABER combined with FADIR is a useful screening battery for hip pathology.

17. Q: An 85-year-old male with hip OA and multiple comorbidities (cardiac failure, CKD eGFR 35, anticoagulated for AF) needs analgesia for hip pain. NSAIDs are contraindicated. What are the safest analgesic options?
    A: High-dose ibuprofen — most effective for OA pain
    B: Paracetamol 1g QID (safe in CKD and cardiac failure — hepatotoxicity concern only with overdose); topical diclofenac (minimal systemic absorption); low-dose opioids (cautiously) if adequate analgesia not achieved; duloxetine for chronic pain
    C: Celecoxib — safer than non-selective NSAIDs in CKD
    D: Aspirin — analgesic doses safe in CKD
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Analgesic selection in complex comorbid elderly patients: Avoid NSAIDs (all — including celecoxib) with CKD eGFR <30–45 and cardiac failure — fluid retention, AKI, cardiovascular events. Paracetamol: safe in cardiac failure and CKD (renally excreted metabolites at eGFR >30 generally safe — use regular dosing); hepatotoxicity concern only with overdose or liver disease. Topical diclofenac: minimal systemic absorption — safer than oral NSAIDs in elderly. Opioids (oxycodone, tramadol with caution — renally cleared metabolites): low-dose, short-acting, carefully monitored. Duloxetine: evidence for chronic OA pain, acceptable in CKD at reduced dose. Physical therapies and activity optimisation remain important.

18. Q: A 70-year-old female sustains a hip fracture and undergoes hemiarthroplasty. On post-operative day 3 she develops confusion, hypoxia (SpO2 88% on air), and tachycardia. What is the most urgent concern?
    A: Pneumonia — CXR and antibiotics
    B: Pulmonary embolism — CTPA; therapeutic anticoagulation if confirmed; hip fracture and surgery are very high VTE risk
    C: Post-operative delirium — haloperidol
    D: Myocardial infarction — ECG and troponin
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Hip fracture and orthopaedic surgery carry the highest VTE risk of any surgical procedure. PE presenting post-operatively day 2–5 with hypoxia, tachycardia, and confusion must be urgently excluded. CTPA is the investigation of choice. Therapeutic anticoagulation (LMWH or DOAC) if PE confirmed. Extended VTE prophylaxis (28–35 days) with LMWH or DOAC (rivaroxaban, apixaban) is standard after THR/hemiarthroplasty. Confusion is a common feature of PE in elderly — do not dismiss acute deterioration post-hip surgery as "post-operative delirium" without excluding PE and MI (also check ECG and troponin).

19. Q: What is the "cross-leg" (FADIR) sign in hip OA assessment and why is the history of pain distribution important?
    A: The cross-leg sign is a specific test for trochanteric bursitis
    B: FADIR (Flexion, ADduction, Internal Rotation) reproduces groin pain in intra-articular hip pathology; pain distribution is important because hip OA typically causes groin pain ± anterior thigh ± knee — lateral hip pain is more likely GTPS; buttock pain suggests lumbar referred pain or SI joint
    C: FADIR assesses PCL integrity
    D: The cross-leg sign diagnoses iliotibial band syndrome
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Pain location helps determine the source of hip pain: Groin (anterior) = intra-articular (OA, FAI, labral tear, septic arthritis) — FADIR positive. Lateral hip = periarticular, GTPS/gluteal tendinopathy — tenderness over greater trochanter. Posterior buttock = referred from lumbar spine, SI joint, piriformis. Anterior thigh and knee = referred from hip (L3 distribution) — always examine the hip in patients with anterior knee pain. FADIR is most sensitive for intra-articular pathology — positive result = pain reproduced in the groin. Lateral hip tenderness and positive resistance tests (abduction) = GTPS.

20. Q: A 55-year-old male on high-dose prednisolone for vasculitis develops bilateral hip pain. MRI shows signal changes in both femoral heads consistent with early avascular necrosis without collapse. What management is indicated?
    A: Immediate bilateral THR
    B: Urgent orthopaedic referral; reduce steroids to minimum effective dose; consider core decompression (bilateral) to prevent collapse; monitor with serial MRI; weight protect if significant pain
    C: Continue current steroid dose — AVN will resolve spontaneously
    D: NSAIDs only — analgesia until collapse occurs
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Early AVN (pre-collapse, Ficat stage I–II) — best opportunity to prevent femoral head collapse. Management: reduce steroids to the minimum effective dose (discuss with treating physician — balance AVN risk against underlying disease control), orthopaedic referral for core decompression (drilling of the necrotic area to reduce intraosseous pressure and allow revascularisation — best results in early stage). Protected weight bearing. Serial MRI monitoring (every 3–6 months) to assess progression. Once collapse occurs (Ficat III–IV) — THR is required. NPs managing patients on long-term corticosteroids should have a low threshold for MRI if hip pain develops — early diagnosis saves the joint.
