# MSK — Chest Wall — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Always Exclude Before Diagnosing MSK Chest Pain
- Acute coronary syndrome — ECG and troponin mandatory for any exertional component or risk factors
- Aortic dissection — tearing pain radiating to the back, unequal BP in arms — emergency transfer
- Pulmonary embolism — pleuritic chest pain, dyspnoea, tachycardia, risk factors — CTPA
- Tension pneumothorax — dyspnoea, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation, haemodynamic instability — immediate needle decompression
- Oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave) — severe chest pain after vomiting, surgical emphysema — emergency transfer
- Rib fracture with haemothorax or pneumothorax — respiratory compromise — emergency transfer
- Flail chest — paradoxical chest wall movement with multiple rib fractures — emergency transfer
- Sternal fracture — significant trauma mechanism — imaging urgently

### Overview
Chest wall pain is extremely common and is frequently misattributed to cardiac causes, generating significant patient anxiety and unnecessary investigation. However, chest wall pain can also represent serious pathology — cardiac, pulmonary, and vascular causes must always be systematically excluded before a musculoskeletal diagnosis is made. NPs have a critical role in distinguishing MSK chest wall pain from life-threatening causes and in providing accurate reassurance once serious pathology is excluded.

### Common Chest Wall Conditions

#### Costochondritis
Inflammation of the costal cartilages — the most common cause of anterior chest wall pain in primary care. Affects the 2nd to 5th costochondral junctions most commonly. Presents with localised anterior chest pain, reproduced exactly by palpation of the affected costochondral junction. May be unilateral or bilateral. No visible swelling (distinguishes from Tietze syndrome). Onset may follow viral upper respiratory tract infection, repetitive physical activity, or trauma. May be associated with seronegative spondyloarthropathy (check for other SpA features — back pain, uveitis, psoriasis).

Management: NSAIDs (ibuprofen 400mg TDS) for 2–4 weeks, heat, activity modification. Local corticosteroid injection (triamcinolone) for refractory cases. Most resolve within weeks to months. Reassurance is essential — many patients fear cardiac disease.

#### Tietze Syndrome
Similar to costochondritis but distinguished by visible and palpable swelling at the affected costochondral junction. Usually affects a single junction — 2nd or 3rd most common. Less common than costochondritis. Management: as for costochondritis. NSAIDs, heat, corticosteroid injection. Self-limiting.

#### Rib Fractures
Rib fractures occur from direct trauma (sport, assault, falls, motor vehicle accidents) or from insufficiency mechanisms (osteoporosis, prolonged coughing, vigorous sport in older adults). The 4th–9th ribs are most commonly fractured. Clinical features: acute onset localised chest pain, point tenderness on rib palpation, pain sharply worsened by deep inspiration, coughing, or movement, splinted breathing.

Complications: pneumothorax (air in pleural space — 1–2%), haemothorax (blood in pleural space), pulmonary contusion, pneumonia (from splinting and atelectasis — the most common serious complication). In elderly patients and those with underlying lung disease, even a single rib fracture carries significant morbidity risk. Flail chest (≥3 contiguous ribs fractured in ≥2 places — paradoxical breathing movement) is a life-threatening emergency.

Management: Adequate analgesia is the cornerstone — it enables deep breathing and coughing, preventing atelectasis and pneumonia. NSAIDs + paracetamol ± opioids. Incentive spirometry and physiotherapy are critical. Chest binding is contraindicated — reduces tidal volume and worsens atelectasis. Intercostal nerve blocks or thoracic epidural for multiple rib fractures provide superior analgesia.

Indications for hospital admission: elderly, ≥3 rib fractures, bilateral fractures, underlying COPD or respiratory disease, SpO2 declining, pain uncontrolled with oral analgesia, suspected complications (pneumothorax, haemothorax, pulmonary contusion).

#### Cough Fractures
Spontaneous rib fractures from forceful coughing — common in patients with chronic cough (COPD, whooping cough), osteoporosis, or those on long-term corticosteroids. The 5th–9th ribs are most vulnerable. Often not visible on plain X-ray initially (periosteal reaction takes 2–3 weeks to appear). CT or bone scan more sensitive. Management as for traumatic rib fractures. Treat the underlying cause of the cough.

#### Intercostal Muscle Strain
Injury to the intercostal muscles from forceful twisting, overhead activity, or direct impact. Localised rib cage pain, worse with rotation and deep breathing. Tenderness over the intercostal space (not the rib itself — distinguishes from rib fracture). No crepitus. Normal breathing mechanics. Management: RICE, NSAIDs, activity modification, physiotherapy. Usually resolves within 3–4 weeks.

#### Herpes Zoster (Shingles) — Chest Wall
Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in thoracic dermatomes — presents as severe dermatomal chest wall pain (often burning or electric) before (prodromal phase), during, and after the vesicular rash. NPs must recognise pre-rash zoster (prodromal pain without rash) to avoid misdiagnosis as cardiac or pleural pain. The rash does not cross the midline. Management: valaciclovir 1g TDS for 7 days (most effective if commenced within 72 hours of rash). Analgesia: NSAIDs + paracetamol, gabapentin or pregabalin for neuropathic component. Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN): persistent pain >3 months after rash — managed with gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline, topical lidocaine or capsaicin. Shingles vaccination (Shingrix — recombinant adjuvanted zoster vaccine): recommended for adults ≥50, two doses 2–6 months apart.

#### Slipping Rib Syndrome (Painful Rib Syndrome)
Hypermobility and slipping of the lower rib cartilages (8th–10th ribs) — the anterior cartilaginous tips slip under the rib above, causing sharp pain. Hooking manoeuvre: examiner hooks fingers under the lower rib margin and pulls anteriorly — reproduces the patient's pain and may reproduce the clicking sensation. Often misdiagnosed and a cause of chronic undiagnosed chest or upper abdominal pain. Management: reassurance, activity modification, physiotherapy, intercostal nerve block. Surgical stabilisation for refractory cases.

#### Fibromyalgia and Chest Wall Tenderness
Fibromyalgia commonly causes chest wall tenderness — often multiple tender points across the chest wall without localised costochondral involvement. Associated features: widespread pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, cognitive difficulties, anxiety, depression. Management: multidisciplinary — exercise (aerobic), CBT, sleep management, low-dose amitriptyline or duloxetine. Avoid prolonged opioid use.

#### Pectoral Muscle Injury
Pectoralis major rupture: forceful eccentric contraction (bench press, fall). Sudden pain and weakness, ecchymosis, palpable defect at the anterior axillary fold. Ultrasound or MRI confirms. Surgical repair recommended for complete rupture in active patients. Pectoral strain: less severe — RICE, NSAIDs, physiotherapy.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### Chest Wall Pain History

#### Mandatory Cardiac and Vascular Exclusion
Before diagnosing MSK chest wall pain, always ask:
- Is the pain exertional? — cardiac
- Does it radiate to the jaw, arm, or back? — cardiac or aortic
- Is it tearing or ripping in quality? — aortic dissection
- Are there cardiac risk factors? — diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, smoking, family history
- Is there dyspnoea, pleuritic character, haemoptysis, recent surgery or immobility? — PE
- Is the pain associated with palpitations, syncope, or near-syncope? — arrhythmia
- Recent trauma? — rib fracture, pneumothorax

#### MSK Chest Wall Pain Characteristics
- Reproduced by palpation of a specific chest wall point (most important MSK sign)
- Localised — not diffuse or vague
- Worsens with trunk movement, rotation, reaching overhead
- Positional — worse with certain positions
- Non-exertional (cardiac pain is exertional)
- Associated with recent physical activity, coughing, trauma, or viral URTI

#### Past History
- Previous chest wall injuries or conditions
- Osteoporosis — fragility fracture risk
- COPD or chronic cough — rib fracture risk
- Malignancy — pathological fracture, pleural involvement
- Herpes zoster history — recurrence rare; post-herpetic neuralgia
- Corticosteroid use — bone fragility

### Chest Wall Examination

#### Systematic Approach
1. Vital signs: BP (both arms if dissection concern), HR, RR, SpO2, temperature
2. General: respiratory distress, posture, accessory muscle use
3. Inspection: bruising, swelling at costochondral junction (Tietze), surgical emphysema (crepitus under skin — oesophageal rupture), paradoxical movement (flail chest), rash (herpes zoster)
4. Auscultation: breath sounds bilaterally — absent or reduced (pneumothorax, haemothorax, effusion), added sounds
5. Percussion: resonance (normal), hyper-resonance (pneumothorax), dullness (haemothorax, consolidation)
6. Palpation: systematic palpation of all costochondral junctions, ribs, sternum, intercostal spaces, clavicles, anterior chest wall — is the pain exactly reproduced?
7. Movement: does deep inspiration, coughing, or trunk rotation reproduce the pain?

#### Investigations
- ECG: mandatory for any chest pain (exclude ACS, arrhythmia)
- Troponin: if any exertional component, cardiac risk factors, or diagnostic uncertainty
- CXR: rib fractures (many missed — clinical diagnosis), pneumothorax, haemothorax, cardiac size, pulmonary infiltrate
- CT chest: pneumothorax (if X-ray equivocal), aortic dissection (CT angiogram), complex rib fractures, haemothorax, pulmonary embolism (CTPA)
- D-dimer: low-probability PE (Wells score ≤4)
- FBE, CRP: infection, malignancy
- Bone scan or CT rib: confirmed rib fractures not visible on X-ray
- VZV serology: if zoster suspected before rash appears (prodromal phase)
- Bone density (DXA): if cough or fragility rib fracture — osteoporosis assessment

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 68-year-old with Rib Fractures After a Fall

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 68-year-old female with left-sided chest pain after a fall 24 hours ago.

History: Fell from a step ladder while gardening — landed on the left side. Immediate left lateral chest pain. Pain has been worsening — currently 8/10, severe on deep inspiration, coughing, and movement. Breathing is shallow. Slept poorly. No dyspnoea at rest. No haemoptysis. No fever. No cardiac symptoms — pain is clearly positional and does not radiate.

Past History: Osteoporosis (on alendronate 70mg weekly and calcium/vitamin D). Hypertension (perindopril 10mg). COPD (mild — on Spiriva inhaler). Non-smoker for 20 years (30 pack-year history). BMI 22.

Medications: Alendronate, perindopril, calcium/vitamin D, tiotropium inhaler.

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: BP 136/84 | HR 92 | Temp 36.8°C | RR 20 | SpO2 95% on air (baseline unknown, but COPD patient — concerning)

General: In pain, splinting left chest. Shallow breathing pattern. No respiratory distress at rest.

Respiratory: Reduced air entry left lower zone. Dull to percussion left base. No absent breath sounds. No wheeze. No tracheal deviation.

Chest Wall: Point tenderness on palpation of left 7th and 8th ribs at the mid-axillary line — exquisitely tender. Crepitus on palpation of the 8th rib (suggests fracture). No paradoxical movement. No surgical emphysema.

Cardiovascular: Regular rate and rhythm. No murmur. No raised JVP.

ECG: Sinus tachycardia (HR 92). No ischaemic changes.

CXR: Left 7th and 8th rib fractures visible on CXR. Small left pleural effusion/haemothorax noted at left base. No pneumothorax.

**A — Assessment**
1. Left 7th and 8th rib fractures — clinically confirmed, radiologically confirmed.
2. Small left haemothorax — clinically stable at present. Monitoring required.
3. COPD — significantly increases risk of respiratory complications from rib fractures and splinted breathing.
4. SpO2 95% — reduced, consistent with COPD + haemothorax + splinting.
5. Osteoporosis — fragility fracture from relatively minor fall.

**P — Plan**
Admission — Required (criteria met: elderly, COPD, ≥2 rib fractures, haemothorax, SpO2 95%)
- Call 000 for transfer to hospital

While awaiting transfer:
- Oxygen: 1–2L via nasal prongs — titrate SpO2 to 88–92% (COPD patient — avoid high-flow oxygen)
- Analgesia: paracetamol 1g IV + ketorolac 15mg IV (renal function to be checked) — adequate analgesia is the priority
- Position: sitting upright — improves respiratory mechanics

Hospital Management (to communicate in handover):
- Serial CXR — monitor haemothorax progression
- Consider intercostal nerve block (anaesthesia) or thoracic epidural — superior analgesia for rib fractures
- Incentive spirometry and physiotherapy — essential for COPD patient
- Respiratory monitoring — early detection of deterioration
- If haemothorax enlarges — intercostal catheter drainage

Osteoporosis Review:
- Fragility rib fracture — review alendronate adequacy. Consider switching to zoledronic acid IV annually (better compliance, proven fracture reduction). Ensure calcium and vitamin D adequate.
- Falls assessment and prevention — referral post-discharge

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 55-year-old male presents with left-sided chest pain that is reproduced exactly by palpation of the 3rd left costochondral junction. He has no dyspnoea, no radiation, no exertional component, and normal ECG and troponin. What is the most likely diagnosis?
   A: Unstable angina — urgent cardiology referral
   B: Costochondritis — NSAIDs, heat, and reassurance
   C: Pulmonary embolism — CTPA
   D: Aortic dissection — CT angiogram
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Costochondritis: localised anterior chest pain reproduced exactly by palpation of the costochondral junction — this is the defining clinical feature. Normal ECG and troponin exclude ACS. No exertional, radiation, or vascular features. Management: NSAIDs (ibuprofen 400mg TDS) for 2–4 weeks, heat, activity modification. Reassurance is critical — many patients fear cardiac disease and the reassurance that the pain is musculoskeletal can be profoundly relieving. Most cases resolve within weeks to months. Local corticosteroid injection for refractory cases. Distinguish from Tietze syndrome (same presentation but with visible swelling at the junction).

2. Q: What distinguishes Tietze syndrome from costochondritis?
   A: Tietze affects the lower ribs; costochondritis affects the upper ribs
   B: Tietze syndrome has visible and palpable swelling at the affected costochondral junction; costochondritis has no swelling — tenderness only
   C: Tietze is always bilateral; costochondritis is always unilateral
   D: Tietze requires surgery; costochondritis resolves without treatment
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Both conditions present with anterior chest wall pain at the costochondral junctions. The key distinguishing feature: Tietze syndrome has visible, palpable swelling (soft tissue swelling) at the affected junction — usually a single joint, most commonly 2nd or 3rd. Costochondritis has no swelling — tenderness only. Tietze is less common. Both are managed similarly — NSAIDs, heat, local corticosteroid injection for refractory cases, reassurance. Both are benign and self-limiting. Cardiac causes must be excluded in both presentations before a musculoskeletal diagnosis is accepted. Tietze syndrome may occasionally require ultrasound or MRI to confirm the swelling is at the cartilage junction and not a lymph node or tumour.

3. Q: A 72-year-old female with osteoporosis presents after a fall. She has point tenderness over the left 6th rib laterally and severe pain with deep inspiration. CXR shows no pneumothorax. SpO2 is 94% on air. She has COPD. What is the most appropriate management?
   A: Prescribe NSAIDs and discharge home with GP follow-up in 1 week
   B: Hospital admission — criteria met for admission (elderly, COPD, SpO2 94%, rib fracture); adequate analgesia, incentive spirometry, physiotherapy, oxygen, monitoring for complications
   C: Chest binding for comfort and discharge
   D: CT chest immediately to exclude aortic dissection
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Rib fracture admission criteria: ≥3 rib fractures OR ≥2 fractures in an elderly patient, underlying COPD or respiratory disease, SpO2 declining or below baseline, pain uncontrolled with oral analgesia, haemothorax or pneumothorax. This patient meets multiple criteria. Chest binding is contraindicated — reduces tidal volume and worsens atelectasis and pneumonia risk. Hospital management: NSAIDs + paracetamol ± opioids for analgesia (adequate pain relief is the most important intervention — it enables deep breathing); incentive spirometry (10 deep breaths hourly); chest physiotherapy; oxygen titrated to maintain SpO2 88–92% in COPD; serial CXR to monitor for pneumothorax or haemothorax development; consider intercostal nerve block or thoracic epidural for superior analgesia.

4. Q: A 65-year-old male presents with severe burning left-sided chest pain for 3 days, without a rash. He describes it as an electric, constant burning along a band around the left side of his chest. He has no cardiac risk factors. ECG is normal. Troponin is negative. What diagnosis must be considered and what should be examined for?
   A: Musculoskeletal strain — NSAIDs
   B: Herpes zoster (prodromal phase — pain before rash) — examine carefully for early vesicles along the dermatomal distribution; start valaciclovir if suspicion is high even without visible rash
   C: Pulmonary embolism — CTPA immediately
   D: Costochondritis — palpation confirms
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Herpes zoster (shingles) prodromal phase: VZV reactivation causes dermatomal pain 1–5 days before the vesicular rash appears. The pain is typically described as burning, electric, or stabbing in a unilateral dermatomal band distribution. It can be mistaken for cardiac, pleural, or musculoskeletal pain. Key: examine carefully along the dermatomal distribution for early vesicles — they may be sparse or just appearing. If clinical suspicion is high, consider starting valaciclovir empirically (1g TDS for 7 days) even without visible rash — antivirals are most effective when started early. Once the rash appears, diagnosis is clinical. Pre-rash zoster is one of the most commonly misdiagnosed causes of unexplained chest pain.

5. Q: An 80-year-old man has been coughing persistently for 3 weeks with a viral respiratory illness. He now develops sudden onset right lateral chest pain, severe on inspiration. His COPD is mild. X-ray shows a right 7th rib fracture. What is the specific mechanism of this fracture and how should it be managed?
   A: Traumatic fracture from a fall — standard rib fracture management
   B: Cough fracture (fatigue fracture from forceful repetitive coughing) — analgesia, treat the underlying cough cause, incentive spirometry; admission criteria should be assessed given age
   C: Pathological fracture — urgent malignancy workup
   D: Osteoporotic fragility fracture — anti-osteoporotic therapy only
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Cough fractures: spontaneous rib fractures from repetitive forceful coughing — a fatigue/stress fracture mechanism. Common in: elderly patients, COPD (chronic cough), whooping cough, post-viral coughs, and those on long-term corticosteroids (reduced bone density). Most common at ribs 5–9. Management: analgesia (NSAIDs + paracetamol ± opioids), treat the underlying cough (short course prednisolone for severe post-viral bronchospasm, antibiotics if secondary infection), incentive spirometry, physiotherapy. Assess for osteoporosis — DXA and anti-osteoporotic therapy. Assess admission criteria: age alone in an 80-year-old with COPD and a rib fracture should trigger strong consideration of admission for monitoring and analgesia optimisation.

6. Q: A patient presents with right-sided chest wall pain that has been present for 6 months. Multiple investigations including ECG, CXR, CT chest, and troponin are all normal. The pain is intermittent, sharp, and occurs in the right lower rib margin. Pressing a finger under the lower right rib margin and pulling anteriorly reproduces the exact pain. What is the diagnosis?
   A: Costochondritis — NSAIDs and reassurance
   B: Slipping rib syndrome — positive hooking manoeuvre; activity modification, physiotherapy, intercostal nerve block; surgical stabilisation for refractory cases
   C: Fibromyalgia — multidisciplinary pain management
   D: Atypical chest pain — refer to cardiology
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Slipping rib syndrome (painful rib syndrome): hypermobility of the lower costal cartilages (8th–10th ribs) causing the anterior cartilaginous tips to slip under the rib above. Sharp, intermittent lower rib cage or upper abdominal pain — often misdiagnosed as biliary, GI, or cardiac pain. The hooking manoeuvre is diagnostic: examiner hooks fingers under the lower rib margin and pulls anteriorly — reproduces the pain (and may reproduce the characteristic click/slip sensation). Management: reassurance (diagnosis alone provides significant relief), activity modification, physiotherapy, intercostal nerve block (diagnostic and therapeutic). Surgical stabilisation (costal cartilage resection) for refractory cases. Often misdiagnosed for years — making the diagnosis provides enormous reassurance.

7. Q: Which analgesic approach is most evidence-based for severe rib fractures in a hospitalised patient?
   A: Oral paracetamol alone — adequate for all rib fracture pain
   B: Multimodal analgesia: regular paracetamol + NSAID + opioid as required; with intercostal nerve block or thoracic epidural for optimal analgesia and respiratory function
   C: High-dose opioids only — most effective for severe pain
   D: Chest binding combined with oral paracetamol — immobilisation reduces pain
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Optimal rib fracture analgesia is multimodal: paracetamol 1g QID (regular) + NSAID (ibuprofen 400mg TDS or ketorolac IV) + opioid PRN for breakthrough. Regional analgesia provides superior analgesia and improves respiratory outcomes: intercostal nerve blocks (performed by anaesthetist or trained NP in some settings) — effective for 1–3 consecutive ribs; serratus anterior plane block; thoracic epidural — gold standard for multiple rib fractures, provides bilateral analgesia, enables deep breathing and physiotherapy, reduces pneumonia and mortality in elderly patients with multiple rib fractures. Chest binding is CONTRAINDICATED — increases atelectasis and pneumonia risk. Adequate analgesia is the single most important intervention in rib fracture management — it directly determines respiratory outcomes.

8. Q: A 50-year-old presents with right-sided chest pain that started 2 days ago. A vesicular rash has appeared today along the right T5 dermatome. What is the treatment and what complication must be specifically discussed?
   A: Topical aciclovir cream — sufficient for herpes zoster
   B: Valaciclovir 1g orally three times daily for 7 days (or aciclovir 800mg five times daily); discuss post-herpetic neuralgia risk and management; shingles vaccination for future prevention
   C: IV aciclovir only — oral formulations are not effective
   D: No treatment needed — herpes zoster is self-limiting
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Herpes zoster (shingles) treatment: antiviral within 72 hours of rash onset significantly reduces severity, duration, and post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN) risk. Valaciclovir 1g TDS for 7 days (preferred — better bioavailability than aciclovir). Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN): persistent pain >3 months after rash resolution — occurs in 10–20% of patients, higher in elderly. Management: gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline, duloxetine, topical lidocaine or capsaicin patch. Shingrix vaccine: highly effective (>90%) at preventing shingles recurrence and PHN — two doses recommended for adults ≥50. Ophthalmology referral urgently if ophthalmic distribution (V1 branch — herpes zoster ophthalmicus — risk of corneal ulceration and vision loss). Immunocompromised patients: IV aciclovir inpatient.

9. Q: A 25-year-old male bodybuilder hears a pop while doing a heavy bench press. He develops immediate right anterior chest and axillary pain. He has weakness and pain on resisted internal rotation of the right shoulder. Ecchymosis is developing over the right anterior chest and axilla. A defect is palpable at the anterior axillary fold. What is the diagnosis?
   A: Anterior shoulder dislocation — shoulder reduction
   B: Pectoralis major rupture — MRI or ultrasound to confirm; surgical repair recommended for complete rupture in active patients
   C: Rib fracture — X-ray and analgesia
   D: Costochondritis — NSAIDs and reassurance
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Pectoralis major rupture: typically occurs during bench press — eccentric overload during heavy chest pressing. The tendon inserts on the humerus at the bicipital groove — rupture occurs most commonly at the musculotendinous junction or tendinous insertion. Classic presentation: sudden chest/axillary pain during bench press, weakness on resisted internal rotation and adduction, ecchymosis over the anterior chest and axilla, palpable defect at the anterior axillary fold (where the pectoralis forms the anterior wall of the axilla). MRI or ultrasound confirms complete vs partial rupture. Complete rupture in active patients: surgical repair is recommended for best functional outcome — earlier surgery gives better results. Partial rupture: conservative management with physiotherapy.

10. Q: What is the Hooking Manoeuvre and what diagnosis does a positive result suggest?
    A: Tapping over the carpal tunnel reproduces tingling — carpal tunnel syndrome
    B: Hooking fingers under the lower rib margin and pulling anteriorly reproduces the patient's chest/abdominal pain — slipping rib syndrome
    C: Sustained wrist flexion reproduces arm symptoms — Phalen's test for CTS
    D: Passive dorsiflexion of the great toe reproduces heel pain — plantar fasciitis (Windlass test)
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Hooking manoeuvre for slipping rib syndrome: examiner stands beside the patient, hooks their fingers under the lower rib margin (8th–10th ribs, anterior end), and pulls anteriorly and superiorly. A positive test reproduces the patient's typical pain — and often recreates the clicking or popping sensation the patient describes. This is the diagnostic test for slipping rib syndrome. The condition is often overlooked as a cause of chronic lower chest or upper abdominal pain because it is unfamiliar to many clinicians. Awareness of the diagnosis and application of the hooking manoeuvre is all that is needed — patients are often extremely relieved when their pain is finally reproduced and explained after months or years of investigation.

11. Q: A patient with known malignancy (lung cancer) presents with new-onset right-sided chest wall pain that is constant, not reproduced by palpation, and present at rest and at night. CXR shows a subtle area of rib destruction. What diagnosis must be excluded and what is the appropriate investigation?
    A: Costochondritis — NSAIDs and reassurance
    B: Pathological rib fracture from bone metastasis — CT chest and ribs or bone scan; refer to oncology; pain management with appropriate analgesia including opioids if needed
    C: Musculoskeletal strain — physiotherapy
    D: Herpes zoster — check for rash
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Constant chest wall pain NOT reproduced by palpation in a patient with known malignancy = pathological fracture from bone metastasis until proven otherwise. Red flags: constant pain, night pain, known malignancy, bony destruction on imaging. CT is the most sensitive for cortical bone destruction and establishes the extent of metastatic disease. Bone scan (whole body) assesses for other metastatic sites. Refer to oncology urgently. Pain management: regular analgesia following WHO analgesic ladder (paracetamol → NSAIDs → opioids), consider adding dexamethasone (reduces periosteal and neurogenic inflammation), bisphosphonate (zoledronic acid) for bone pain and fracture risk reduction in bone metastases. Palliative radiotherapy highly effective for localised bone pain. Do not reassure MSK on examination findings alone in a cancer patient with new chest pain.

12. Q: A 45-year-old male presents with acute onset left pleuritic chest pain and mild dyspnoea after a heavy lifting session. He is tall and thin. SpO2 is 95% on air. Breath sounds are reduced on the left. CXR shows a 20% left pneumothorax. He is haemodynamically stable. What is the management?
    A: Immediate needle thoracocentesis — all pneumothorax requires emergency decompression
    B: Small spontaneous pneumothorax in a haemodynamically stable patient — oxygen, observation, or aspiration; do not discharge without confirmation of resolution; refer to respiratory medicine or ED
    C: Reassure and discharge — small pneumothorax always resolves spontaneously
    D: Intercostal catheter (ICD) drain immediately
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP): occurs in tall, thin young males without underlying lung disease (bullae rupture). 20% pneumothorax with mild symptoms and haemodynamic stability: Australian (TSANZ) guidelines recommend: high-flow oxygen (accelerates nitrogen resorption — speeds resolution by 4x), observation for 3–6 hours, aspiration (16G catheter) if not resolving or significant symptoms. ICD for: large pneumothorax (>20% with symptoms), failed aspiration, secondary (underlying lung disease), bilateral, haemopneumothorax, tension pneumothorax. Tension pneumothorax (tracheal deviation, haemodynamic instability, absent breath sounds, expanding pneumothorax) = immediate 2nd intercostal space midclavicular line needle decompression, then ICD. Never discharge without confirmed resolution on repeat CXR. Follow-up: smoking cessation, avoid scuba diving. Recurrence risk 30% without pleurodesis.

13. Q: A 60-year-old female presents with bilateral anterior chest wall pain and fatigue. She has 11 tender points on examination including bilateral anterior chest wall, bilateral trapezius, bilateral lateral epicondyles, bilateral greater trochanters, and bilateral medial knees. What is the diagnosis and management?
    A: Bilateral costochondritis — bilateral corticosteroid injections
    B: Fibromyalgia — multidisciplinary management: aerobic exercise program (strongest evidence), cognitive behavioural therapy, sleep management, low-dose amitriptyline or duloxetine; avoid prolonged opioid use
    C: Rheumatoid arthritis — refer to rheumatology urgently
    D: Multiple rib fractures — CT chest
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Fibromyalgia: widespread musculoskeletal pain (≥3 months), multiple tender points (≥11/18 in classic criteria), fatigue, sleep disturbance, cognitive difficulties ("fibro fog"), and associated anxiety/depression. Chest wall tenderness is common and may be the presenting complaint. Investigations: normal inflammatory markers (distinguishes from inflammatory arthritis), normal CXR. 2010 ACR criteria (more commonly used now): widespread pain index ≥7 + symptom severity score ≥5, OR WPI 3–6 + SSS ≥9. Management: patient education and validation (diagnosis provides relief), graded aerobic exercise (swimming, walking — most evidence), CBT, sleep hygiene, low-dose tricyclic antidepressant (amitriptyline 10–25mg nocte — improves sleep and pain), duloxetine or pregabalin. Avoid prolonged opioids — worsen fibromyalgia outcomes.

14. Q: Why is chest binding (strapping) contraindicated in rib fracture management?
    A: It increases the risk of rib displacement
    B: It restricts chest wall movement, reduces tidal volume, promotes atelectasis, and increases pneumonia risk — particularly dangerous in elderly patients and those with underlying lung disease
    C: It causes skin pressure injuries
    D: It is only contraindicated in athletes — safe for elderly patients
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Chest binding is a historical practice that is now contraindicated in rib fracture management. Although it may temporarily reduce pain by restricting movement, it significantly worsens respiratory outcomes: reduced tidal volume → atelectasis → mucous plugging → pneumonia. This is particularly dangerous in elderly patients (reduced respiratory reserve) and those with COPD or other lung disease. Modern management prioritises adequate analgesia — enabling deep breathing and effective coughing — as the cornerstone of respiratory complication prevention. Incentive spirometry, chest physiotherapy, and early mobilisation complete the respiratory management. Regional analgesia (intercostal nerve blocks, thoracic epidural) provides superior pain relief without the complications of binding.

15. Q: A 40-year-old manual worker develops acute right lateral chest pain while lifting heavy equipment. He has severe point tenderness at the right 8th rib laterally and pain sharply worsened by deep inspiration. He is otherwise well. SpO2 99%. CXR shows a right 8th rib fracture with no pneumothorax or haemothorax. He is young, healthy, and has good pain control on NSAIDs plus paracetamol. What is the appropriate disposition?
    A: Mandatory hospital admission for all rib fractures
    B: Discharge home with appropriate analgesia, written safety netting, and GP follow-up within 48–72 hours — young healthy patient with single rib fracture, adequate analgesia, no complications, and good SpO2
    C: Intercostal catheter insertion before discharge
    D: Refer to thoracic surgery urgently
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Not all rib fractures require hospital admission. Outpatient management is appropriate for: young patient (<65), single or 1–2 rib fractures, no underlying lung disease, pain adequately controlled with oral analgesia, SpO2 normal, no complications (pneumothorax, haemothorax). Discharge with: NSAIDs + paracetamol ± short-term opioids (tramadol or oxycodone) for breakthrough, incentive spirometry instructions (10 deep breaths hourly while awake), activity modification, written safety netting. Return urgently if: increasing dyspnoea, SpO2 drops, pain uncontrolled, fever. Physiotherapy referral. GP follow-up within 48–72 hours. Contrast with elderly patients, COPD patients, or multiple fractures — these require admission regardless of initial SpO2 and pain control.

16. Q: An 18-year-old female presents with left-sided sharp chest pain that has been present for 6 months. It is reproduced by pressing over the 4th left costochondral junction. ECG and CXR are normal. She has no systemic features, no weight loss, and inflammatory markers are normal. She is anxious about her heart. What is the most important management step?
    A: Troponin and 24-hour Holter monitor — must exclude all cardiac causes
    B: Clear explanation that the diagnosis is costochondritis (a benign MSK condition), that the pain is not coming from her heart, and reassurance about prognosis — combined with NSAIDs and heat
    C: Refer to cardiology — chest pain in a young female always requires specialist review
    D: MRI chest — CT radiation not appropriate at this age
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Reassurance is the most important therapeutic intervention in costochondritis — particularly for young patients who fear cardiac disease. The combination of: reproducible chest wall pain with a specific point tender on palpation, normal ECG, and no cardiac features makes costochondritis the diagnosis. Providing a clear, confident, evidence-based explanation reduces anxiety significantly — often more than any medication. Explain: the costal cartilages (not the heart) are inflamed, this is very common, it is not dangerous, and it will resolve. NSAIDs + heat for symptom management. Avoid over-investigating — additional tests driven by patient anxiety (without clinical indication) amplify health anxiety rather than reducing it. If the patient is not reassured, a short course of physiotherapy or a single corticosteroid injection provides further relief and reinforces the MSK diagnosis.

17. Q: A patient presents with chronic diffuse chest wall tenderness. She scores highly on PHQ-9 (moderate depression) and GAD-7 (moderate anxiety). She has widespread tenderness including the chest wall, bilateral trapezii, and forearms. Sleep is very poor. Inflammatory markers are normal. What is the most important aspect of management?
    A: Extensive further investigation — fibromyalgia is a diagnosis of exclusion requiring many tests
    B: Validate the diagnosis (fibromyalgia), address depression and anxiety, and prescribe an evidence-based treatment program: graded aerobic exercise + CBT + low-dose amitriptyline for sleep
    C: Opioid analgesia — most effective for diffuse pain
    D: Corticosteroid injection at each tender point
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Fibromyalgia management: 1) Validation and explanation — acknowledging the reality of the pain and explaining the neurological mechanism (central sensitisation — pain amplification) is essential and often transformative. 2) Exercise — aerobic exercise (swimming, walking) has the strongest evidence; start low and build gradually; pain flares are expected and should be managed rather than stopping exercise. 3) CBT — addresses catastrophising, fear-avoidance, and pacing. 4) Sleep — amitriptyline 10–25mg nocte improves sleep quality and pain. 5) Depression and anxiety management — duloxetine or venlafaxine have dual benefit (depression/anxiety + fibromyalgia pain). Avoid: prolonged opioids (induce central sensitisation), benzodiazepines for sleep long-term, polypharmacy, excessive investigation. Refer to a multidisciplinary pain program for severe or refractory fibromyalgia.

18. Q: A 30-year-old cyclist develops left lateral chest pain while cycling. He has point tenderness at the left 5th rib in the mid-axillary line. No dyspnoea, SpO2 99%, and CXR is normal. He does not recall a fall. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    A: Pulmonary embolism — CTPA
    B: Intercostal muscle strain or stress reaction — RICE, NSAIDs, activity modification; if X-ray negative and pain persists, consider bone scan or CT for occult fracture
    C: Spontaneous pneumothorax — observe
    D: Herpes zoster — examine for rash
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Intercostal muscle strain: relatively common in cycling (repetitive trunk rotation), rowing, and other trunk-intensive activities. Point tenderness over the intercostal space (or rib in this case) with no trauma and normal CXR suggests muscle strain or stress reaction. Distinguishing features from rib fracture: strain tends to have tenderness over the intercostal space (between ribs) not the rib itself, no crepitus, and normal X-ray. However, stress fractures from repetitive loading (e.g. 5th rib in rowers — well described) can also present this way with a normal initial X-ray. If pain does not improve with 1–2 weeks of rest and NSAIDs, arrange bone scan or CT to exclude occult fracture. Management: rest from cycling for 1–2 weeks, NSAIDs, gradual return with attention to bike fit and pedalling mechanics.

19. Q: A 55-year-old female on long-term prednisolone (15mg daily for 3 years) for autoimmune hepatitis develops sudden onset right anterior chest pain when stretching to reach overhead. CXR appears normal. She has point tenderness at the right 4th rib anterolaterally. What additional investigation should be considered and what should be initiated?
    A: Echocardiogram — prednisolone increases cardiac risk
    B: Bone scan or CT rib — to detect occult fracture (X-ray misses rib fractures in 30–40% of cases); DXA scan for osteoporosis assessment; consider zoledronic acid or denosumab for corticosteroid-induced osteoporosis
    C: MRI brain — prednisolone causes neurological complications
    D: Reassure — chest pain on stretching is always musculoskeletal
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Corticosteroid-induced osteoporosis significantly increases fragility fracture risk — including rib fractures from minor mechanisms (reaching, coughing, turning). Long-term prednisolone ≥5mg daily for ≥3 months requires osteoporosis prevention. CXR misses 30–40% of rib fractures — bone scan (high sensitivity) or CT rib (gold standard) should be considered if clinically suspected. DXA scan to quantify bone density loss. Corticosteroid-induced osteoporosis management: calcium + vitamin D supplementation (baseline), bisphosphonate (alendronate or zoledronic acid) or denosumab for significant bone density loss or high fracture risk. FRAX calculator (with adjustment for corticosteroid dose) guides treatment thresholds. This patient should have been on bone protection from the start of long-term corticosteroid therapy.

20. Q: Which chest pain feature most strongly supports a musculoskeletal rather than cardiac diagnosis?
    A: Chest pain occurring at rest — cardiac pain can occur at rest in unstable angina
    B: Chest pain that is exactly reproduced by pressing on a specific point on the chest wall — this is the most specific musculoskeletal feature
    C: Left-sided chest pain — cardiac pain is always left-sided
    D: Chest pain lasting more than 30 minutes — distinguishes MI from angina
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Exact reproduction of the presenting chest pain by palpation of a specific point on the chest wall is the most specific feature of musculoskeletal chest pain. When a patient says "yes, that's exactly my pain" in response to targeted palpation — this is highly reassuring for MSK aetiology. Other MSK features: positional (worse with movement, rotation, deep breathing), non-exertional (cardiac pain is typically exertional), associated with movement or activity, worsened by specific positions. These features support MSK diagnosis but always require cardiac exclusion first (ECG ± troponin) in appropriate patients. Cardiac pain: exertional, radiation to arm/jaw/back, associated dyspnoea, risk factors, S3 gallop, dynamic ECG changes — these features cannot be attributed to MSK regardless of chest wall tenderness.
