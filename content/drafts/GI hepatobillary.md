# GI & Hepatobiliary — NPCollab Module Content Draft

## OVERVIEW PAGE

### Red Flags — Refer Urgently or Call 000
- Haematemesis or melaena — upper GI bleed until excluded
- Rigid or board-like abdomen — peritonitis
- Acute severe abdominal pain with haemodynamic instability — ruptured AAA, mesenteric ischaemia
- Jaundice with fever and rigors — ascending cholangitis (Charcot's triad)
- Suspected bowel obstruction — absolute constipation, distension, vomiting
- Painless rectal bleeding in a patient over 50 — colorectal cancer until excluded
- Unintentional weight loss >5% in 6 months with GI symptoms
- Dysphagia — progressive difficulty swallowing solids then liquids
- New onset ascites — liver cirrhosis, malignancy
- Signs of hepatic encephalopathy — confusion, asterixis, fetor hepaticus

#### Lower GI

- Cirrhosis and its complications
- Drug-induced liver injury

### Key Conditions

#### GORD
Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease affects approximately 10–15% of Australians. Caused by lower oesophageal sphincter incompetence allowing acid reflux. Typical symptoms: heartburn, regurgitation, worse after meals and lying flat. Atypical: chronic cough, laryngitis, dental erosion. Management: lifestyle modification (weight loss, elevate head of bed, avoid triggers), PPI therapy for 4–8 weeks. H. pylori test and treat if peptic ulcer suspected. Alarm symptoms (dysphagia, weight loss, bleeding, age >50 with new symptoms) warrant urgent endoscopy referral.

#### Irritable Bowel Syndrome
IBS affects up to 15% of Australians and is a diagnosis of exclusion. Rome IV criteria: recurrent abdominal pain ≥1 day/week for 3 months, associated with ≥2 of: related to defecation, change in stool frequency, change in stool form. Subtypes: IBS-C (constipation predominant), IBS-D (diarrhoea predominant), IBS-M (mixed). Exclude red flags before diagnosing. Management: dietary modification (low FODMAP diet evidence-based), fibre, stress management, antispasmodics (hyoscine), loperamide for IBS-D, osmotic laxatives for IBS-C.

#### Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD/MASLD)
Now renamed Metabolic-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD). Most common liver disease in Australia, strongly associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and metabolic syndrome. Ranges from simple steatosis to NASH (non-alcoholic steatohepatitis) to cirrhosis. Diagnosis: elevated LFTs (ALT>AST), fatty liver on ultrasound, exclusion of other causes. FIB-4 score used to risk-stratify for advanced fibrosis. Management: weight loss (≥10% reduces liver fat significantly), treat metabolic risk factors, avoid alcohol, GLP-1 agonists show emerging benefit.

#### Hepatitis B
Australia has approximately 200,000 people living with chronic hepatitis B. Transmission: vertical (mother to child), sexual, blood-to-blood. Chronic infection defined as HBsAg positive >6 months. Monitoring: LFTs, HBV DNA, HBeAg/anti-HBe every 6–12 months. Refer to gastroenterology or hepatology for antiviral therapy consideration. Vaccination: three-dose course on Australian NIP. Screen: people born in intermediate/high prevalence countries, MSM, people who inject drugs, household contacts of HBsAg positive individuals.

#### Hepatitis C
Approximately 120,000 Australians living with chronic hepatitis C. Transmission: blood-to-blood (PWID primary risk in Australia). Curative direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy available — 8–12 week treatment, >95% cure rate. NPs can now prescribe DAA therapy under PBS authority. Screen: people who inject drugs, born in high prevalence countries, blood transfusion before 1990, tattoos in unregulated settings. Testing: HCV antibody first, if positive HCV RNA to confirm active infection.

#### Biliary Colic and Cholecystitis
Biliary colic: episodic severe RUQ or epigastric pain radiating to right shoulder, 30 minutes to 6 hours, often post-fatty meal, nausea and vomiting. No fever. Murphy's sign negative. Management: analgesia, ultrasound, elective cholecystectomy referral. Acute cholecystitis: persistent RUQ pain >6 hours, fever, Murphy's sign positive, elevated WCC and CRP. Requires hospital admission for IV antibiotics and surgical review.

## ASSESSMENT PAGE

### GI History

#### Abdominal Pain Assessment
- Site — nine regions of abdomen, radiation pattern
- Onset — sudden (perforation, ischaemia) vs gradual
- Character — colicky (bowel, biliary, ureteric) vs constant (peritonitis, ischaemia)
- Severity — VAS score, functional impact
- Timing — relationship to meals, defecation, menstrual cycle, position
- Associated symptoms — nausea, vomiting, change in bowel habit, bleeding
- Aggravating and relieving factors — food, position, antacids, defecation
- Previous episodes — same or different character?

#### Bowel Habit History
- Frequency — normal range 3 times/day to 3 times/week
- Bristol Stool Chart — types 1–2 (constipation), 3–4 (normal), 5–7 (loose/diarrhoea)
- Blood — fresh red (lower GI), dark or mixed with stool (higher), melaena (upper GI)
- Mucus — inflammatory bowel disease, IBS
- Urgency, tenesmus, incomplete evacuation — IBD, rectal pathology
- Nocturnal symptoms — organic disease more likely than functional

#### Alarm Features (always ask)
- Unintentional weight loss
- Progressive dysphagia
- Persistent vomiting
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Rectal bleeding or melaena
- Palpable abdominal or rectal mass
- Age >50 with new symptoms
- Family history of colorectal cancer or IBD

#### Liver and Hepatobiliary History
- Alcohol intake — units per week, duration, AUDIT-C score
- Jaundice — skin, sclera, urine colour (dark), stool colour (pale)
- Pruritus — cholestatic liver disease
- Abdominal swelling — ascites
- Risk factors for viral hepatitis — country of birth, IDU, sexual history, tattoos
- Medications — including complementary, herbal, over the counter
- Family history — liver disease, haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease

### GI Examination

#### General
- Jaundice — scleral icterus, skin
- Chronic liver disease stigmata — spider naevi, palmar erythema, leuconychia, clubbing, Dupuytren's, parotid enlargement, gynaecomastia, caput medusae, testicular atrophy
- Nutritional status — muscle wasting, cachexia
- Mental status — hepatic encephalopathy

#### Abdominal Examination
- Inspection — distension, visible peristalsis, scars, hernias, caput medusae
- Auscultation — bowel sounds (before palpation), bruits
- Percussion — tympanic vs dull, liver span, shifting dullness (ascites), fluid thrill
- Palpation — light then deep, all nine regions, organomegaly, tenderness, guarding, rigidity, Murphy's sign, Rovsing's sign

#### Key Signs
- Murphy's sign — acute cholecystitis (inspiratory arrest on RUQ palpation)
- Rovsing's sign — appendicitis (RIF pain on LIF palpation)
- Rebound tenderness — peritonitis
- Shifting dullness and fluid thrill — ascites
- Liver span — normal 10–12cm in MCL
- Splenomegaly — portal hypertension, haematological causes

#### Investigations
- LFTs — ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, bilirubin, albumin, total protein
- FBE — anaemia (bleeding, chronic disease), WCC (infection)
- CRP — inflammation, infection
- Coagulation — INR elevated in liver disease
- H. pylori — stool antigen test (preferred) or urea breath test
- HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc — hepatitis B status
- HCV antibody then HCV RNA — hepatitis C
- Coeliac serology — anti-tTG IgA with total IgA
- Faecal occult blood test (FOBT) — colorectal cancer screening
- Ultrasound abdomen — liver, gallbladder, biliary tract, spleen
- FIB-4 score — non-invasive liver fibrosis assessment (age × AST / (platelets × √ALT))
- Endoscopy referral — alarm features, GORD not responding, IBD assessment

## SOAP NOTE
### Example: 45-year-old with RUQ Pain — Biliary Colic

**S — Subjective**
Presenting Complaint: 45-year-old female with 3 episodes of severe right upper quadrant pain over the past 6 weeks, most recently last night.

History of Presenting Complaint: Episodic severe RUQ pain, cramping in character, radiating to the right shoulder and scapula. Each episode lasts approximately 2–3 hours then completely resolves. Nausea with each episode, no vomiting. Triggered by fatty meals — last night had fish and chips. No fever. No jaundice. No change in urine or stool colour. No diarrhoea. Between episodes she is entirely well with no abdominal discomfort. No weight loss.

Past History: Type 2 diabetes (metformin 500mg BD), hypertension (perindopril 5mg daily), hyperlipidaemia (atorvastatin 20mg nocte). No previous abdominal surgery.

Social History: Works as a school teacher. BMI 31. Non-smoker. Minimal alcohol — 1–2 standard drinks per week. Married with two children.

Allergies: NKDA

Patient Concerns: "The pain is unbearable when it happens — I Googled it and I think it might be gallstones."

**O — Objective**
Vital Signs: BP 136/84 mmHg | HR 78 bpm | Temp 36.7°C | RR 14 | SpO2 99% RA

General: Well-appearing, comfortable at rest, no jaundice, no pallor.

Abdominal Examination:
- Inspection: mild central adiposity, no distension, no visible masses, no surgical scars
- Bowel sounds: present and normal
- Percussion: tympanic throughout, no shifting dullness
- Palpation: soft, mild tenderness on deep palpation RUQ, no guarding, no rigidity
- Murphy's sign: negative (no inspiratory arrest)
- No organomegaly palpable
- No rebound tenderness

Investigations:
- LFTs: ALT 28, AST 24, ALP 82, GGT 45, bilirubin 14 — all normal
- FBE: normal
- CRP: 4 (normal)
- Ultrasound abdomen ordered — pending

**A — Assessment**
Primary Diagnosis: Biliary colic — symptomatic cholelithiasis (K80.20)

Differential Diagnoses Considered:
- Acute cholecystitis — excluded: no fever, Murphy's sign negative, normal WCC and CRP, episodes fully resolve
- GORD — less likely: pain too severe, radiates to shoulder, no heartburn, no response to antacids
- Peptic ulcer disease — less likely: no epigastric pain, no NSAID use, episodic and fully resolving
- Hepatitis — excluded: normal LFTs, no jaundice, no risk factors

Risk factors for cholelithiasis present: female, age 45, overweight (BMI 31), type 2 diabetes — "four Fs" (Female, Fat, Forty, Fertile)

**P — Plan**
Investigations:
- Ultrasound abdomen — to confirm gallstones and assess biliary tree. If confirms cholelithiasis, refer for elective cholecystectomy.
- LFTs already normal — repeat if symptoms change or jaundice develops

Immediate Management:
- Diclofenac 50mg oral with food for pain during episodes (check renal function — metformin patient)
- Avoid fatty foods to reduce episode frequency while awaiting surgery
- Low-fat diet education provided

Referral:
- Refer to general surgery for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy once ultrasound confirms cholelithiasis
- Referral letter sent today

Safety Netting — Return urgently or go to ED if:
- Fever develops with RUQ pain — acute cholecystitis
- Jaundice develops — biliary obstruction or choledocholithiasis
- Pain becomes constant and not resolving — escalation to cholecystitis
- Severe vomiting preventing oral intake

Patient Education: Discussed likely diagnosis of gallstones, need for ultrasound confirmation, surgical management, and dietary modification in the interim. Patient understands and agrees with plan.

## QUIZ — 20 QUESTIONS

1. Q: A 52-year-old male presents with 3 months of heartburn and regurgitation, worse after meals and at night. No dysphagia, no weight loss, no anaemia. What is the most appropriate initial management?
   A: Urgent endoscopy referral
   B: H. pylori stool antigen test and triple therapy if positive
   C: Lifestyle modification and 4–8 week trial of PPI therapy
   D: Barium swallow
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Uncomplicated GORD without alarm features in a patient under 55 is managed initially with lifestyle modification (weight loss, elevate head of bed, avoid triggers — alcohol, coffee, fatty foods, late meals) and a 4–8 week trial of PPI (omeprazole 20mg or pantoprazole 40mg daily). Urgent endoscopy is indicated if alarm features are present: dysphagia, weight loss, bleeding, anaemia, age >55 with new symptoms, or symptoms not responding to PPI.

2. Q: Which of the following is the most accurate non-invasive test for diagnosing active H. pylori infection?
   A: H. pylori IgG serology
   B: Urea breath test or stool antigen test
   C: Endoscopic biopsy only
   D: Full blood examination showing iron deficiency anaemia
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Urea breath test and stool antigen test are the most accurate non-invasive tests for active H. pylori infection (sensitivity and specificity >95%). Serology (IgG) detects past or present infection and remains positive after eradication — not useful for confirming active infection or successful eradication. Endoscopic biopsy (rapid urease test or histology) is the gold standard but invasive. Stop PPIs 2 weeks and antibiotics 4 weeks before testing to avoid false negatives.

3. Q: A 38-year-old female with IBS-D is commenced on a low FODMAP diet. After 6 weeks her symptoms have significantly improved. What is the correct next step?
   A: Continue strict low FODMAP diet permanently
   B: Systematic reintroduction of FODMAP foods one group at a time to identify specific triggers
   C: Stop the diet and restart normal eating
   D: Add loperamide 2mg daily regardless of dietary response
   Correct: B
   Explanation: The low FODMAP diet has three phases: elimination (4–6 weeks), reintroduction (systematically test each FODMAP group), and personalisation (long-term diet based on individual triggers). Permanent strict restriction is unnecessary, nutritionally suboptimal, and socially limiting. Reintroduction identifies which FODMAPs trigger symptoms for that individual. Dietitian support improves outcomes. The low FODMAP diet has the best evidence base for IBS symptom management.

4. Q: A 60-year-old male has FIB-4 score of 3.2 calculated from routine bloods. He has known MASLD (fatty liver on ultrasound, elevated ALT). What does this indicate and what is the next step?
   A: FIB-4 <3.25 is reassuring — no further action needed
   B: FIB-4 ≥2.67 indicates high risk of advanced fibrosis — refer to hepatology for further assessment including liver stiffness measurement
   C: FIB-4 >3 means cirrhosis is confirmed — refer for liver transplant assessment
   D: Repeat FIB-4 in 12 months only
   Correct: B
   Explanation: FIB-4 score is used to risk-stratify liver fibrosis non-invasively. Score <1.30 = low risk (F0–F1), 1.30–2.67 = intermediate (needs further assessment), ≥2.67 = high risk of advanced fibrosis (F3–F4) — refer to hepatology for liver stiffness measurement (FibroScan) and specialist management. A score of 3.2 is in the high-risk range. FIB-4 = (age × AST) / (platelets × √ALT).

5. Q: A 35-year-old Vietnamese-born male has a routine HBsAg result that returns positive. He has no symptoms. LFTs are normal. What is the correct interpretation and initial management?
   A: False positive — retest in 6 months
   B: Acute hepatitis B — notify public health and refer urgently
   C: Confirm chronic infection with repeat HBsAg at 6 months, check HBV DNA, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and refer to gastroenterology or hepatology
   D: Start antiviral therapy immediately
   Correct: C
   Explanation: A single positive HBsAg requires confirmation of chronic infection (positive >6 months). Order HBV DNA (viral load), HBeAg/anti-HBe (e antigen status), and LFTs. Hepatitis B is a notifiable disease — notify your state/territory health department. Refer to gastroenterology or hepatology for ongoing management and antiviral therapy assessment. Screen household and sexual contacts. Vaccination of non-immune contacts. Chronic HBV requires regular monitoring for hepatocellular carcinoma risk.

6. Q: Which of the following patients is MOST appropriate for hepatitis C RNA testing following a positive HCV antibody result?
   A: A patient with a positive HCV antibody who was successfully treated with interferon 10 years ago
   B: A 45-year-old who injects drugs and has never been tested before
   C: A patient whose HCV antibody became positive after successful DAA therapy
   D: All of the above
   Correct: D
   Explanation: HCV RNA (viral load) is required to confirm active infection in all patients with a positive HCV antibody regardless of treatment history. Patients previously treated with interferon or DAAs may have cleared the virus (antibody remains positive lifelong) or may have been reinfected. A positive antibody alone does not confirm active infection — only HCV RNA does. All three scenarios require HCV RNA testing to determine current infection status.

7. Q: A 48-year-old female presents with acute onset severe RUQ pain, fever 38.9°C, and jaundice. Murphy's sign is positive. WCC 16.2 and CRP 148. What is the most likely diagnosis and appropriate management?
   A: Biliary colic — analgesia and ultrasound as outpatient
   B: Acute cholecystitis — hospital admission for IV antibiotics and surgical review
   C: Ascending cholangitis — emergency transfer for ERCP and IV antibiotics
   D: Hepatitis A — supportive care and notification
   Correct: C
   Explanation: Charcot's triad — RUQ pain, fever, and jaundice — is diagnostic of ascending cholangitis until proven otherwise. This is a surgical emergency with significant mortality if untreated. Emergency transfer is required for IV antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam or cephalosporin + metronidazole) and urgent ERCP for biliary decompression. Acute cholecystitis presents with RUQ pain and fever but typically without jaundice (unless Mirizzi syndrome). Do not delay transfer.

8. Q: A 55-year-old male presents with 6 weeks of rectal bleeding — fresh red blood on the toilet paper and in the bowl, no pain, associated with constipation. On examination, haemorrhoids are visible. What is the most appropriate management?
   A: Treat haemorrhoids and discharge — obvious cause identified
   B: Treat haemorrhoids AND refer for colonoscopy — age and rectal bleeding require colorectal cancer exclusion
   C: Commence iron supplementation and review in 3 months
   D: Reassure — haemorrhoids are benign, no further investigation needed
   Correct: B
   Explanation: Any rectal bleeding in a patient over 50 requires colorectal cancer exclusion regardless of the presence of an apparent benign cause. Haemorrhoids are common and may coexist with colorectal cancer. Refer for colonoscopy. Additional indications for urgent colonoscopy referral: change in bowel habit, iron deficiency anaemia, weight loss, or family history of CRC. Never attribute rectal bleeding to haemorrhoids in this age group without excluding malignancy.

9. Q: According to the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program, at what age does free FOBT screening begin in Australia and how often is it offered?
   A: From age 45, every 2 years
   B: From age 50, every year
   C: From age 45, every 2 years — reducing to annual from age 50
   D: From age 50, every 2 years
   Correct: C
   Explanation: The Australian National Bowel Cancer Screening Program offers free FOBT (immunochemical faecal occult blood test, iFOBT) to eligible Australians from age 45, every 2 years. From age 50 the frequency increases to annual. Kits are mailed directly to eligible participants. NPs have a role in encouraging participation, explaining the test, and arranging follow-up colonoscopy for positive results. A positive FOBT requires colonoscopy — do not repeat the FOBT.

10. Q: A 42-year-old male with known Crohn's disease presents with a flare — increased stool frequency (8/day), abdominal cramping, and CRP 68. He is on azathioprine maintenance. What is the most appropriate acute management?
    A: Increase azathioprine dose immediately
    B: Start oral prednisolone 40mg daily, review in 1 week, refer to gastroenterology
    C: IV metronidazole for 7 days
    D: Commence infliximab without gastroenterology review
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Moderate Crohn's flare is managed with oral corticosteroids — prednisolone 40mg daily for 4–6 weeks then taper. Azathioprine is a maintenance agent and takes weeks to months to take effect — increasing the dose does not help an acute flare. Refer to gastroenterology for optimisation of maintenance therapy (consider biologic therapy if steroid-dependent or frequent flares). Check FBE, CRP, and stool MCS (exclude infectious flare). IV steroids reserved for severe flares requiring hospital admission.

11. Q: Which lifestyle intervention has the strongest evidence for reducing liver fat and improving liver histology in MASLD?
    A: Alcohol cessation alone
    B: Mediterranean diet without weight loss
    C: Weight loss of ≥10% of body weight
    D: Vitamin E supplementation
    Correct: C
    Explanation: Weight loss of ≥10% of body weight is the most effective intervention for MASLD — it reduces liver fat, inflammation, and fibrosis. Even 5–7% weight loss improves liver fat. GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide) show emerging evidence for MASLD and have PBS listing for type 2 diabetes and obesity which commonly coexist with MASLD. The Mediterranean diet improves metabolic parameters but weight loss remains the primary target. Vitamin E has modest evidence in non-diabetic NASH but is not first-line.

12. Q: A 28-year-old female presents with bloating, alternating constipation and diarrhoea, and lower abdominal cramping for 8 months. Symptoms improve with defecation. Examination is normal. FBE, CRP, coeliac serology, and thyroid function are all normal. What is the diagnosis?
    A: Inflammatory bowel disease — refer for colonoscopy
    B: Irritable bowel syndrome — meets Rome IV criteria
    C: Coeliac disease — commence gluten-free diet
    D: Colorectal cancer — refer urgently
    Correct: B
    Explanation: This presentation meets Rome IV criteria for IBS: recurrent abdominal pain ≥1 day/week for 3 months, associated with change in stool frequency and form, improving with defecation. Normal investigations exclude organic pathology. IBS is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion — red flags and organic causes must be excluded before diagnosing. Management: explanation and reassurance, low FODMAP diet trial, antispasmodics for pain, fibre adjustment, psychological support for refractory cases.

13. Q: A 50-year-old male with cirrhosis secondary to alcohol presents for routine review. He is well-compensated. Which screening is mandatory in this patient?
    A: Annual colonoscopy for colorectal cancer
    B: 6-monthly liver ultrasound and AFP for hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance
    C: Annual FOBT only
    D: No cancer screening required — cirrhosis is managed symptomatically
    Correct: B
    Explanation: All patients with liver cirrhosis require 6-monthly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance with liver ultrasound ± AFP regardless of aetiology. HCC risk in cirrhosis is 2–4% per year. Early detection significantly improves survival — HCC caught early may be amenable to curative resection or ablation. Additionally screen for oesophageal varices at diagnosis and monitor for complications of cirrhosis: ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.

14. Q: What is the recommended first-line H. pylori eradication regimen in Australia for a patient with no previous macrolide exposure and no penicillin allergy?
    A: Metronidazole + clarithromycin + PPI for 7 days
    B: Amoxicillin + clarithromycin + PPI (standard triple therapy) for 14 days
    C: Amoxicillin + metronidazole + PPI for 7 days
    D: Clarithromycin monotherapy for 14 days
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Australian Therapeutic Guidelines recommend standard triple therapy for H. pylori eradication: amoxicillin 1g + clarithromycin 500mg + PPI (full dose) twice daily for 14 days. Fourteen days is recommended over 7 days due to higher eradication rates. Confirm eradication with urea breath test or stool antigen test 4 weeks after completing treatment (stop PPI 2 weeks before testing). If clarithromycin resistance suspected (previous macrolide exposure), use quadruple therapy instead.

15. Q: A 65-year-old male presents with 4 months of progressive dysphagia — initially to solids, now also to liquids. He has lost 7kg. He is a long-term smoker. What is the most appropriate management?
    A: Trial of PPI for 4 weeks and review
    B: Reassure — dysphagia in older adults is usually functional
    C: Urgent endoscopy referral — progressive dysphagia with weight loss is an alarm feature for oesophageal malignancy
    D: Barium swallow as first investigation, then endoscopy if abnormal
    Correct: C
    Explanation: Progressive dysphagia (solids then liquids) with weight loss and smoking history in a 65-year-old is oesophageal cancer until proven otherwise. This requires urgent endoscopy referral — do not delay with empirical PPI treatment. Oesophageal cancer has a poor prognosis when detected late. Urgent 2-week wait referral pathway. Additional alarm features warranting urgent endoscopy: haematemesis, iron deficiency anaemia, persistent vomiting, epigastric mass, age >55 with new unexplained dyspepsia.

16. Q: A patient with known ulcerative colitis asks about the risk of colorectal cancer and surveillance. When should surveillance colonoscopy begin?
    A: At age 50, same as the general population
    B: After 8–10 years of extensive colitis (pancolitis or left-sided colitis)
    C: At the time of IBD diagnosis regardless of disease extent
    D: Only if the patient develops symptoms
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Patients with extensive UC (pancolitis or left-sided colitis) have an increased risk of colorectal cancer. Surveillance colonoscopy should begin after 8–10 years of disease and then every 1–3 years depending on risk factors (disease activity, pseudopolyps, family history). Proctitis alone does not carry increased CRC risk. Gastroenterology should guide the surveillance schedule. The risk is lower in Crohn's colitis but surveillance is still recommended after similar duration of colonic involvement.

17. Q: Which of the following best describes the correct interpretation of hepatitis B serology showing: HBsAg negative, anti-HBs positive, anti-HBc negative?
    A: Past infection with resolved hepatitis B
    B: Chronic hepatitis B carrier
    C: Successful vaccination — immune due to hepatitis B vaccine
    D: Window period of acute hepatitis B
    Correct: C
    Explanation: HBsAg negative + anti-HBs positive + anti-HBc negative = immune due to vaccination. Vaccination produces anti-HBs without anti-HBc (core antibody). Past natural infection produces both anti-HBs AND anti-HBc (total). The window period (acute infection) shows all markers negative or only anti-HBc IgM positive. Anti-HBs ≥10 IU/L indicates adequate vaccine response. This is important for NPs administering vaccines — document vaccination and response in AIR.

18. Q: A 55-year-old female with type 2 diabetes and BMI 34 has routine LFTs showing ALT 68 (normal <35). Ultrasound confirms fatty liver. Alcohol intake is minimal. HBsAg, HCV antibody, ANA, and coeliac serology are negative. What is the diagnosis and what FIB-4 components do you need?
    A: Alcoholic liver disease — refer to alcohol and drug service
    B: MASLD — calculate FIB-4 using age, AST, ALT, and platelet count to risk-stratify fibrosis
    C: Autoimmune hepatitis — start prednisolone
    D: Hepatitis C — retest with HCV RNA
    Correct: B
    Explanation: This presentation is classic MASLD — fatty liver on ultrasound, elevated ALT, metabolic risk factors (T2DM, obesity), and exclusion of other liver disease causes. FIB-4 = (age × AST) / (platelets × √ALT) — risk-stratifies for advanced fibrosis without liver biopsy. Score <1.30 = low risk, 1.30–2.67 = intermediate (consider FibroScan), ≥2.67 = high risk — refer to hepatology. Management: weight loss target ≥10%, optimise metabolic risk factors (glycaemic control, lipids, BP), avoid alcohol, consider GLP-1 agonist.

19. Q: A 40-year-old presents with 3 months of perianal discomfort, fresh rectal bleeding, and a feeling of incomplete evacuation. On examination, grade II haemorrhoids are visible. Digital rectal examination shows no masses. What is appropriate management?
    A: Haemorrhoidectomy referral immediately
    B: Increased dietary fibre, adequate hydration, topical treatment, and colonoscopy referral to exclude proximal pathology given age and symptoms
    C: Topical hydrocortisone cream only — no further investigation
    D: Banding referral only — no colonoscopy needed under age 50
    Correct: B
    Explanation: Conservative management of haemorrhoids includes increased dietary fibre (25–30g/day), adequate hydration, sitz baths, and topical agents (lignocaine-containing creams for pain, glyceryl trinitrate for fissure). However, rectal bleeding with incomplete evacuation in a 40-year-old warrants colonoscopy to exclude proximal pathology — colorectal cancer can present at any age. Don't attribute rectal bleeding solely to visible haemorrhoids without investigation. Surgical referral (banding, haemorrhoidectomy) for grade III–IV or refractory grade II haemorrhoids.

20. Q: Which of the following patients should be offered hepatitis C testing according to Australian guidelines?
    A: Only people who currently inject drugs
    B: People born between 1945 and 1975 only
    C: People who have ever injected drugs, had a blood transfusion before 1990, were born in a high prevalence country, have unexplained liver disease, or are in custody
    D: Only patients with elevated LFTs
    Correct: C
    Explanation: Australian guidelines recommend HCV testing for: anyone who has ever injected drugs (even once), recipients of blood products before February 1990 (when screening was introduced), people born in high-prevalence countries (Egypt, Pakistan, Central Asia, sub-Saharan Africa), people in or released from prison, people with unexplained liver disease or elevated LFTs, and healthcare workers with needlestick injuries. HCV testing is also recommended opportunistically in primary care. Curative DAA therapy is available and NPs can prescribe under PBS authority — testing and treatment saves lives.
