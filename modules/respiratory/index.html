🫁 Respiratory

Dyspnoea differentials, asthma, COPD, pneumonia, PE, and spirometry interpretation

Clinical Overview

Respiratory presentations range from acute life-threatening emergencies to chronic disease management. As a Nurse Practitioner, your core skills are rapid assessment of respiratory distress, differentiating obstructive from restrictive and infective pathology, and knowing when to escalate versus manage in primary care.

⚠️ Red Flags — Call 000 / Emergency Transfer Immediately

  • SpO2 <92% on air (or <88% in known COPD) — or rapidly falling
  • RR >30 breaths/min or accessory muscle use at rest
  • Silent chest on auscultation in asthma — near-fatal attack
  • Cyanosis — central or peripheral
  • Altered consciousness or exhaustion in respiratory distress
  • Tension pneumothorax — absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation, haemodynamic instability
  • Massive haemoptysis (>100mL / 24h)
  • Stridor — upper airway obstruction
  • Suspected PE with haemodynamic compromise

Dyspnoea — The Critical Differentials

🚨

Acute — Must Not Miss

  • Acute severe asthma
  • Acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD)
  • Pulmonary embolism
  • Pneumothorax (spontaneous or tension)
  • Acute pulmonary oedema (cardiac cause)
  • Pneumonia with sepsis
  • Anaphylaxis with bronchospasm
  • Foreign body airway obstruction
🩺

Chronic or Subacute

  • Asthma — poorly controlled or new diagnosis
  • COPD — stable or mild exacerbation
  • Pleural effusion
  • Lung malignancy
  • Interstitial lung disease
  • Cardiac failure (dyspnoea at rest or exertion)
  • Anaemia
  • Deconditioning and obesity

Asthma

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease characterised by variable airflow obstruction. Australian prevalence is approximately 11% — one of the highest in the world. NPs play a key role in chronic asthma management, education, and written action plan development.

📊

Severity Classification

  • Mild intermittent — symptoms <2 days/week, no nocturnal symptoms
  • Mild persistent — symptoms >2 days/week, some nocturnal
  • Moderate persistent — daily symptoms, nocturnal >1/week
  • Severe persistent — continuous symptoms, frequent nocturnal
  • Well controlled: no daytime sx, no reliever use, no waking, no limitation
  • Partially controlled: 1–2 of the above present
  • Uncontrolled: 3–4 of the above present
💊

Stepwise Pharmacotherapy (GINA 2024)

  • Step 1: Low-dose ICS-formoterol PRN (preferred) or SABA PRN
  • Step 2: Low-dose ICS daily + SABA PRN
  • Step 3: Low-dose ICS-LABA daily + SABA PRN
  • Step 4: Medium/high-dose ICS-LABA daily
  • Step 5: Add tiotropium, anti-IgE, anti-IL5, or oral corticosteroid
  • SMART therapy: budesonide-formoterol as both maintenance and reliever
  • Review and step down when well controlled for 3 months

Acute Severe Asthma

🚨

Assessment of Severity

  • Mild: speaks in sentences, SpO2 ≥94%, RR <25, HR <110
  • Moderate: speaks in phrases, SpO2 ≥90%, wheeze audible
  • Severe: speaks in words, SpO2 <90%, RR >30, HR >120, accessory muscles
  • Near-fatal: silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, altered consciousness
  • PEFR <50% predicted = severe; <33% = near-fatal
💊

Acute Management

  • Salbutamol 2.5–5mg nebulised or 4–8 puffs via spacer, repeat every 20 min
  • Ipratropium bromide 0.5mg nebulised — add for moderate to severe
  • Prednisolone 40–50mg oral (or IV hydrocortisone 100mg if unable to swallow)
  • Supplemental oxygen to target SpO2 93–95%
  • Call 000 for severe or near-fatal — do not delay
  • Magnesium sulphate IV — for severe cases in hospital setting

COPD

COPD is a progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by chronic airway inflammation, typically from smoking. It affects approximately 1.5 million Australians, with many undiagnosed.

📉

GOLD Staging (Post-Bronchodilator FEV₁)

  • GOLD 1 (Mild): FEV₁ ≥80% predicted
  • GOLD 2 (Moderate): FEV₁ 50–79% predicted
  • GOLD 3 (Severe): FEV₁ 30–49% predicted
  • GOLD 4 (Very severe): FEV₁ <30% predicted
  • All require FEV₁/FVC <0.70 post-bronchodilator for diagnosis
  • CAT score and mMRC dyspnoea scale guide symptom severity
💊

Stable COPD Management

  • Smoking cessation — most effective intervention at any stage
  • LAMA (tiotropium, umeclidinium) — first-line bronchodilator
  • LABA (salmeterol, formoterol) — add if symptomatic on LAMA
  • LAMA + LABA combination — for persistent symptoms
  • ICS — only if frequent exacerbations AND eosinophils ≥300
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation — improves exercise tolerance and QoL
  • Influenza, pneumococcal, COVID, and pertussis vaccinations
  • Oxygen therapy if resting SpO2 ≤88% on air

Pneumonia

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a common and potentially serious infection. NPs must assess severity, guide antibiotic choice, and determine appropriate site of care.

🌡️

CURB-65 Severity Score

  • C — Confusion (new)
  • U — Urea >7 mmol/L
  • R — RR ≥30 breaths/min
  • B — BP systolic <90 or diastolic ≤60 mmHg
  • 65 — Age ≥65 years
  • Score 0–1: low risk — outpatient treatment
  • Score 2: moderate risk — consider short admission
  • Score 3–5: high risk — hospital admission, ICU if ≥4
💊

CAP Antibiotic Treatment (Therapeutic Guidelines)

  • Mild CAP (outpatient): Amoxicillin 1g TDS for 5 days
  • If atypical suspected (younger, dry cough, insidious): Doxycycline 100mg BD for 5–7 days
  • Penicillin allergy (non-anaphylactic): Doxycycline or Cefalexin
  • Penicillin allergy (anaphylactic): Doxycycline or Moxifloxacin
  • Moderate CAP (inpatient): Amoxicillin + Doxycycline, or Ceftriaxone + Azithromycin
  • Always review allergy history and local resistance patterns

Pulmonary Embolism

🩸

Wells Score for PE

  • Clinical signs of DVT (+3)
  • PE more likely than alternative diagnosis (+3)
  • HR >100 (+1.5)
  • Immobilisation or surgery in past 4 weeks (+1.5)
  • Previous DVT or PE (+1.5)
  • Haemoptysis (+1)
  • Malignancy (+1)
  • Score ≤4: low probability — D-dimer first
  • Score >4: high probability — proceed to CTPA
📊

Spirometry Interpretation

  • FVC — total air exhaled in a forced breath
  • FEV₁ — air exhaled in first second
  • FEV₁/FVC ratio — key discriminating value
  • Obstructive: FEV₁/FVC <0.70, FEV₁ reduced, FVC normal or reduced
  • Restrictive: FEV₁/FVC ≥0.70, FVC reduced, FEV₁ reduced proportionally
  • Mixed: FEV₁/FVC <0.70 AND FVC <80% predicted
  • Reversibility: ≥12% and 200mL improvement in FEV₁ post-bronchodilator = significant
  • Always perform post-bronchodilator for COPD diagnosis
